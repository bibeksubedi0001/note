8.1 Effective Length and Column Classification

A column transfers axial load and bending moments to the members below. Even a nominally concentric load has some eccentricity because of construction tolerances, imperfect alignment and lateral displacement. Design therefore requires a consistent set of axial force, moments about both axes, restraint conditions and effective lengths.

Unsupported length is the actual length between lateral restraints. Effective length le = kl is the equivalent buckling length and depends on rotational restraint and whether the frame can sway. It is not automatically the storey height, and it can differ in the two principal directions.

Idealized restraintCommon IS 456 effective-length factorCondition
Both ends held in position and restrained against rotation0.65Use the matching non-sway idealization.
Both ends held; rotation restrained at one end0.80The other end can rotate.
Both ends held; neither end rotationally restrained1.00Idealized pin-ended case.
One end fixed, the other free2.00Cantilever idealization.

These design values must not be confused with ideal Euler-theory factors such as 0.5 for a perfectly fixed-fixed elastic strut. A real frame may require Annex E or a second-order analysis instead of a simple tabulated idealization. An unbraced frame must not be assigned a braced factor merely because its beam-column connections are monolithic.

For a rectangular section with depth D for bending about x and breadth b for bending about y, check lex/D and ley/b. Both must satisfy the short-column criterion (less than 12 in IS 456); otherwise account for slenderness in the affected direction. A column can be short about its major axis and slender about its minor axis.

Example: the 2080 Baishakh column is 400 × 500 mm, 4.0 m unsupported, held at both ends and rotationally restrained at one end. With the matching braced factor 0.8, le = 3.2 m. The ratios are 3200/500 = 6.4 and 3200/400 = 8.0, so it is short about both axes.

Minimum Eccentricity

For each relevant direction, emin = l/500 + D/30, subject to a minimum of 20 mm; use the unsupported length and the dimension in that bending direction. The corresponding moment is Puemin. Check the code's treatment of minimum eccentricity in biaxial design; it is not permission to ignore a larger moment already obtained from analysis.

For the 4.0 m, 400 × 500 mm example, the two values are 21.33 mm and 24.67 mm. Pair each with its corresponding axis. If a question gives only effective lengths, do not silently substitute them for the missing unsupported length in this expression.

8.2 Interaction Diagrams for Columns 2080

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. Define the interaction diagram with its features and neat sketches. Discuss the modes of failure for compression members in eccentric compression within the interaction diagram. [6] (2080 Bhadra, Q3a)

A column carries an axial load P together with a moment M = P·e. An interaction diagram is the section-resistance envelope of compatible axial-load and moment pairs. A design point inside the appropriate design envelope satisfies that section-capacity check; it does not by itself establish adequate slenderness, anchorage, confinement or overall frame stability.

Key features:

  • Top (Puz, pure axial): the maximum squash load, M = 0.
  • Balanced point: the limiting concrete compression strain and the relevant tension-steel yield condition occur together. It separates characteristic tension- and compression-controlled behaviour; it is not a universal assertion that the moment is exactly the maximum possible value for every section model.
  • Bottom (pure moment): P = 0, the section acts as a beam.
  • Above the balanced point → compression-controlled (brittle) failure — adding axial load reduces the moment capacity; below → tension-controlled (ductile) failure — adding axial load increases the moment capacity.

How an Interaction Curve Is Constructed

  1. Choose a neutral-axis position and a compatible limiting strain distribution.
  2. Calculate the concrete compression resultant and its position from the design stress block.
  3. Calculate strain and design stress in each reinforcement layer; some bars may be in compression and others in tension.
  4. Subtract the concrete displaced by compression bars if the concrete area was included in the gross block.
  5. Add axial forces to obtain P and take moments about the section centroid to obtain M.
  6. Repeat for other neutral-axis positions to trace the resistance envelope.

Where the entire section is compressed, the allowable strain distribution is different from the pure-flexure case; uniform axial compression uses the 0.002 limiting strain. Extending a 0.0035 flexural triangle beyond the section without the relevant compression-strain condition gives an incorrect high-load interaction curve.

Design method (SP-16 biaxial)

First distinguish two axial expressions. For an eligible short tied column with sufficiently small eccentricity, the axial design expression is Pu = 0.4fckAc + 0.67fyAsc. Its use is subject to the minimum-eccentricity restrictions. The separate quantity Puz = 0.45fckAc + 0.75fyAsc appears in the biaxial interaction procedure. Substituting Puz as an unrestricted axial design capacity overestimates the permitted short-column resistance.

  1. Squash load: Puz = 0.45fckAc + 0.75fyAsc (assume a steel ratio p = 0.8–4%, Asc = p·bD100).
  2. Uniaxial capacities Mux1, Muy1 for the given PufckbD and d′D from the SP-16 interaction charts.
  3. Exponent αn = 1.0 at PuPuz = 0.2 → 2.0 at 0.8 (linear between).
  4. Biaxial check: (MuxMux1)αn + (MuyMuy1)αn ≤ 1.0 — if not, increase steel or size.
  5. Provide lateral ties: diameter at least one-quarter of the largest longitudinal bar and at least 6 mm; ordinary spacing not greater than the least of 16 times the smallest longitudinal-bar diameter, the least lateral dimension and 300 mm. Seismic confinement is a separate, often stricter check.

Reading SP 16 Correctly

Choose a chart for the correct concrete/steel grades, reinforcement arrangement and d′/D ratio. A chart for steel on two faces is not a chart for bars distributed on four faces. Determine the normalized axial load Pu/(fckbD), the steel parameter required by that chart, and the normalized moment M/(fckbD²). Interpolate between the actual plotted curves and record the values read.

For bending about the other axis, interchange the section dimensions and recompute the effective-cover ratio and the moment normalization. Both uniaxial capacities in the biaxial expression must correspond to the same applied axial load, not the two independent maximum moments on their curves.

The interaction exponent is αn = 1 for Pu/Puz ≤ 0.2 and 2 for ratios ≥ 0.8. Between them, αn = 1 + [(Pu/Puz) − 0.2]/0.6. Recalculate it after changing the steel area; do not retain the trial percentage after selecting actual bars.

  5. Write down the design steps for the design of a RC long column of an unbraced frame. [10] (2065 Kartik, Q5b)

A slender column develops additional bending because axial load acts through its lateral displacement. Global frame sway produces a P–Δ effect, while curvature within a member produces a P–δ effect. The short-column classification is not restricted to braced frames; bracing affects effective length and analysis. A slender member may reach material failure after second-order amplification, so "all slender columns fail by Euler buckling" is too crude.

Where the IS 456 simplified procedure is applicable and second-order effects have not already been included, the unreduced additional moments are:

Max = Pu·D2000(lexD)²   (× a reduction factor k = Puz − PuPuz − Pbal ≤ 1)

May = (Pub/2000)(ley/b)²

Take Pbal from the appropriate balanced condition, and apply a reduction only where its code conditions are satisfied. Taking k = 1 is an unreduced conservative study choice. The reduction factor k is not the effective-length factor used earlier. Do not add the simplified moments again to an analysis that already includes the same second-order effects.

Single and Double Curvature

Record end moments with a consistent sign convention. In single curvature the bending diagram has the same curvature sense along the member; double curvature includes a point of contraflexure. For the applicable braced-column procedure, an equivalent initial moment uses 0.6M2 + 0.4M1, not less than 0.4M2, with M2 the larger end moment and the code's relative sign for M1. Check the end sections as well as the amplified interior section. Never use this interpolation as a universal sway-frame rule.

May = (1600 × 0.45/2000)(6.5/0.45)² = 75.11 kN·m

8.5 Reinforcement Detailing / Capacity Check 2065

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. A reinforced-concrete column of cross-section 600 mm × 400 mm is reinforced with 8 longitudinal bars of 28 mm diameter. It is subjected to an axial load Pu = 2000 kN and bending moments Mux = 350 kN·m and Muy = 50 kN·m. Check whether the column is safe for this combination of actions. Use M20 concrete and Fe415 steel, and take the effective cover as 60 mm. [10] (2065 Kartik, Q1b)

To check an existing column: compute Puz from the provided steel, read Mux1 and Muy1 from SP-16 for the actual PufckbD, find αn from PuPuz, then verify (MuxMux1)αn + (MuyMuy1)αn ≤ 1.0. Detailing: longitudinal steel 0.8–6% of the gross area (≥ 4 bars, ≥ 12 mm), lateral ties as in 8.3, and for earthquake resistance special confining hoops over a length l0 at each end (see Ch 11).

Construction and Detailing Requirements

  • Use at least four longitudinal bars in a rectangular column and six in a circular column; longitudinal bars are at least 12 mm diameter.
  • The ordinary gross-area limits are 0.8% minimum and 6% maximum steel. Congestion and lap zones often justify a substantially lower practical maximum; the numerical maximum is not a target.
  • Provide the required nominal cover to the outside of the ties, ordinarily at least 40 mm for these column sizes and not less than the relevant bar-diameter requirement. Derive effective cover to the main-bar centre separately.
  • Restrain longitudinal bars against buckling with closed ties and cross-ties as required. A large outer rectangular tie alone may not restrain all intermediate bars.
  • Show splice locations, lap/coupler details, starter anchorage, normal tie zones and special confinement zones on the elevation and section.
  • When a question asks for ductile transverse detailing, perform the confinement and capacity-design checks in Chapter 11; passing the ordinary tie-spacing rule is insufficient.

Completion criterion: an assumed percentage of steel is only a trial. A completed answer records the actual bar area, cover ratios, the two uniaxial capacities at the specified axial load, the interaction exponent, the utilization, slenderness treatment and the transverse detail. If no feasible bar arrangement satisfies the fixed size, state that limitation instead of inventing a chart capacity.
