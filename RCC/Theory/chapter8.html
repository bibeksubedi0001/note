8.1 Effective Length and Column Classification

A column transfers axial load and bending moments to the members below. Even a nominally concentric load has some eccentricity because of construction tolerances, imperfect alignment and lateral displacement. Design therefore requires a consistent set of axial force, moments about both axes, restraint conditions and effective lengths.

Unsupported length is the actual length between lateral restraints. Effective length le = kl is the equivalent buckling length and depends on rotational restraint and whether the frame can sway. It is not automatically the storey height, and it can differ in the two principal directions.

These design values must not be confused with ideal Euler-theory factors such as 0.5 for a perfectly fixed-fixed elastic strut. A real frame may require Annex E or a second-order analysis instead of a simple tabulated idealization. An unbraced frame must not be assigned a braced factor merely because its beam-column connections are monolithic.

For a rectangular section with depth D for bending about x and breadth b for bending about y, check lex/D and ley/b. Both must satisfy the short-column criterion (less than 12 in IS 456); otherwise account for slenderness in the affected direction. A column can be short about its major axis and slender about its minor axis.

Example: the 2080 Baishakh column is 400 × 500 mm, 4.0 m unsupported, held at both ends and rotationally restrained at one end. With the matching braced factor 0.8, le = 3.2 m. The ratios are 3200/500 = 6.4 and 3200/400 = 8.0, so it is short about both axes.

Minimum Eccentricity

For each relevant direction, emin = l/500 + D/30, subject to a minimum of 20 mm; use the unsupported length and the dimension in that bending direction. The corresponding moment is Puemin. Check the code's treatment of minimum eccentricity in biaxial design; it is not permission to ignore a larger moment already obtained from analysis.

For the 4.0 m, 400 × 500 mm example, the two values are 21.33 mm and 24.67 mm. Pair each with its corresponding axis. If a question gives only effective lengths, do not silently substitute them for the missing unsupported length in this expression.

Q1. Define the interaction diagram with its features and neat sketches. Discuss the modes of failure for compression members in eccentric compression within the interaction diagram. [6] (2080 Bhadra, Q3a)

A column carries an axial load P together with a moment M = P·e. An interaction diagram is the section-resistance envelope of compatible axial-load and moment pairs. A design point inside the appropriate design envelope satisfies that section-capacity check; it does not by itself establish adequate slenderness, anchorage, confinement or overall frame stability.

Key features:

  • Top (Puz, pure axial): the maximum squash load, M = 0.
  • Balanced point: the limiting concrete compression strain and the relevant tension-steel yield condition occur together. It separates characteristic tension- and compression-controlled behaviour; it is not a universal assertion that the moment is exactly the maximum possible value for every section model.
  • Bottom (pure moment): P = 0, the section acts as a beam.
  • Above the balanced point → compression-controlled (brittle) failure — adding axial load reduces the moment capacity; below → tension-controlled (ductile) failure — adding axial load increases the moment capacity.
  1. Choose a neutral-axis position and a compatible limiting strain distribution.
  2. Calculate the concrete compression resultant and its position from the design stress block.
  3. Calculate strain and design stress in each reinforcement layer; some bars may be in compression and others in tension.
  4. Subtract the concrete displaced by compression bars if the concrete area was included in the gross block.
  5. Add axial forces to obtain P and take moments about the section centroid to obtain M.
  6. Repeat for other neutral-axis positions to trace the resistance envelope.

Where the entire section is compressed, the allowable strain distribution is different from the pure-flexure case; uniform axial compression uses the 0.002 limiting strain. Extending a 0.0035 flexural triangle beyond the section without the relevant compression-strain condition gives an incorrect high-load interaction curve.

Construction and Detailing Requirements

  • Use at least four longitudinal bars in a rectangular column and six in a circular column; longitudinal bars are at least 12 mm diameter.
  • The ordinary gross-area limits are 0.8% minimum and 6% maximum steel. Congestion and lap zones often justify a substantially lower practical maximum; the numerical maximum is not a target.
  • Provide the required nominal cover to the outside of the ties, ordinarily at least 40 mm for these column sizes and not less than the relevant bar-diameter requirement. Derive effective cover to the main-bar centre separately.
  • Restrain longitudinal bars against buckling with closed ties and cross-ties as required. A large outer rectangular tie alone may not restrain all intermediate bars.
  • Show splice locations, lap/coupler details, starter anchorage, normal tie zones and special confinement zones on the elevation and section.
  • When a question asks for ductile transverse detailing, perform the confinement and capacity-design checks in Chapter 11; passing the ordinary tie-spacing rule is insufficient.
