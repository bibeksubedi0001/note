CHAPTER 1 — RC STRUCTURES & DESIGN METHODS

Every past-exam question of this chapter is stated in full — with its exam sitting (year & session), question number and marks — and answered below.

1.  Describe the requirement of steel as reinforcement in RCC structures. 2076 Chaitra · Q1a

Concrete is strong in compression (20–30 N/mm²) but weak in tension (only about one-tenth of its compressive strength) and fails in a brittle manner. Steel reinforcement is therefore provided to—

(i) carry the tensile stresses in the tension zone of flexural members (beams, slabs) that concrete cannot resist;
(ii) resist diagonal tension caused by shear and torsion (stirrups, bent-up bars);
(iii) share compression and reduce member size (columns, doubly-reinforced beams);
(iv) control shrinkage and temperature cracking (distribution / nominal steel);
(v) impart ductility, so that failure is gradual (steel yields, giving warning) instead of sudden.

Steel acts compositely with concrete because of their good mutual bond, nearly equal coefficient of thermal expansion (≈12×10−6/°C), and the alkaline concrete cover that protects the steel from corrosion and fire.

2.  Write the characteristics of steel reinforcing bars used in reinforced concrete structures. 2065 Kartik · Q1a [5 marks]

A good reinforcing bar should possess—

(i) High tensile/yield strength (fy = 250–550 N/mm²);
(ii) a well-defined yield point (mild steel Fe250) or, for cold-worked bars (Fe415/Fe500), a 0.2% proof stress taken as fy;
(iii) adequate ductility / percentage elongation, so the member fails in a ductile manner and hooks/bends can be formed safely;
(iv) good bond with concrete — deformed (ribbed/twisted) bars bond far better than plain bars;
(v) weldability and bendability without cracking;
(vi) uniform quality and a constant modulus of elasticity Es = 2×105 N/mm²;
(vii) resistance to corrosion and fatigue.

Common grades: Mild steel (Fe250), TOR/HYSD (Fe415) and TMT (Fe500/Fe550).

3.  Explain the different types of design methods used in RC design. 2069 Chaitra · Q1a [6 marks]

There are three design philosophies:

1. Working Stress Method (WSM) — based on elastic (linear) theory. The section is proportioned so that stresses under service (working) loads do not exceed permissible values (= material strength ÷ a factor of safety). Concrete and steel are combined through the modular ratio m. Simple, but conservative and uneconomical, and it gives no true measure of the collapse margin.

2. Ultimate Load Method (ULM) — the section is designed for the collapse load = service load × load factor, using the actual (plastic) stress block at failure. It uses the reserve strength (economical) but ignores serviceability (deflection, cracking) at working load.

3. Limit State Method (LSM) — ensures the structure does not reach any limit state: the limit state of collapse (strength — flexure, shear, torsion, buckling) and the limit states of serviceability (deflection, cracking, vibration). It applies partial safety factors γf on loads and γm on materials to characteristic values, giving a rational, probability-based and economical design (adopted by IS 456:2000).

4.  Explain how the limit state method of design differs from the working stress method. 2081 Bhadra · Q1a [4 marks]

In short, LSM uses the true stress block (0.446fck, 0.87fy) with partial safety factors and checks both strength and serviceability, whereas WSM merely keeps elastic stresses within permissible limits.

5.  Describe the difference between working stress and limit state design; explain characteristic strength and load. 2079 Bhadra · Q1a [2+2 marks]

Difference: WSM — elastic theory, single factor of safety, service-load stresses kept within permissible limits, uneconomical. LSM — partial safety factors on loads and materials, ultimate-load strength plus serviceability checks, economical (see the table in Q4).

Characteristic strength (fck for concrete, fy for steel) = the strength below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall, i.e. the 5% fractile, fck = fm − 1.65σ.

Characteristic load = the load that has a 95% probability of not being exceeded during the design life of the structure (the IS 875 values are taken as characteristic). Design values: fd = f/γm, Fd = F·γf.

6.  Differentiate between working stress and limit state methods. 2074 Chaitra · Q3a [5 marks]

The five key differences are: (1) elastic theory vs limit-state basis; (2) single factor of safety vs separate partial safety factors γf, γm; (3) deterministic vs probabilistic (characteristic) values; (4) no true collapse margin vs realistic margin with serviceability checks; (5) uneconomical vs economical sections. 7.  Why is the limit state method better than the working stress method? Explain in brief. 2072 Chaitra · Q2b [5 marks]

LSM is superior because—

(i) it provides adequate safety against collapse and adequate serviceability at working load through separate limit states, whereas WSM only limits elastic stress;
(ii) it uses separate partial safety factors for each load and each material, reflecting their different uncertainties, instead of one arbitrary factor of safety;
(iii) it is probability-based (characteristic values), giving a rational and realistic margin of safety;
(iv) it uses the true plastic stress block at collapse, so sections are more economical;
(v) it accounts for load combinations (DL, LL, WL, EQ) with appropriate factors.

8.  Discuss WSM vs LSM with their advantages/disadvantages; compare balanced, under- and over-reinforced sections in both. 2071 Chaitra · Q4a [8 marks]

WSM vs LSM: WSM advantages: simple, familiar, low service stresses give good serviceability (small deflection/cracks); disadvantages: uneconomical, no true safety margin, ignores material non-linearity. LSM advantages: economical, rational safety, checks both strength and serviceability; disadvantages: more complex, needs statistical data. (Comparison table as in Q4.)

Balanced / under- / over-reinforced sections:

• Balanced — steel reaches its design stress exactly as concrete reaches its limiting strain. WSM: actual NA x = critical NA Xc; LSM: xu = xu,max (0.48d for Fe415).
• Under-reinforced — less steel than balanced; steel yields first ⇒ ductile, ample warning. WSM: x < Xc (steel governs); LSM: xu < xu,max. Preferred.
• Over-reinforced — more steel; concrete crushes first ⇒ sudden brittle failure. WSM: x > Xc (concrete governs); LSM: xu > xu,max (not permitted — M limited to Mu,lim). Avoided.

9.  Compare the factor of safety (WSM) and the partial safety factor (LSM) for concrete and steel. 2069 Chaitra · Q5a [4 marks]

WSM applies a single factor of safety to the material strength to obtain the permissible stress: for concrete σcbc ≈ fck/3 (factor ≈ 3), for steel σst ≈ 0.55fy (factor ≈ 1.8). One deterministic factor covers all uncertainties.

LSM uses partial safety factors: on materials γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel (design strengths 0.446fck and 0.87fy); and on loads γf (1.5 for DL+LL; 1.2 or 1.5 for combinations with WL/EQ). Separate factors reflect the different variabilities of concrete, steel and the loads.

10.  Define characteristic loads and strength; discuss the stress-strain relation for steel and concrete in LSM and WSM. 2078 Bhadra · Q1a [1+1+2+2 marks]

Characteristic load = load with 95% probability of not being exceeded in the structure's life. Characteristic strength = strength below which not more than 5% of results fall (fck = fm − 1.65σ).

Stress-strain in LSM: Concrete — parabolic from 0 to strain 0.002 reaching a design stress 0.446fck (= 0.67fck/1.5), then constant up to the ultimate strain 0.0035. Steel — linear (Es = 2×105) up to the design yield 0.87fy (= fy/1.15); mild steel has a flat yield plateau, HYSD is idealised with a 0.2% proof stress.

Stress-strain in WSM: both materials are assumed linearly elastic; concrete stress ≤ σcbc, steel stress ≤ σst, related through the modular ratio m = 280/(3σcbc).

Idealized stress-strain diagrams — concrete (parabolic to 0.002, then constant 0.446fck to 0.0035) and mild steel (elastic to fy, then yield plateau; design value 0.87fy).

11.  How do you consider earthquake loads while designing RCC structures? Explain briefly. 2072 Kartik · Q2a [4 marks]

Earthquake load is an inertial lateral load. Following IS 1893, the design horizontal seismic coefficient is Ah = (Z/2)(I/R)(Sa/g), where Z = zone factor, I = importance factor, R = response-reduction factor (larger for ductile detailing), and Sa/g = spectral acceleration for the structure's fundamental period T.

The design base shear VB = Ah·W (W = seismic weight = DL + a fraction of LL) is distributed up the height (∝ Wihi²) as storey forces and the frame is analysed for them. EQ load is combined with gravity by the factored combinations 1.5(DL±EL), 1.2(DL+LL±EL) and 0.9DL±1.5EL, and members are given ductile detailing per IS 13920.

1.  What are the different types of RCC section of a rectangular beam? Explain with neat sketches. 2082 Bhadra · Q1a [4 marks]

By the amount of tension steel a rectangular RC section is classified as:

• Balanced section — steel and concrete reach their permissible (or limiting) stresses simultaneously; actual NA equals the critical NA (x = Xc).
• Under-reinforced — less steel than balanced; the steel reaches its permissible stress first (x < Xc) ⇒ ductile.
• Over-reinforced — more steel than balanced; the concrete reaches its permissible stress first (x > Xc) ⇒ brittle.

By reinforcement arrangement: singly-reinforced (tension steel only) and doubly-reinforced (tension + compression steel — used when the moment exceeds the balanced capacity or the depth is restricted). (Sketch: the elastic stress diagram — triangular concrete stress block, straight-line strain, with the NA position shifting for each type.)

Section types by the depth of the neutral axis xu relative to the limiting depth xu,lim (= xu,max).

2.  Discuss different types of flexural sections by amount of tension reinforcement. Describe the failure modes. 2080 Bhadra · Q1a [8 marks]

• Balanced — the neutral axis lies at the critical depth; concrete crushing and steel yielding occur together; failure is simultaneous.
• Under-reinforced (pt < balanced) — the steel yields first; large deflections and wide cracks give ample warning before the concrete finally crushes ⇒ ductile (tension) failure — preferred.
• Over-reinforced (pt > balanced) — the concrete crushes first, before the steel yields; the failure is sudden, explosive and brittle with little warning ⇒ compression failure — avoided.

Thus the failure mode is governed by the steel ratio: tension (ductile) for under-reinforced, compression (brittle) for over-reinforced, and simultaneous for balanced sections.

1. Ast = Σn(π/4)φ²;   m = 280/(3σcbc).
2. Critical NA: Xc = kcd, where kc = mσcbc/(mσcbc + σst).
3. Actual NA (transformed area about NA): (b/2)x² = mAst(d − x) ⇒ solve the quadratic (b/2)x² + mAstx − mAstd = 0.
4. Compare: x ≤ Xc → under-reinforced (steel governs); x > Xc → over-reinforced (concrete governs).
5. MOR: under-reinforced → M = σstAst(d − x/3); over-reinforced → M = ½σcbcb·x(d − x/3).
Permissible stresses used: σcbc = 5 (M15), 7 (M20), 8.5 (M25) N/mm²; σst = 140 (Fe250), 230 (Fe415), 275 (Fe500) N/mm².

CHAPTER 3 — LIMIT STATE METHOD

Every past-exam question of this chapter is stated in full — with its exam sitting (year & session), question number and marks — and answered below.

1.  State all safety and serviceability requirements of the limit state method; define the limit state of strength and the limit state of serviceability. 2072 Kartik · Q1a [6 marks]

A structure must satisfy two groups of limit states:

• Limit states of collapse (strength/safety): flexure, shear, torsion, compression/buckling, and overall stability (overturning, sliding) — the structure must not collapse under the worst factored loads.
• Limit states of serviceability: deflection (≤ span/250), cracking (crack width ≤ 0.3 mm for mild exposure), vibration, durability/corrosion and fire resistance — the structure must stay fit for use under service loads.

Limit state of strength = the state at which the structure/member becomes unsafe due to collapse (loss of equilibrium, rupture, buckling). Limit state of serviceability = the state at which it becomes unfit for normal use due to excessive deflection, cracking or vibration, though it has not collapsed.

2.  Explain the different categories of limit state design. 2075 Ashwin · Q4a [6 marks]

There are two categories:

1. Limit state of collapse (strength) — checks against flexure, shear, torsion, compression, tension and stability, using factored (ultimate) loads (γf) and design material strengths 0.446fck and 0.87fy.
2. Limit state of serviceability — checks deflection, cracking and vibration (with durability/fire), using service (unfactored) loads (γf = 1.0) and the full material stiffness.

3.  What are the serviceability requirements in the limit state method? Explain briefly. 2075 Chaitra · Q1b [4 marks]

They ensure the structure stays usable under working loads:

• Deflection: total ≤ span/250; deflection after construction ≤ span/350 or 20 mm (to protect finishes) — controlled by span/effective-depth ratios.
• Cracking: surface crack width ≤ 0.3 mm (mild exposure), controlled by bar spacing/diameter and minimum steel.
• Vibration: kept within comfortable limits.
• Durability & fire: adequate cover and concrete grade.

4.  Explain the limit state of serviceability and its requirements. List the types of splicing. 2074 Ashwin · Q3a [1+4 marks]

The limit state of serviceability is the condition beyond which the structure, though not collapsed, becomes unfit for normal use due to excessive deflection, cracking or vibration. Requirements: deflection ≤ span/250; crack width ≤ 0.3 mm; acceptable vibration; adequate durability.

Types of splicing: (i) lap splice (overlap = Ld; not used for φ > 36 mm); (ii) welded splice; (iii) mechanical splice (parallel-thread, taper-thread, or cold-swaged coupler).

5.  Define the design strength and the design load for the limit state method. 2081 Baishakh · Q1c [4 marks]

Design strength fd = characteristic strength ÷ partial safety factor for material = f/γm. For concrete fcd = fck/1.5 (used as 0.446fck in the stress block); for steel fyd = fy/1.15 = 0.87fy.

Design load Fd = characteristic load × partial safety factor for load = F·γf (e.g. 1.5(DL+LL)). These design values are used in the limit state of collapse.

• Steel: Mild steel (Fe250) — linear (slope Es = 2×105) up to the design yield 0.87fy, then a flat yield plateau. HYSD (Fe415/500) — linear up to ~0.8fyd, then a curved inelastic branch to 0.87fy; the 0.2% proof stress defines fy.

• Characteristic strength = the strength below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall (fck = fm − 1.65σ).

8.  What are balanced, under- and over-reinforced sections? Sketch the stress distributions and give the moment-of-resistance expressions. 2080 Baishakh · Q1a [6 marks]

The type is decided by the depth of the neutral axis xu relative to the limiting depth xu,max (= 0.53d, 0.48d, 0.46d for Fe250/415/500). The LSM stress block is a constant 0.446fck with resultant at 0.42xu from the top.

• Balanced (xu = xu,max): Mu,lim = 0.36fckb·xu,max(d − 0.42xu,max) = 0.87fyAst(d − 0.42xu,max).
• Under-reinforced (xu < xu,max): Mu = 0.87fyAstd[1 − Astfy/(bd·fck)]; steel yields ⇒ ductile.
• Over-reinforced (xu > xu,max): not permitted — Mu is limited to Mu,lim; concrete crushes ⇒ brittle.

Stress distribution at collapse (LSM): section, linear strain (0.0035 at top), and the rectangular-parabolic stress block giving C = 0.36fckbxu at 0.42xu; lever arm z = d − 0.42xu.

9.  Describe under-, over- and balanced reinforced sections. 2081 Baishakh · Q1a [7 marks]

A balanced section has just enough steel so that the steel reaches its design stress 0.87fy exactly as the extreme concrete fibre reaches 0.0035 (xu = xu,max). An under-reinforced section has less steel (xu < xu,max): the steel yields first, so failure is preceded by large deflection and cracking (ductile) — this is preferred and is the basis of IS 456 design. An over-reinforced section has more steel (xu > xu,max): the concrete crushes before the steel yields, giving a sudden brittle failure — IS 456 does not allow it (moment is capped at Mu,lim).

10.  Describe the failure modes in under-reinforced, balanced and over-reinforced sections. 2081 Bhadra · Q1b [4 marks]

Under-reinforced → tension (ductile) failure: the steel yields and stretches, producing wide cracks and large deflection that warn of failure before the concrete finally crushes. Balanced → steel yielding and concrete crushing occur simultaneously. Over-reinforced → compression (brittle) failure: the concrete crushes suddenly, with little warning, before the steel yields.

11.  Explain under-, balanced and over-reinforced sections in the limit state method. 2074 Ashwin · Q1a [6 marks]

In LSM the limiting neutral-axis depth is xu,max = 0.48d for Fe415 (0.53d for Fe250, 0.46d for Fe500). A section is balanced when xu = xu,max, under-reinforced when xu < xu,max (steel governs, ductile, preferred) and over-reinforced when xu > xu,max (concrete governs, brittle, not permitted — M capped at Mu,lim). IS 456 therefore restricts designs to xu ≤ xu,max.

12.  Explain, with sketches, under-, over- and balanced sections. 2075 Chaitra · Q1a [4 marks]

On the strain diagram: for an under-reinforced section the steel strain exceeds the yield strain (0.87fy/Es + 0.002) while the concrete strain is still below 0.0035 (NA high, xu < xu,max). For a balanced section both reach their limits together (xu = xu,max). For an over-reinforced section the concrete reaches 0.0035 while the steel strain is still below yield (NA low, xu > xu,max). 13.  Define balanced, under- and over-reinforced sections. 2076 Chaitra · Q4a [3 marks]

Balanced — steel reaches 0.87fy as concrete reaches 0.0035 (xu = xu,max). Under-reinforced — steel yields before concrete crushes (xu < xu,max). Over-reinforced — concrete crushes before steel yields (xu > xu,max).
