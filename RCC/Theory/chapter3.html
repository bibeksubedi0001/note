Q1. State all the possible safety and serviceability requirements of limit state, and define the limit state of strength and serviceability. [4] (2072 Kartik, Q1a)

A limit state is the state beyond which a structure (or part of it) becomes unfit for its intended use. In the limit state method the member is designed so that it does not reach any relevant limit state under the appropriate loads. These are grouped into two:

(a) Limit states of collapse — safety requirements. Under the factored (ultimate) loads the structure must have adequate strength and stability against:

  • flexure (bending)
  • shear
  • torsion
  • compression / buckling
  • tension
  • overall stability — overturning and sliding.

(b) Limit states of serviceability requirements. Under the service (working) loads the structure must stay fit for use with respect to:

  • deflection — final ≤ span/250, and ≤ span/350 or 20 mm after finishes/partitions;
  • cracking — surface crack width ≤ 0.3 mm (mild exposure);
  • vibration, and durability & fire resistance.

Limit state of strength (collapse) — the state at which the structure or member reaches its maximum load-carrying capacity and collapse is imminent (rupture of a section, crushing of concrete, buckling, or loss of overall equilibrium).

Limit state of serviceability — the state at which the structure, although it has not collapsed, becomes unfit for normal use because of excessive deflection, cracking or vibration.

Q2. Explain the different categories of limit state design with necessary details. [6] (2075 Ashwin, Q3b)

There are two categories:

1. Limit state of collapse (strength). It safeguards against collapse and is checked for flexure, shear, torsion, compression, tension and stability. It uses factored loads Fd = γf·F (e.g. 1.5(DL+LL)) and design strengths f/γm — 0.446fck for concrete (γm = 1.5) and 0.87fy for steel (γm = 1.15). Because it endangers life, its probability of occurrence is kept very low.

2. Limit state of serviceability. It keeps the structure usable and comfortable under everyday loads and is checked for deflection, cracking and vibration (with durability and fire). It uses service (unfactored) loads (γf = 1.0) and the full elastic material properties.

In short, the collapse limit state deals with strength at ultimate load, the serviceability limit state with performance at working load.

Q3. What are the serviceability requirements in the limit state design of RC structures? Explain them briefly. [4] (2075 Chaitra, Q1b)

Under service loads the structure must remain fit for use; IS 456 lays down:

  • Deflection — final deflection ≤ span/250; the part after partitions/finishes ≤ span/350 or 20 mm (whichever is less). Controlled through the span/effective-depth ratio.
  • Cracking — surface crack width ≤ 0.3 mm (mild), 0.2/0.1 mm (severe). Controlled by bar spacing, bar diameter, minimum steel and cover.
  • Vibration — kept within limits comfortable to the occupants.
  • Durability & fire resistance — adequate cover, concrete grade and detailing for the design life.

Q4. Explain the limit state of serviceability and its requirements in RCC structures. Also list the different types of splicing of reinforcement. [4+1] (2074 Ashwin, Q3a)

The limit state of serviceability is the condition beyond which the structure, though it has not collapsed, becomes unfit for normal use due to excessive deflection, cracking or vibration. Its requirements are: deflection ≤ span/250 (and ≤ span/350 or 20 mm after finishes); crack width ≤ 0.3 mm (mild exposure); acceptable vibration; and adequate durability and fire resistance.

Types of splicing of reinforcement:

  1. Lap splice — overlapping bars transfer force through the concrete. In flexural tension use at least max(Ld, 30φ); direct tension and compression have different limits. Lap lengths are not universally 30φ. Stagger laps and use permitted low-stress locations.
  2. Welded splice — the bars are butt- or lap-welded; used for large bars where lapping would congest the section (weldable steel required).
  3. Mechanical splice (coupler) — a threaded/swaged sleeve joins the bars (parallel-thread, taper-thread or cold-swaged) and develops the full bar strength independently of the concrete.

Q5. Define the limit state of serviceability. Differentiate between short-term and long-term deflection. [4] (2082 Baishakh, Q5a)

Serviceability limit state is the condition beyond which the structure is unsuitable for normal use because of excessive deformation, cracking or vibration, even if collapse has not occurred.

Short-term deflection is the immediate response to load. It depends on the load arrangement, span, boundary conditions and the member's effective rigidity. For a simply supported prismatic beam with uniform rigidity and a uniformly distributed service load, δ = 5wL&sup4/(384EI); for a central point load, δ = PL³/(48EI). For a cantilever under a full-length UDL, δ = wL&sup4/(8EI). These expressions are not interchangeable between support conditions.

Long-term deflection develops with time under sustained loading and imposed strains. Its main components are:

  1. Creep: concrete strain increases under sustained stress. The creep coefficient is the ratio of creep strain to initial elastic strain. In a simplified sustained-load calculation, an effective modulus Ec,eff = Ec/(1 + φ) represents the reduction of stiffness. It must not be applied indiscriminately to transient live load.
  2. Shrinkage curvature: concrete shortens without external loading. Unequal restraint from top and bottom reinforcement produces curvature as well as axial shortening.
  3. Further cracking and construction history: early removal of formwork, temporary stacking loads, the age at loading and the sequence of adding finishes affect the eventual deflection.

Separate the immediate deflection from additional creep and shrinkage effects. Keep track of the date when brittle finishes or partitions are installed: it is the additional deflection after that date that is compared with the finish-damage limit. Compression reinforcement helps by restraining creep and shrinkage deformation, but its effect depends on its area and position.

3.2 Design Strength of Materials & Design Loads 2081

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. Define design strength and design load for the limit state design method. [4] (2081 Baishakh, Q1c)

Q1. Define design strength and design load for the limit state design method. [4] (2081 Baishakh, Q1c)

LSM starts from characteristic values and applies partial safety factors to obtain design values.

Design strength fd = characteristic strengthγm = fγm. The material factor γm allows for the difference between the strength of the material in the structure and in a test specimen, and for the seriousness of the limit state.

  • Concrete: fck/1.5 is the characteristic-strength/material-factor ratio. The concrete design curve also includes the code's 0.67 allowance for strength in the structure, giving a peak stress of approximately 0.446fck; do not confuse this peak with the average stress over the compression block.
  • Steel: fyd = fy1.15 = 0.87fy (γm = 1.15).

Design load Fd = γf × characteristic load. The factor reflects load uncertainty and the relevant limit state. Typical collapse combinations are 1.5(DL+LL), 1.5(DL±WL/EL), 1.2(DL+LL±WL/EL), and 0.9DL±1.5WL/EL when dead load is stabilizing. Serviceability uses the applicable service combinations; not every simultaneous variable action necessarily has coefficient 1.0. See the worked Table 18 example in Chapter 1.

Q1. What do you understand by the idealized stress-strain diagram of concrete and steel bar? Draw the idealized stress-strain diagrams. Define the characteristic strength of concrete and steel. [2+2+2] (2073 Shrawan, Q4b)

Concrete (LSM): the design curve rises as a parabola from the origin to a strain of 0.002, where it reaches the design stress 0.446fck (= 0.67fck/1.5), and then stays constant up to the ultimate strain 0.0035 at which the concrete crushes.

Steel: the curve is linear (slope Es = 2×105 N/mm²) up to the design yield 0.87fy. Mild steel (Fe250) has a sharp yield point and a flat plateau; cold-worked HYSD bars (Fe415/500) have no sharp yield, so a curved branch runs to 0.87fy and fy is taken as the 0.2% proof stress.

Characteristic strength = the strength below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall (the 5% fractile). Assuming a normal distribution, fck = fm − 1.65σ, where fm = mean strength and σ = standard deviation. The diagrams are shown below.

Q2. Define characteristic loads and characteristic strength. Discuss the stress-strain relation for steel and concrete in the limit state method and the working stress method. [1+1+2+2] (2078 Bhadra, Q1a)

Characteristic load = the load with a 95% probability of not being exceeded during the design life (the IS 875 values). Characteristic strength = the strength below which not more than 5% of results fall, fck = fm − 1.65σ.

Stress-strain in LSM (design curves, γm applied): concrete parabolic to 0.002 at 0.446fck then constant to 0.0035; steel linear to 0.87fy then a yield plateau / proof-stress branch (the sketch below).

Stress-strain in WSM: both materials are assumed to remain linearly elastic throughout. The concrete stress is kept ≤ the permissible σcbc and the steel stress ≤ σst, and the two are tied by the modular ratio m = 2803σcbc. There is no plastic branch — the concrete stress diagram is triangular.

Q3. Draw the idealized stress-strain curve for both steel and concrete and discuss the design values of stresses. [5] (2067 Ashadh, Q2c)

The curves are as in the sketch below. The design values follow from dividing the characteristic strengths by the material partial safety factors γm:

  • Concrete: applying the material factor and the code's in-structure strength allowance gives peak design stress 0.67fck/1.5 ≈ 0.446fck, reached at strain 0.002 and held to 0.0035 in the flexural design curve.
  • Steel: fy is reduced by γm = 1.15 ⇒ design stress fy/1.15 = 0.87fy.

The larger γm for concrete (1.5) reflects its greater variability and brittleness; the smaller value for steel (1.15) reflects its more reliable, ductile behaviour.

Q4. Explain the actual and idealized stress-strain diagram of concrete and deformed bars. [3] (2082 Baishakh, Q1b)

Actual concrete curve. A compression test gives a nearly linear initial response, increasing nonlinearity as internal cracking grows, a peak stress and a descending branch. Its form depends on age, confinement, loading rate and specimen geometry. A measured cube curve is not itself the design curve for a structural section.

Idealized concrete curve. For flexural design, use the code's parabolic rise to strain 0.002 and the constant design stress of approximately 0.446fck up to the limiting extreme-fibre strain 0.0035. Integrating this distribution gives the usual 0.36fckbxu resultant at 0.42xu.

Actual deformed-bar curve. Steel initially follows slope Es = 2 × 105 N/mm². A sharp yield plateau is not present in every grade/manufacturing process; where necessary, yield strength is specified by the 0.2% proof-stress convention. At larger strains there may be strain hardening and eventual fracture.

Idealized steel curve. The code supplies a simplified stress-strain relationship incorporating the material factor; its full design stress is approximately 0.87fy. For a compression bar or insufficiently strained tension bar, read the stress at its compatible strain rather than automatically using the full design value. These idealizations make equilibrium calculations repeatable while allowing for material variability.

3.4 Limit States of Collapse

Limit statePhysical mechanismNecessary design checks
FlexureConcrete compression and steel tension form the internal resisting couple.Moment resistance, strain compatibility, minimum tensile strain, reinforcement limits and anchorage.
ShearDiagonal cracking and possible crushing of the concrete compression field.Nominal shear, concrete resistance, maximum stress, transverse steel and detailing.
TorsionPerimeter cracking and space-truss action in a reinforced section.Equivalent bending/shear, additional closed-stirrup demand and longitudinal corner/side bars.
CompressionCombined axial force and bending, with possible second-order amplification.Minimum eccentricity, effective length, axial-moment interaction, slenderness and bar restraint.

A member may have enough flexural capacity yet fail first in shear, anchorage or a connection. The relevant checks must therefore be considered together. Factoring loads does not eliminate the need for equilibrium, a realistic support model or a continuous load path.

3.5 Limit States of Serviceability

Use service-level actions and suitable cracked/time-dependent stiffness to assess performance in use. Final deflection is normally limited to L/250; the increment after partitions and finishes is limited to the smaller of L/350 and 20 mm. Cracking should remain acceptable for appearance, durability and the particular function of the structure.

IS 456's general crack-width guidance distinguishes a usual 0.3 mm limit from tighter limits where exposure makes cracking harmful; it is not a universal table assigning a single value to every exposure class. A serviceability check may use a deemed-to-satisfy span/depth rule or an explicit calculation. Chapter 6 develops cracked sections, creep, shrinkage, modification factors and the crack-width equation in detail.

Q1. What are balanced, under-reinforced and over-reinforced sections? Explain with neat sketches of the stress distributions and expressions for the moment of resistance of each section. [6] (2080 Baishakh, Q1a)

Code-limit terminology: many exam answers call the IS limiting section "balanced". The code's limit includes a minimum post-elastic tensile strain, not merely first yield. Its condition is εst ≥ 0.002 + 0.87fy/Es when the extreme flexural concrete strain is 0.0035. This differs from the WSM condition of reaching two permissible stresses simultaneously.

xu,max/d = 0.0035/[0.0035 + 0.002 + 0.87fy/Es]

At collapse the extreme concrete fibre strain is 0.0035 and the strain varies linearly to the steel. The type is fixed by the neutral-axis depth xu relative to the limiting depth xu,max (at which the steel just reaches its design strain as the concrete reaches 0.0035): xu,maxd = 0.53, 0.48, 0.46 for Fe250, Fe415, Fe500.

The resultant of the parabolic-rectangular concrete distribution is C = 0.36fckbxu at 0.42xu from the top. With tension steel at its design stress, T = 0.87fyAst and z = d − 0.42xu. The 0.446fck peak stress must not be drawn as a uniform block over the whole xu while retaining the 0.36 resultant.

  • Balanced (xu = xu,max): concrete and steel reach their limits together. Mu,lim = 0.36fckb·xu,max(d − 0.42xu,max) = 0.87fyAst,lim(d − 0.42xu,max) = 0.138fckbd² (Fe415).
  • Under-reinforced (xu < xu,max): from C = T, xu = 0.87fyAst0.36fckb, and Mu = 0.87fyAst(d − 0.42xu) = 0.87fyAstd[1 − Astfybd·fck].
  • Beyond the code limit (xu > xu,max): the section fails the minimum tensile-strain requirement and must be redesigned. Its actual ultimate resistance needs compatible steel stresses; merely clipping xu to xu,max does not satisfy force equilibrium for the existing bars.

Q2. Describe the failure modes in under-reinforced, balanced and over-reinforced sections. [4] (2081 Bhadra, Q1b)

  • Under-reinforced → tension (ductile) failure: the steel reaches its yield strain first and stretches, giving wide cracks and large deflection — ample warning before the concrete finally crushes. Safe and preferred.
  • Balanced → the steel yields and the concrete crushes simultaneously (xu = xu,max) — a theoretical boundary case.
  • Compression-controlled ultimate failure: concrete crushing occurs without adequate tensile yielding/rotation and can give little warning. Exceeding the stricter IS strain limit indicates inadequate design ductility, but does not prove that the steel remains wholly elastic.

Q3. Describe under-reinforced, over-reinforced and balanced reinforced concrete sections. [7] (2081 Baishakh, Q1a)

A balanced section has exactly the steel (Ast,lim) needed so that the steel reaches its design stress 0.87fy at the very instant the extreme concrete fibre reaches 0.0035 — the neutral axis is at xu = xu,max, and its moment of resistance is the limiting value Mu,lim = 0.138fckbd² (Fe415).

An under-reinforced section has less steel than balanced, so xu < xu,max: the steel yields and undergoes large strain before the concrete crushes, so failure is gradual and ductile with plenty of warning. This is the basis of all IS 456 flexural design; Mu = 0.87fyAst(d − 0.42xu).

A section with xu > xu,max has less tensile strain than the design minimum. Increase depth or use a suitably designed doubly reinforced section when the specified section cannot satisfy the design moment and strain condition. For an existing section, determine the real steel strain and stress before claiming a physical failure mode or ultimate resistance.

Q4. Discuss different types of flexural sections according to the amount of reinforcement in the tension zone. Describe the different failure modes in the above-mentioned sections. [8] (2080 Bhadra, Q1a)

By the amount of tension steel a singly-reinforced section is balanced, under-reinforced or over-reinforced, decided by xu against xu,max (= 0.53d/0.48d/0.46d for Fe250/415/500):

  • Balanced (xu = xu,max): steel and concrete reach their limits together; MOR = Mu,lim = 0.138fckbd² (Fe415).
  • Under-reinforced (xu < xu,max): steel governs; ductile; Mu = 0.87fyAst(d − 0.42xu).
  • Beyond the design limit (xu > xu,max): minimum tensile strain is not satisfied; redesign the section. Actual ultimate resistance requires compatible steel stresses.

Failure modes: under-reinforced → ductile tension failure (steel yields first, wide cracks/large deflection give warning); balanced → simultaneous yielding and crushing; over-reinforced → brittle compression failure (concrete crushes suddenly). Codes keep sections under-reinforced (xu ≤ xu,max) so that failure is always ductile.

Q5. Explain under-reinforced, balanced and over-reinforced sections in limit state design. [6] (2074 Ashwin, Q1a)

The three types are distinguished by the neutral-axis depth at collapse:

  • Under-reinforced (xu < xu,max): steel reaches 0.87fy and yields before the concrete crushes ⇒ ductile; Mu = 0.87fyAst(d − 0.42xu).
  • Balanced (xu = xu,max): both reach their limits together ⇒ Mu,lim = 0.36fckb·xu,max(d − 0.42xu,max).
  • Beyond the design limit (xu > xu,max): insufficient tensile strain for the code's design condition. A capped limiting moment is not a strain-compatible analysis of the existing section.

Q6. Explain, with the help of sketches, under-reinforced, over-reinforced and balanced sections. [4] (2075 Chaitra, Q1a)

The three cases differ only in the neutral-axis (and hence strain) position at collapse. In every case the top concrete strain is fixed at 0.0035; the steel strain grows as xu becomes smaller:

  • Under — xu < xu,max, large steel strain (well past yield) ⇒ ductile.
  • Balanced — xu = xu,max, steel just at its design strain.
  • Over the code limit — xu > xu,max, tensile strain below the required design minimum.

Q7. Define balanced, under-reinforced and over-reinforced sections. [3] (2076 Chaitra, Q4a)

  • Balanced section — the steel reaches its design stress 0.87fy exactly as the concrete reaches 0.0035 (xu = xu,max).
  • Under-reinforced section — less steel than balanced (xu < xu,max); the steel yields first, giving ductile failure.
  • Over-reinforced section — more steel than balanced (xu > xu,max); the concrete crushes first, giving sudden brittle failure (not permitted).
