11.1 & 11.2 Damage in Earthquakes & Philosophy of Design 20802072

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. Philosophy of design of structures in an earthquake-prone region. Design for strength and ductility. [4+4] (2080 Baishakh, Q4a)
  2. How do you consider earthquake loads while designing RCC structures? Explain briefly. [4] (2072 Kartik, Q2a)

Earthquakes shake a building with inertial lateral forces. Typical damage comes from a soft/weak storey (open ground floor), short-column effect, plan/mass irregularity & torsion, pounding of adjacent buildings, and poor detailing at beam-column joints.

Design philosophy (IS 1893 / IS 13920): it is uneconomical to keep a building fully elastic in a strong quake, so it is designed to—

  • resist minor shaking without damage;
  • resist moderate shaking with only repairable (non-structural) damage;
  • resist the maximum considered shaking without collapse, dissipating energy through controlled ductile yielding.

This is achieved by the strong-column–weak-beam concept (hinges form in beams, not columns) plus ductile detailing. The seismic load itself is taken as the base shear VB = Ah·W with Ah = Z2·IR·Sag (see Ch 1.3), distributed up the height and combined with gravity by 1.5(DL±EL), 1.2(DL+LL±EL) and 0.9DL±1.5EL.

11.3 Design for Strength & Ductility 2082207820732072

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. What is ductility? Significances of ductility in RC structures. [2+3] (2073 Shrawan, Q2b)
  2. Define ductility in RC design. Sketch a beam-column joint with ductile details. [1+4] (2072 Chaitra, Q3b)
  3. What is the ductile behaviour of a structure and how is it induced? [3+3] (2082 Bhadra, Q5b)
  4. Factors affecting ductility. Ductility requirements of a RCC beam as per IS 13920. [4] (2078 Bhadra, Q1c)

Ductility is the ability of a member/structure to undergo large inelastic deformation beyond yield without a significant loss of strength (measured by the ductility ratio μ = ΔuΔy). Significance: it dissipates earthquake energy, gives warning before collapse (large deflections/cracks), and lets moments redistribute — converting a sudden brittle failure into a safe ductile one.

Ductility is induced by keeping sections under-reinforced (xu < xu,max), confining the concrete with closely-spaced closed hoops, limiting the axial load on columns, using adequate but not excessive tension steel, and providing continuous well-anchored reinforcement. Factors affecting ductility: tension-steel ratio (↓ ductility as it ↑), compression steel and confinement (↑ ductility), axial load (↓ ductility), concrete & steel grade, and the shear span.

  6. Confining reinforcement in RC columns for earthquake-resistant design (with sketch). [6] (2075 Ashwin, Q1c)
  7. … plus 2072 Kartik (Q4a), 2074 Chaitra (Q2a), 2080 Bhadra (Q4a).

Beams (IS 13920)

  • At least 2 bars continuous top and bottom; tension steel ratio 0.24√fck/fy ≤ p ≤ 2.5%.
  • Bottom (positive) steel at a joint face ≥ ½ the top steel there; steel at any section ≥ ¼ the maximum.
  • Closed hoops (stirrups) over a length 2d at each end at a spacing ≤ the least of d4, 8φmin, 100 mm; first hoop within 50 mm of the face.

Columns & joints

  • Special confining reinforcement (closely-spaced closed hoops) over a length l0 = max(D, clear height/6, 450 mm) at each end and through the joint; spacing ≤ B4 and 100 mm.
  • Hoop area Ash = 0.18·s·h·fckfy(AgAk − 1); the joint is confined so it does not fail before the members.

Why detailing is critical at joints: the joint transfers large reversing forces between beams and columns; without confinement the concrete core crushes and bars slip, causing brittle failure. Confining hoops keep the core intact so plastic hinges form in the beams (strong-column–weak-beam), giving a ductile, energy-dissipating, collapse-resistant frame.
