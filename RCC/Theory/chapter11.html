Earthquakes shake a building with inertial lateral forces. Typical damage comes from a soft/weak storey (open ground floor), short-column effect, plan/mass irregularity & torsion, pounding of adjacent buildings, and poor detailing at beam-column joints.

11.2 Earthquake-Resistant Design Philosophy

Design philosophy (IS 1893 / IS 13920): it is uneconomical to keep a building fully elastic in a strong quake, so it is designed to—

  • resist minor shaking without damage;
  • resist moderate shaking with only repairable (non-structural) damage;
  • resist the maximum considered shaking without collapse, dissipating energy through controlled ductile yielding.

This is achieved by the strong-column–weak-beam concept (hinges form in beams, not columns) plus ductile detailing. The seismic load itself is taken as the base shear VB = Ah·W with Ah = Z2·IR·Sag (see Ch 1.3), distributed up the height and combined with gravity by 1.5(DL±EL), 1.2(DL+LL±EL) and 0.9DL±1.5EL.

These are performance objectives, not a guarantee that every building will remain undamaged. A complete load path is needed from the floor diaphragm through frames or walls, connections and foundations to the ground. Nonstructural components, stairs, cladding and equipment also require appropriate displacement compatibility and anchorage.

Design Sequence

  1. Select a suitable structural system and a reasonably regular configuration in plan and elevation.
  2. Determine mass, gravity loads, seismic hazard, soil/site class, importance and the response-reduction factor permitted for that system and detailing.
  3. Choose the code-appropriate analysis method; a tall or irregular structure may not qualify for an equivalent-static analysis.
  4. Analyse both principal directions, torsion, relevant combinations and second-order effects. Check drift and the stability of gravity-load-carrying members.
  5. Design flexural yielding regions and protect shear, joints, anchorage and other brittle mechanisms using capacity-design principles.
  6. Detail the reinforcement, diaphragms and foundations consistently with the assumed ductility and inspect their construction.

11.3 Design for Strength & Ductility 2082207820732072

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. What is ductility? What are the significances of ductility in RC structures? [2+3] (2073 Shrawan, Q2b)
  2. Define the term ductility in RC design. Draw a neat sketch of a beam-column joint including ductile details. [1+4] (2072 Chaitra, Q3b)
  3. What do you mean by ductile behaviour of a structure and how do you induce ductile behaviour in a structure? [3+3] (2082 Bhadra, Q5b)
  4. What are the factors affecting ductility? Explain the ductility requirements of a RCC beam as per IS 13920. [4] (2078 Bhadra, Q1c)

Ductility is the ability of a member/structure to undergo large inelastic deformation beyond yield without a significant loss of strength (measured by the ductility ratio μ = ΔuΔy). Significance: it dissipates earthquake energy, gives warning before collapse (large deflections/cracks), and lets moments redistribute — converting a sudden brittle failure into a safe ductile one.

Ductility is induced by keeping sections under-reinforced (xu < xu,max), confining the concrete with closely-spaced closed hoops, limiting the axial load on columns, using adequate but not excessive tension steel, and providing continuous well-anchored reinforcement. Factors affecting ductility: tension-steel ratio (↓ ductility as it ↑), compression steel and confinement (↑ ductility), axial load (↓ ductility), concrete & steel grade, and the shear span.

Strength, Stiffness and Ductility Are Different

Strength is the resistance to force or moment. Stiffness relates load to displacement and controls elastic drift. Ductility is the capacity for inelastic deformation without unacceptable degradation. Adding longitudinal steel can increase strength while reducing curvature ductility and increasing shear demand. A strong member with poor anchorage or unconfined compression bars can still fail in a brittle manner.

Displacement ductility is μΔ = Δu/Δy; curvature ductility is μφ = φu/φy. Define the adopted yield and ultimate points when comparing these ratios. The area within a stable force-displacement hysteresis loop represents energy dissipated in a loading cycle. Pinching, bar slip, shear cracking and strength loss reduce that useful cyclic performance.

Capacity Design and Strong-Column/Weak-Beam Behaviour

Choose where ductile yielding is allowed, then design the other mechanisms for the forces that can develop when those yielding regions reach their specified capacities. For example, beam shear demand must consider the combination of gravity shear and end flexural capacities, not only the elastic-analysis shear. Column shear, joints and anchorage also need protection.

For the applicable IS 13920:2016 strong-column/weak-beam check at a joint, the sum of column moment resistances is required to be at least 1.4 times the sum of beam moment resistances, subject to the code's scope and exceptions. Use the column capacities at the relevant axial loads and evaluate both sway directions. These are member resistances, not simply the bending moments printed by a linear analysis.

The objective is to avoid a storey mechanism formed by hinges in all its columns. Beam yielding at several levels spreads energy dissipation while columns retain gravity-load support. A beam mechanism is not assured by an under-reinforced section alone; shear, confinement, anchorage and joint checks must also pass.

11.4 Role of the Ductile-Detailing Code

IS 456 provides general RC strength, serviceability and ordinary detailing. IS 13920 adds requirements intended to preserve strength and deformation capacity under reversing inelastic demands. These include material ductility, member proportions, reinforcement limits, capacity-based shear, splice locations, joint performance and special confinement. A larger response-reduction factor in the loading code is justified only when the corresponding structural system and detailing are actually provided.

Do not combine a hook-tail dimension from one edition, confinement equations from another and an unrelated national code's system factor. For examination sketches, identify the governing code edition. For construction, use the full adopted clauses and amendments, not a short study table.

11.5 Beam Requirements

  • Provide at least two continuous longitudinal bars at both top and bottom, with the prescribed minimum diameter. In the usual beam formulation, ρmin = 0.24√fck/fy, where ρ is an area ratio; the maximum on a face is 0.025, or 2.5%. Do not compare the decimal ratio directly with a numerical percentage.
  • Bottom (positive) steel at a joint face ≥ ½ the top steel there; steel at any section ≥ ¼ the maximum.
  • Closed hoops (stirrups) over a length 2d at each end at a spacing ≤ the least of d4, 8φmin, 100 mm; first hoop within 50 mm of the face.
  • Provide close hoops around other potential yielding regions as required; outside the special end regions apply the remaining seismic spacing limits, not automatically the ordinary 300 mm limit.
  • Check capacity-based shear for both directions of moment reversal and provide the required transverse reinforcement without relying on bent-up bars as its substitute.
  • Avoid beam lap splices within joints and critical end/yielding regions. Stagger permitted laps and provide the specified transverse confinement over them.

Example: for b = 250 mm, d = 450 mm, M25 and Fe415, ρmin = 0.0028916, giving As,min = 325.30 mm² on the relevant face. Two 16 mm bars provide 402.12 mm², but the flexural demand can require more. With the smallest longitudinal bar 16 mm, the close-hoop spacing bound is min(112.5, 128, 100) = 100 mm over 2d = 900 mm from the joint face. Put the first hoop within 50 mm. This is a detailing example, not a flexural or shear capacity check for a loaded beam.

Column Confinement

  • Provide special confining reinforcement over an end length lo at least the largest of the larger column dimension, one-sixth the clear height and 450 mm, with the required continuation into joint/foundation regions.
  • Check the special hoop-spacing limits for the adopted edition. A conservative study selection takes the least of one-quarter of the minimum member dimension, six times the smallest longitudinal-bar diameter and 100 mm; the required confinement area can demand even closer spacing.
  • Restrain the longitudinal bars with closed hoops and cross-ties, not just widely spaced rectangular links. Use properly detailed seismic hooks directed into the core, with the hook extension required by the adopted edition.
  • Place permitted column laps away from joints and critical end regions, normally in the designated central portion, with the required confinement and limits on the fraction spliced at one level.
  • Check situations needing confinement beyond the ordinary end lengths, including short columns, changes in stiffness, discontinuities and particularly high compression demand.

In the classical rectangular-hoop expression, Ash = 0.18sh(fck/fyh)(Ag/Ak − 1), where Ag is gross column area and Ak is the confined core area measured according to the code. Apply the required lower-bound confinement area and all other applicable conditions as well. Ash is the effective transverse leg area in the direction being checked, not the total length or weight of a hoop.

Illustrative area comparison: take a 400 × 500 mm column, core dimensions 320 × 420 mm measured to the outside of hoops, M25, fyh = 415 MPa and s = 100 mm. For h = 420 mm, the classical expression gives 222.29 mm². Comparing with a 0.09shfck/fyh lower-bound expression gives 227.71 mm². Three effective 10 mm legs provide 235.62 mm². Check the perpendicular direction separately and detail the actual hoop/cross-tie arrangement that provides those effective legs. This comparison does not replace the full adopted confinement clause.

Confinement keeps the compression core effective after cover spalling, delays buckling of longitudinal bars, improves deformation capacity and helps maintain bond under load reversals. It is therefore needed because of cyclic deformation demand as well as ordinary shear.

Beam-Column Joints

The joint transfers forces between intersecting members while also carrying column axial force. Under reversed lateral loading, beam bar forces can reverse sign and create large joint shear. Interior, exterior and corner joints have different restraint and anchorage conditions; a detail suitable for an interior joint cannot simply be copied to an exterior one.

  1. Establish joint shear from the equilibrating beam and column forces at the relevant capacities and load combination.
  2. Check the effective joint area and permitted shear resistance for its confinement category.
  3. Continue column confinement through the joint as required. Do not omit joint hoops merely because beam bars make installation inconvenient.
  4. Ensure sufficient column/joint dimensions and anchorage for the beam bars, especially at an exterior joint where they terminate.
  5. Avoid laps and uncontrolled bar terminations inside the joint. Coordinate the cage so concrete can be placed and compacted through the intersection.

Why detailing is critical at joints: the joint transfers large reversing forces between beams and columns; without confinement the concrete core crushes and bars slip, causing brittle failure. Confining hoops keep the core intact so plastic hinges form in the beams (strong-column–weak-beam), giving a ductile, energy-dissipating, collapse-resistant frame.

Answering the Repeated Detailing Questions

For a beam question, show continuous top/bottom bars, support reinforcement proportions, close-hoop end lengths, first-hoop location, hoop anchorage and permitted splice zones. For a column question, show main-bar restraint, special end confinement, lap location and the required hoop/cross-tie area. For a joint question, show the actual beam-bar load path, column bars and joint-core hoops, then explain shear transfer, bond and moment reversal.

Do not claim earthquake resistance from a drawing of a few closely spaced stirrups alone. The analysis, strength hierarchy, member proportions, drift, anchorage, foundation load path and construction quality must support the assumed ductile mechanism.
