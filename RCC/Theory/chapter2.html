2.1 Basic Assumptions in Working Stress Design

The working stress method checks a member under its unfactored service actions. It is an elastic design model with code-prescribed permissible stresses. Its moment of resistance is the moment at which the first permissible stress is reached; it is not the physical ultimate collapse moment.

  1. Plane sections before bending remain plane after bending, so longitudinal strain varies linearly with depth.
  2. Bond is adequate to prevent significant relative slip; the steel and surrounding concrete have compatible strain at the same level.
  3. Concrete and steel are treated as elastic within the working-stress range.
  4. Concrete in the cracked tensile zone is neglected in flexural resistance. Tensile force is carried by reinforcement.
  5. Steel is replaced by equivalent concrete area using the prescribed modular ratio m. For singly reinforced cracked-section analysis, the equivalent tensile area is mAst.
  6. Stresses calculated under each relevant service combination must not exceed the appropriate permissible values. Checks for shear, anchorage, deflection and detailing are additional requirements.

Notation: b is breadth, D is overall depth, d is depth from the compression face to the centroid of tension steel, x is neutral-axis depth, and z is the internal lever arm. For one layer of bars inside links, d = D − nominal cover − link diameter − half the main-bar diameter. An explicitly supplied effective cover already measures to the bar centroid and must not have the link and bar radius subtracted again.

Q1. Using the working stress method, design a rectangular section 300 mm wide and 450 mm deep carrying 30 kN/m load over an effective span of 3.6 m. Use mild steel and M20 concrete. [4] (2071 Chaitra, Q1a)

Assumptions: interpret 450 mm as overall depth, adopt an effective cover of 50 mm, and treat 30 kN/m as the total service UDL. Then b = 300 mm, d = 400 mm, m = 13.333, σcbc = 7 and σst = 140 N/mm². If the stated load excludes self-weight, the loading calculation must be revised.

M = 30 × 3.6²/8 = 48.60 kN·m
Xc = [13.333 × 7/(13.333 × 7 + 140)] × 400 = 160 mm
Mbal = 0.5 × 7 × 300 × 160 × (400 − 160/3) = 58.24 kN·m

Since M < Mbal, a steel-controlled solution exists. Set fst = 140 and use strain compatibility fc = 140x/[m(d − x)] together with M = 0.5fcbx(d − x/3). Solving gives x = 148.745 mm, z = 350.418 mm, and Ast = M/(140z) = 990.65 mm². Concrete stress is 6.216 N/mm², below 7.

A practical choice is four 20 mm bars, Ast = 1256.64 mm². Re-analysis of the provided section gives x = 162.781 mm, fc = 5.757 N/mm² and fst = 111.861 N/mm² at 48.60 kN·m. Both permissible-stress checks pass. Its WSM classification changes slightly after rounding up the steel; ultimate ductility requires its own LSM check. Bar fit, shear and development length remain separate design checks.

The Working Stress Method (WSM) assumes both concrete and steel are linearly elastic. Plane sections remain plane, concrete resists no tension, and the two materials are tied through the modular ratio m = 2803σcbc. Members are proportioned so that the stresses under service (working) loads stay within permissible limits.

2.3 Behaviour of a Beam under Loading

Initially an uncracked beam has nearly linear stress and strain distributions. With increasing moment, the tension face reaches the concrete's flexural tensile strength and vertical cracks develop. The tension steel then carries the principal tensile force, the neutral axis moves toward the compression face, and the cracked stiffness is lower. Bond between cracks makes the actual member somewhat stiffer than an ideal section with all tensile concrete ignored.

At still higher loads the steel may yield, concrete compression becomes nonlinear, and an elastic WSM calculation no longer describes the actual response. Depending on reinforcement and detailing, ultimate failure may be ductile in flexure or brittle through concrete crushing, shear or anchorage. The permissible-stress threshold is intentionally below those ultimate states.

Q1. What are the different types of RCC section of a rectangular beam? Explain with neat sketches. [4] (2082 Bhadra, Q1a)

By reinforcement arrangement, a rectangular section is singly reinforced when the flexural tension is carried by reinforcement without a designed compression-steel contribution, and doubly reinforced when both tension and compression steel contribute to the resisting couple.

In working stress design, the critical neutral-axis depth is Xc = dmσcbc/(mσcbc + σst). If the actual x is smaller than Xc, steel reaches its permissible stress first; if equal, both permissible stresses are reached together; if larger, concrete controls the permissible moment. These are elastic design limits, not physical crushing or yielding.

At ultimate flexure, a tension-controlled section develops substantial steel strain before concrete crushing. A compression-controlled section can crush with limited warning. The IS limiting neutral-axis depth enforces a minimum tensile strain; a section beyond that limit must be redesigned, rather than accepted by simply ignoring excess reinforcement.

Sketch: draw the rectangular concrete section, compression face, tension bars and neutral axis; show the neutral axis successively deeper for the steel-controlled, limiting and compression-controlled cases. The strain line is straight, with tensile and compressive strains on opposite sides of zero. Ductility also requires adequate shear resistance, anchorage and confinement.

By the amount of tension steel a rectangular RC section is classified by comparing the actual neutral axis x with the critical (balanced) neutral axis Xc:

  • Balanced section (x = Xc) — concrete and steel reach their permissible stresses simultaneously.
  • Under-reinforced in WSM (x < Xc) — steel reaches its permissible tensile stress first; steel controls the working-stress moment of resistance.
  • Over-reinforced in WSM (x > Xc) — concrete reaches its permissible compressive stress first; concrete controls the working-stress moment of resistance.

By reinforcement arrangement: singly-reinforced (tension steel only) and doubly-reinforced (tension + compression steel, used when the moment exceeds the balanced capacity or the depth is restricted).

Distinguish the ultimate failure discussion: at collapse, a tension-controlled beam develops substantial steel strain before concrete crushing, while a compression-controlled beam may crush with limited warning. These physical modes are evaluated using ultimate strain compatibility, as discussed in Chapter 3. Reaching a WSM permissible stress is not yielding or crushing. A section described as over-reinforced by WSM's elastic limits is not automatically over-reinforced by the LSM strain limit.

Q12. A reinforced concrete beam of size 300 mm × 640 mm overall is reinforced with 4 bars of 20 mm diameter at the bottom. The beam carries a superimposed load of 50 kN/m, excluding its self-weight, over a span of 4 m. Find the actual stresses developed in steel and concrete. The neutral-axis equation gives x = 208.968 mm, and z = 530.344 mm. Therefore fc,top = 2 × 109.60 × 106/(300 × 208.968 × 530.344) = 6.593 MPa, while fst = 109.60 × 106/(1256.64 × 530.344) = 164.454 MPa.

Because compression stress decreases linearly from the top to zero at x, the stress 50 mm below the top is fc,50 = 6.593(208.968 − 50)/208.968 = 5.015 MPa. The requested output is actual stress; a safety verdict would additionally need the applicable permissible limits.

Q13. A simply supported rectangular RC beam of effective span 4.2 m and overall dimensions 230 mm × 450 mm is reinforced with four 20 mm diameter bars in tension. Determine the moment of resistance. Take permissible stresses for M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. [6] (2074 Ashwin, Q1b)

1. Geometry and working-stress data. The paper omits cover. Adopt an explicitly assumed effective cover of 45 mm, giving d = 450 − 45 = 405 mm. Use the M20/Fe415 permissible bending stresses. Use b = 230 mm and d = 405 mm. The tension area is Ast = 4π(20)²/4 = 1256.64 mm². The permissible stresses are 7 MPa in concrete and 230 MPa in steel; m = 13.3333. No ultimate load factor is applied in this working-stress analysis.

2. Neutral axis. From equal first moments of the cracked transformed section, bx²/2 = mAst(d − x). The positive root gives x = 180.754 mm. The critical depth is Xc = dmσcbc/(mσcbc + σst) = 116.907 mm. The lever arm is z = d − x/3 = 344.749 mm.

3. Independent permissible moments. Concrete permits Mc = 0.5σcbcbxz = 50.163 kN·m; steel permits Ms = σstAstz = 99.642 kN·m. Therefore MR = min(Mc, Ms) = 50.163 kN·m.

4. Stresses at the permissible moment of resistance. With M = 50.163 kN·m, fc = 2M/(bxz) = 7.000 MPa, and fst = M/(Astz) = 115.791 MPa. Use M in N·mm in the stress equations.

Q14. The moment of resistance of a rectangular reinforced-concrete beam of width b and effective depth d is 0.85bd², using N and mm. The extreme concrete and steel stresses must not exceed 7 N/mm² and 140 N/mm², respectively, and the modular ratio is 18.33. Determine the ratio of neutral-axis depth from the compression face to effective depth. The beam is reinforced on the tension side only. [12] (2067 Ashadh, Q1b)

1. Write the dimensionless moment equation. Let k = x/d. Since M = 0.85bd² and the concrete compression diagram is triangular, 0.85 = 0.5fck(1 − k/3). Compatibility also gives fst = mfc(1 − k)/k.

2. Find the controlling stress. Taking fc = 7 MPa in the first equation would give a shallow neutral axis and a steel stress greater than 140 MPa. That root cannot be the permissible moment of resistance. Instead set fst = 140 MPa, and substitute fc = 140k/[18.33(1 − k)].

0.85 = [140/(2 × 18.33)]k²(1 − k/3)/(1 − k)

3. Solve and verify. The physical root between zero and one is x/d = 0.39404. The accompanying concrete stress is 4.9666 MPa, less than 7 MPa, while the steel is at 140 MPa. Thus steel governs this working-stress resistance. The statement must not be interpreted as requiring both permissible stresses to occur simultaneously, because that would conflict with the supplied moment coefficient.

Analysis method (used for every numerical)

For a rectangular cracked section, the compression stress diagram is triangular. Its resultant is C = 0.5fcbx, acting x/3 from the compression face. The tensile resultant is T = fstAst, acting at depth d. Equal forces and strain compatibility give:

fst/fc = m(d − x)/x
0.5fcbx = fstAst
bx²/2 = mAst(d − x)
x = [√((mAst)² + 2bmAstd) − mAst]/b

The lever arm is z = d − x/3. Independently calculate Mc = 0.5σcbcbxz and Ms = σstAstz, then take MR = min(Mc, Ms). The smaller value enforces both stress limits without guessing which material governs. To find stresses at any given service moment, use fc = 2M/(bxz) and fst = M/(Astz).

The equivalent inertia method gives the same answer: Itr = bx³/3 + mAst(d − x)², fc = Mx/Itr, and fst = mM(d − x)/Itr. Use N, mm and N·mm consistently; multiply kN·m by 106 before calculating stress.

  1. Steel & modular ratio: Ast = Σn·π4φ²;   m = 2803σcbc.
  2. Critical NA: Xc = kcd, where kc = mσcbcmσcbc + σst.
  3. Actual NA (transformed area about the NA): b2x² = mAst(d − x) ⇒ solve the quadratic.
  4. Compare: x ≤ Xc → under-reinforced (steel governs); x > Xc → over-reinforced (concrete governs).
  5. MOR: under-reinforced → M = σstAst(d − x3); over-reinforced → M = ½σcbcb·x(d − x3).
