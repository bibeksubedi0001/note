6.3 Control of Deflection in Design 2065

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. How can deflection be controlled in a beam? Explain briefly. [4] (2065 Shrawan, Q2a)
  2. Principle of the sufficient-stiffness method to control deflection; how the deflection of a RC flexure member is controlled. [6] (2065 Kartik, Q3a)

Deflection checks are also embedded in every beam & slab design — see Ch 4 (2079, 2072) and Ch 7.

IS 456 limits deflection to keep a member serviceable: the total deflection ≤ span250, and the deflection occurring after construction (which could damage finishes/partitions) ≤ span350 or 20 mm, whichever is less.

The practical sufficient-stiffness (span/depth) method avoids an explicit deflection calculation: deflection is kept within limits by controlling the span-to-effective-depth ratio. The basic ratios are 7 (cantilever), 20 (simply supported) and 26 (continuous), each multiplied by modification factors kt (tension steel), kc (compression steel) and kf (flanged section). Keeping the actual spand below the allowable value guarantees adequate stiffness.

Other ways to reduce deflection: increase the depth (most effective, EI ∝ D³), add compression steel (reduces long-term creep deflection), use a higher concrete grade, reduce the span, or provide a camber / prestress.

6.4 Control of Cracking in Design 20762073

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS:

  1. Describe the method of controlling deflection and cracking in RCC structures. [2+4] (2076 Chaitra, Q2a)
  2. Methods of controlling deflection and crack width; empirical formula for the design surface crack width. [3+2] (2076 Ashwin, Q5c)
  3. Discuss the methods of crack control as per IS 456:2000. [5] (2073 Shrawan, Q3b)

Cracking is unavoidable in RC (concrete is weak in tension) but must be kept fine so it does not spoil appearance or admit moisture that corrodes the steel. The permissible surface crack width is 0.3 mm for mild exposure (0.2 mm / 0.1 mm for more severe exposure).

Methods of crack control (IS 456):

  • Use several small-diameter bars well distributed rather than a few large bars — more bars = closer, finer cracks.
  • Limit the clear spacing of bars (deemed-to-satisfy spacing rules) and provide adequate minimum steel.
  • Provide sufficient (but not excessive) cover, and side-face reinforcement for deep beams.
  • Keep the steel service stress low and use deformed bars for better bond.

The design surface crack width is estimated by the empirical formula:

Wcr = 3 acr εm1 + 2(acr − cmin)/(D − x)

where acr = distance from the point to the nearest bar surface, cmin = minimum cover, εm = average strain at the level considered (net of a tension-stiffening allowance), D = overall depth and x = neutral-axis depth.
