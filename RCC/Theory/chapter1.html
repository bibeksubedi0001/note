Q1. Describe the requirement of steel as reinforcement in RCC structures. Explain the moment of resistance of a doubly reinforced section. Derive the formula. [2+2+4] (2076 Chaitra, Q1a)

Requirement of steel. Concrete has a comparatively low tensile strength and cracks under flexural tension. Longitudinal reinforcement carries tension across those cracks; compression reinforcement shares compressive force; stirrups resist diagonal tension and restrain longitudinal bars. Distributed reinforcement controls shrinkage and temperature cracks. Adequate bond, anchorage and confinement allow the steel and concrete to act together and develop useful ductility.

Doubly reinforced section. Reinforcement is provided in both the tension and compression zones. This is useful when the beam depth is restricted, when the required moment exceeds the singly reinforced limiting resistance, or where compression reinforcement is needed for deformation control. Reinforcement on both faces is also needed where bending reverses.

Derivation using the limit state method. Let b be the beam width, d the tension-steel effective depth, d′ the compression-steel depth and xu the neutral-axis depth. The concrete compression resultant is Cc = 0.36fckbxu, acting at 0.42xu from the compression face. The net compression-steel force is Cs = (fsc − fcc)Asc, where fcc is the stress in the concrete displaced by the bars.

0.36fckbxu + (fsc − fcc)Asc = fstAst
MR = 0.36fckbxu(d − 0.42xu) + (fsc − fcc)Asc(d − d′)

The moment expression follows by taking moments of both compression forces about the tension steel. Obtain fsc and fst from compatible strains, with εsc = 0.0035(1 − d′/xu). For a section meeting the tensile-strain requirement, fst = 0.87fy. At the design limit, use xu,max and add the compression-steel couple to the limiting concrete couple. Do not assume that compression steel has yielded or that the displaced concrete is on its stress plateau without checking its strain.

Q2. Write the characteristics of steel reinforcing bars used in reinforced concrete structures. [5] (2065 Kartik, Q1a)

Reinforcing bars must develop the required force, remain compatible with concrete and tolerate fabrication and service deformation. Their important characteristics are:

  1. Specified strength: a reliable characteristic yield or proof strength suitable for the design grade.
  2. Ductility: sufficient elongation and inelastic deformation capacity to avoid sudden fracture and permit the intended structural response.
  3. Bond: an appropriate surface profile; ribs improve mechanical interlock with concrete when cover and confinement are adequate.
  4. Bendability and suitable weldability: bars must withstand permitted bends without cracking; welding is used only with suitable steel and an approved procedure.
  5. Consistent dimensions and stiffness: controlled diameter, area and quality, with elastic modulus normally taken as 2 × 105 N/mm².
  6. Durability and cyclic suitability: sound, clean bars protected by adequate concrete cover and suitable for any specified fatigue or seismic demand.

Mild steel can exhibit a distinct yield plateau, while steel without a clear yield point is specified using a proof stress. The grade alone does not establish weldability or seismic ductility; those properties must satisfy the relevant material specification.

Reinforced concrete (RC) is a composite material in which concrete and reinforcement act together through bond. Concrete is relatively strong in compression and weak in tension; its specified grade, rather than a universal 20–30 MPa range, defines its characteristic compressive strength. Steel carries tension across cracks and can also contribute compression, shear resistance and confinement.

The designation M25 means a characteristic 28-day cube compressive strength of 25 N/mm². Fe415 denotes a specified characteristic yield/proof strength of 415 N/mm²; it does not identify every aspect of ductility, manufacture or weldability. A designer must also consider concrete workability, compaction, curing, durability, creep and shrinkage, and the steel's elongation and bond characteristics.

Requirement / role of steel as reinforcement

  1. Carries tensile stresses in the tension zone of flexural members (beams, slabs) that concrete cannot resist.
  2. Resists diagonal tension due to shear and torsion (stirrups, bent-up bars).
  3. Shares compression and reduces member size (columns, doubly-reinforced beams).
  4. Controls shrinkage & temperature cracking (distribution / nominal steel).
  5. Imparts ductility, so failure is gradual (steel yields, giving warning) instead of sudden.

Steel and concrete act compositely because of their good mutual bond, nearly equal coefficient of thermal expansion (≈ 12×10−6/°C), and the alkaline concrete cover that protects the steel from corrosion and fire.

Characteristics of good reinforcing bars

  1. High tensile / yield strength (fy = 250–550 N/mm²).
  2. A specified yield or proof strength: mild steel has a pronounced yield point; steels without one use a defined proof stress. Do not assume that every Fe415/Fe500 bar is cold-worked merely from its grade.
  3. Adequate ductility / percentage elongation, so the member fails in a ductile manner and hooks/bends can be formed safely.
  4. Good bond with concrete — deformed (ribbed/twisted) bars bond far better than plain bars.
  5. Weldability and bendability without cracking.
  6. Uniform quality and a constant modulus of elasticity Es = 2×105 N/mm².
  7. Suitable performance under the expected cyclic/fatigue demand. Ordinary reinforcing steel is not inherently corrosion-proof; durable concrete, cover and crack control protect it.

Common grades: Mild steel Fe250, TOR/HYSD Fe415, and TMT Fe500/Fe550.

Advantages & limitations of RC

  • Advantages: strong against structural and service loads, mouldable into any shape, durable with a long life, good fire/weather resistance, low maintenance, and can be made earthquake-resistant to the desired extent.
  • Limitations: heavy self-weight, low tensile strength of concrete, cracking, difficulty in later modification/demolition, and quality depends heavily on workmanship and curing.

Q1. Explain different types of design methods used in reinforced concrete structure design. [6] (2069 Chaitra, Q1a)

Three main methods have been used in reinforced-concrete design:

  1. Working stress method: calculate stresses under service loads using an elastic cracked-section model and ensure that concrete and steel remain within their permissible stresses. The modular ratio transforms steel into an equivalent concrete area. It is convenient for service-stress calculations but does not directly measure the ultimate collapse margin.
  2. Ultimate load method: determine resistance at collapse using inelastic material behaviour and factored loading. It recognizes strength beyond the working-stress range, but a strength-only calculation does not establish acceptable cracking or deflection at normal loads.
  3. Limit state method: check both collapse and serviceability with appropriate actions, resistance models and partial factors. It combines adequate strength with acceptable performance in use and is the usual basis of IS 456 design.

The same member geometry can behave differently under working and ultimate loads. Do not use a WSM triangular compression diagram with LSM ultimate design stresses, or claim adequate serviceability merely from an ultimate moment check.

Q5. Why is the limit state method better than the working stress method? Explain in brief. [5] (2072 Chaitra, Q2b)

  1. It provides adequate safety against collapse and adequate serviceability at working load through separate limit states, whereas WSM only limits the elastic stress.
  2. It uses separate partial safety factors for each load and material, reflecting their different uncertainties, instead of one arbitrary factor of safety.
  3. It is probability-based (characteristic values), giving a rational and realistic margin of safety.
  4. It uses the true plastic stress block at collapse, so sections are more economical.
  5. It accounts for load combinations (DL, LL, WL, EQ) with appropriate factors.

These are advantages of the design framework, not a guarantee that every LSM section will be smaller. Durability, deflection, minimum reinforcement and construction constraints can still govern.

By reinforcement arrangement, a rectangular section is singly reinforced when the flexural tension is carried by reinforcement without a designed compression-steel contribution, and doubly reinforced when both tension and compression steel contribute to the resisting couple.

In working stress design, the critical neutral-axis depth is Xc = dmσcbc/(mσcbc + σst). If the actual x is smaller than Xc, steel reaches its permissible stress first; if equal, both permissible stresses are reached together; if larger, concrete controls the permissible moment. These are elastic design limits, not physical crushing or yielding.

At ultimate flexure, a tension-controlled section develops substantial steel strain before concrete crushing. A compression-controlled section can crush with limited warning. The IS limiting neutral-axis depth enforces a minimum tensile strain; a section beyond that limit must be redesigned, rather than accepted by simply ignoring excess reinforcement.

Sketch: draw the rectangular concrete section, compression face, tension bars and neutral axis; show the neutral axis successively deeper for the steel-controlled, limiting and compression-controlled cases. The strain line is straight, with tensile and compressive strains on opposite sides of zero. Ductility also requires adequate shear resistance, anchorage and confinement.

Q7. Compare the factor of safety used in the Working Stress Method and the partial safety factor used in the Limit State Method for concrete and steel. [4] (2067 Ashadh, Q5a)

WSM limits working-load stresses to code-prescribed permissible values. These contain material safety allowances, but there is not one universal factor shared by concrete, steel and all stress states. Typical bending-compression values 5, 7 and 8.5 MPa for M15, M20 and M25 are roughly one-third of the respective cube strengths; tensile steel limits such as 140 MPa for mild steel and 230 MPa for Fe415 must be taken from the appropriate provisions. An approximate factor is an explanation, not a replacement for the specified permissible stress.

LSM uses partial safety factors: on materials γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel (design strengths 0.446fck and 0.87fy); and on loads γf (1.5 for DL+LL; 1.2 or 1.5 for combinations with WL/EQ). Separate factors reflect the different variabilities of concrete, steel and the loads.

An RC section can be designed by three philosophies:

  1. Working Stress Method (WSM) — based on elastic (linear) theory. The section is proportioned so that stresses under service (working) loads stay within permissible values (= material strength ÷ a factor of safety). Concrete and steel are combined through the modular ratio m. Simple, but conservative, uneconomical, and it gives no true measure of the collapse margin.
  2. Ultimate Load Method (ULM) — the section is designed for the collapse load = service load × load factor, using the actual (plastic) stress block at failure. Uses the reserve strength (economical) but ignores serviceability (deflection, cracking) at working load.
  3. Limit State Method (LSM) — ensures the structure does not reach any limit state: the limit state of collapse (strength) and the limit states of serviceability (deflection, cracking, vibration). Partial safety factors γf on loads and γm on materials are applied to characteristic values, giving a rational, probability-based and economical design (adopted by IS 456:2000).

Q1. Define characteristic loads and characteristic strength. Discuss the stress-strain relation for steel and concrete in the Limit State Method (LSM) and Working Stress Method (WSM). [1+1+2+2] (2078 Bhadra, Q1a)

Characteristic strength is the strength below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall. For concrete, fck is the specified 28-day cube compressive strength. If test strengths are reasonably represented by a normal distribution, fck = fmean − 1.65s, where s is the standard deviation. The steel characteristic strength is its specified yield or proof strength.

Characteristic load is the load with a 95% probability of not being exceeded during the design life under the code's definition. The relevant loading standards provide the adopted dead, imposed, wind and other characteristic actions.

Design values: apply load factors to characteristic actions and material factors to characteristic strengths. Thus Fd = γfFk and fd = fk/γm. Characteristic values describe the statistical/design basis; design values include the safety allowances for the limit state being checked.

In LSM: Concrete — parabolic from the origin to a strain of 0.002 reaching the design stress 0.446fck, then constant up to the ultimate strain 0.0035. Steel — linear (Es = 2×105) up to the design yield 0.87fy; mild steel has a flat yield plateau, HYSD is idealised with a 0.2% proof stress.

In WSM: both materials are assumed linearly elastic; concrete stress ≤ σcbc, steel stress ≤ σst, related through the modular ratio m = 2803σcbc.

Q3. How do you consider earthquake loads while designing RCC structures? Explain briefly. [4] (2072 Kartik, Q2a)

Earthquake load is an inertial lateral load. Following IS 1893, the design horizontal seismic coefficient is Ah = Z2·IR·Sag, where Z = zone factor, I = importance factor, R = response-reduction factor (larger for ductile detailing) and Sa/g = spectral acceleration for the fundamental period T.

The design base shear VB = Ah·W (W = seismic weight = DL + a fraction of LL) is distributed up the height (∝ Wihi²) as storey forces, and the frame is analysed for them. EQ load is combined with gravity by the factored combinations 1.5(DL±EL), 1.2(DL+LL±EL) and 0.9DL±1.5EL, and members are given ductile detailing as per IS 13920.

The design process of an RC structure follows these steps: (1) fix the layout and estimate member sizes; (2) work out the characteristic loads (DL, LL, WL, EQ per IS 875 / IS 1893); (3) analyse the structure to get design actions; (4) form the factored load combinations (IS 456 Table 18); (5) design each member for the most critical combination at the limit state of collapse; (6) check the limit states of serviceability (deflection, cracking); and (7) detail the reinforcement per IS 456 / IS 13920.

1.5 Relevant Codes and the Design Basis

A design code specifies the minimum acceptable reliability, analysis assumptions, material properties, resistance equations and reinforcement details. It does not replace structural analysis or the engineer's judgement. The following references belong to the IS-code framework used in these examination questions; for an actual project in Nepal, establish the governing Nepal National Building Code, adopted editions and approval requirements before starting the design.

Information to establish before a calculation

  1. Geometry and restraints: distinguish clear, centre-to-centre and effective spans; establish whether supports permit rotation, translation or uplift.
  2. Materials and exposure: record fck, fy, concrete unit weight, exposure class, nominal cover and fire-resistance requirements. A concrete grade is a specified characteristic strength, not a universal strength of all concrete.
  3. Load status: identify whether each action is characteristic/service or already factored. Add self-weight only when it is excluded, and do not multiply an already factored load by 1.5 again.
  4. Analysis model: obtain moments, shears, axial forces and torsion for each compatible combination. A column's effective length for buckling is not automatically its physical loaded height.
  5. Resistance and serviceability: compare design actions with resistance, then check deflection, cracking, stability and durability using the appropriate load level.
  6. Construction details: select actual bars, recheck the effective depth and capacity, and provide an intelligible reinforcement arrangement with cover, spacing, anchorage, laps and transverse steel.

Study scope: these are educational notes in the examination's IS-code framework, not construction-ready designs. Where a paper omits a support condition, material grade or dimension, an explicitly stated study assumption must not be mistaken for supplied question data.
