1a. A four-lane divided urban road (two lanes each direction) is to be constructed on A-7-6 soil (highly plastic clay) with a soaked CBR of 2.5%. Sketch a typical cross-section labeling carriageway width, median, footpath, and camber. If camber is 2.5%, calculate the vertical rise (mm) from the edge to the centre for a 7.0 m wide carriageway (single side).
[3]

Typical Cross-Section of a Four-Lane Divided Urban Road

Calculation of Vertical Rise (Camber)

Given:

  • Carriageway width (one side, edge-to-edge of one direction) $W = 7.0$ m
  • Camber = 2.5% (i.e. slope $1{:}n$ with $n = 40$)
  • The crown is at the centre of the 7.0 m carriageway, so the rise is over the half-width $W/2 = 3.5$ m

For a straight (two-line) camber, the rise of the crown above the edge is:

$$\text{Vertical Rise} = \text{Camber slope} \times \frac{W}{2} = \frac{W}{2n}$$ $$= 0.025 \times \frac{7.0}{2} = 0.025 \times 3.5 = 0.0875 \text{ m}$$

Equivalently, using the lecture-note form:

$$\frac{W}{2n} = \frac{7.0}{2 \times 40} = \frac{7.0}{80} = 0.0875 \text{ m}$$
∴ Vertical rise from edge to crown (centre) = 87.5 mm

Note: A common error is to multiply the camber by the full width (0.025 × 7.0 = 175 mm). That value corresponds to the rise of a 5% camber on a 7 m carriageway, or to the full‐width drop on a 2.5% one‑way crossfall — not to the edge‑to‑crown rise of a 2.5% crowned camber.

1b. The laboratory CBR test was performed on a remoulded sample at optimum moisture content. The client insists that the field CBR should be at least 5% to reduce pavement thickness. As a highway engineer, explain why using the soaked CBR value is critical for this soil type, and why increasing the field CBR to 5% may be practically difficult.
[3]

Why Soaked CBR Is Critical for A-7-6 Soil

1. Nature of A-7-6 Soil (Highly Plastic Clay)

A-7-6 soils are highly plastic clays with liquid limit > 40% and plasticity index > 11. These soils have a strong affinity for water and undergo significant volume change (swelling) when saturated.

2. Why Soaked CBR Must Be Used

  • Worst-case simulation: The soaked CBR test (specimen soaked for 4 days) simulates the worst moisture condition the subgrade will experience during monsoon or waterlogging. For A-7-6 soil, the soaked CBR (2.5%) is far below the unsoaked value because clay swells and loses strength dramatically when wet.
  • Design safety: Pavement design must be based on the weakest condition. If unsoaked CBR is used, the pavement will be under-designed and fail prematurely when the subgrade becomes wet.
  • IRC/AASHTO recommendation: Standard practice (IRC:37, AASHTO) mandates soaked CBR for subgrade evaluation, especially for cohesive soils.

3. Why Achieving Field CBR of 5% Is Practically Difficult

  • Inherent weakness: A-7-6 soils have very low shear strength when saturated. Even with compaction at OMC, the soaked CBR rarely exceeds 2–3%.
  • Swelling behaviour: These soils swell 3–5% by volume upon wetting, destroying any improvement achieved by compaction.
  • Stabilization needed: To achieve CBR ≥ 5%, chemical stabilization (lime/cement) or replacement with better material would be required, significantly increasing cost.
  • Moisture sensitivity: Field moisture conditions are uncontrollable; seasonal variations will reduce CBR below 5% regardless of initial compaction.
Conclusion: For A-7-6 soil, the soaked CBR of 2.5% must be used for design. Achieving a sustained field CBR of 5% is impractical without soil stabilization or subgrade replacement.

Relevant Codes

  • AASHTO M145 — Soil classification (A-7-6 group)
  • IS:2720 Part 16 / AASHTO T193 — Laboratory CBR test (4-day soaked)
  • IRC:37-2018 — Guidelines for design of flexible pavements (subgrade CBR ≥ 5% requirement)
  • Nepal Road Standard (NRS) 2070 & DoR Standard Specifications for Road & Bridge Works 2073 — Subgrade material requirements
1c. The aggregate for the granular base is required to have a Los Angeles abrasion value not exceeding 30%. Two aggregate sources give average values of 28% and 32%. Which one would you select, and what additional test would you mandate to ensure performance under heavy traffic? Justify.
[2]

Aggregate Selection

Selection: The aggregate with LA abrasion value of 28% should be selected because it meets the specification requirement of ≤ 30%. The aggregate with 32% LA abrasion value does not meet the specification and must be rejected.

Justification

  • LA abrasion value measures resistance to degradation by abrasion and impact. Lower values indicate harder, more durable aggregates.
  • 28% < 30% (limit) → Acceptable
  • 32% > 30% (limit) → Not acceptable

Additional Test for Heavy Traffic

For heavy traffic conditions, the following additional test should be mandated:

  • Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) Test (IS:2386 Part IV): This test measures resistance to sudden shock or impact loads, which are critical under heavy axle loads. The AIV should not exceed 30% for base course under heavy traffic.

Other useful supplementary tests include:

  • Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV): Should not exceed 30% for base course
  • Soundness Test: To assess durability against weathering (sodium/magnesium sulphate test)
∴ Select aggregate with LA abrasion = 28%. Additionally mandate the Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) test to ensure resistance to impact under heavy traffic.

2a. A two-lane two-way highway has a design speed of 90 km/h. The coefficient of longitudinal friction (f) is 0.35, and the standard perception-reaction time is 2.5 seconds. Calculate the Stopping Sight Distance (SSD) on a level grade using the standard formula.
[2]

Stopping Sight Distance (SSD) – Level Grade

Given:

  • Design speed, $V = 90$ km/h
  • Coefficient of friction, $f = 0.35$
  • Perception-reaction time, $t = 2.5$ s
  • Grade, $n = 0$ (level)

Convert speed to m/s:

$$v = \frac{V}{3.6} = \frac{90}{3.6} = 25 \text{ m/s}$$

Formula:

$$\text{SSD} = vt + \frac{v^2}{2gf}$$

Step 1: Lag distance (perception-reaction distance)

$$d_1 = v \times t = 25 \times 2.5 = 62.5 \text{ m}$$

Step 2: Braking distance

$$d_2 = \frac{v^2}{2gf} = \frac{25^2}{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.35} = \frac{625}{6.867} = 91.02 \text{ m}$$

Step 3: Total SSD

$$\text{SSD} = 62.5 + 91.02 = \boxed{153.52 \text{ m}}$$
2b. Now consider the road has a falling gradient of 4% (downhill). A driver not familiar with the area takes 3.0 seconds to react. Recalculate the SSD for this downhill section and comment on the percentage increase compared to the level grade SSD.
[5]

SSD on 4% Downhill Grade

Given:

  • $V = 90$ km/h → $v = 25$ m/s
  • $f = 0.35$
  • $t = 3.0$ s (unfamiliar driver)
  • Gradient, $n = 4\% = 0.04$ (downhill, so braking is harder)

Formula for SSD on grade:

$$\text{SSD} = vt + \frac{v^2}{2g(f \pm n)}$$

For downhill, the gradient opposes braking: use $(f - n)$

Step 1: Lag distance

$$d_1 = v \times t = 25 \times 3.0 = 75.0 \text{ m}$$

Step 2: Braking distance on downhill

$$d_2 = \frac{v^2}{2g(f - n)} = \frac{625}{2 \times 9.81 \times (0.35 - 0.04)} = \frac{625}{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.31} = \frac{625}{6.0822} = 102.76 \text{ m}$$

Step 3: Total SSD

$$\text{SSD}_{\text{downhill}} = 75.0 + 102.76 = \boxed{177.76 \text{ m}}$$

Percentage Increase

$$\% \text{ increase} = \frac{\text{SSD}_{\text{downhill}} - \text{SSD}_{\text{level}}}{\text{SSD}_{\text{level}}} \times 100 = \frac{177.76 - 153.52}{153.52} \times 100 = \frac{24.24}{153.52} \times 100 = 15.79\%$$

Comments

  • The SSD increases by approximately 15.8% on the downhill section compared to the level grade.
  • This increase is due to two compounding effects:
    1. Increased reaction time (3.0 s vs 2.5 s) → 12.5 m additional lag distance
    2. Downhill gradient reduces effective braking friction from $f = 0.35$ to $(f - n) = 0.31$, increasing braking distance by 11.74 m
  • This has significant design implications: longer sight distances must be provided on downgrades, especially for unfamiliar drivers. Warning signs and additional safety measures are needed.
∴ SSD on 4% downhill = 177.76 m — an increase of 15.79% over the level grade SSD of 153.52 m.

2d. Explain why the stopping sight distance calculated from the above method must be checked against the horizontal curve setback and vertical curve length requirements.
[2]

SSD Check Against Curve Geometry

1. Horizontal Curve Setback

On horizontal curves, the driver's line of sight may be obstructed by objects on the inside of the curve (cut slopes, walls, buildings, vegetation). Even if the SSD is numerically adequate on a tangent section, the actual available sight distance on the curve may be less than SSD due to lateral obstructions.

  • The required setback distance (m) from the centre of the inner lane to the obstruction must be calculated using: $$m = R\left(1 - \cos\frac{S}{2R}\right)$$
  • If insufficient setback is available, either the obstruction must be removed or the speed must be reduced.

2. Vertical Curve Length

On summit (crest) curves, the pavement surface itself obstructs the driver's view. The vertical curve must be long enough so that the SSD is available over the crest.

  • The minimum length of vertical curve for adequate SSD is: $$L = \frac{NS^2}{2(\sqrt{2h_1} + \sqrt{2h_2})^2} \quad \text{(when } S < L\text{)}$$ where $N$ = algebraic difference of grades, $h_1$ = driver eye height (1.2 m), $h_2$ = object height (0.15 m)
  • On sag (valley) curves, headlight beam range limits nighttime visibility.

Why Both Checks Are Necessary

The SSD formula gives the required stopping distance based on speed, friction, and reaction time. However, the available sight distance depends on the road geometry. If the horizontal setback or vertical curve length is inadequate, the driver cannot see the obstruction in time, even though the braking distance itself is sufficient.

∴ SSD must be cross-checked with horizontal setback and vertical curve length to ensure that the required sight line is geometrically available to the driver.

Relevant Codes

  • IRC:73-1980 — Geometric design standards for rural (non-urban) highways (SSD values)
  • IRC:SP:23-1993 — Vertical curves for highways (h₁ = 1.2 m, h₂ = 0.15 m)
  • IRC:38-1988 — Guidelines for design of horizontal curves (setback distance)
  • Nepal Road Standard (NRS) 2070 & Nepal Rural Road Standard (NRRS) 2071 — SSD and geometric requirements
  • AASHTO Green Book (2018) — A Policy on Geometric Design of Highways and Streets
3a. A national highway in hilly terrain has a circular horizontal curve of radius 200 m. Design speed is 70 km/h, maximum allowable superelevation (e) is 7%. Pavement width on the tangent is 7.0 m (two lanes, two-way). The curve is fully superelevated with the cross-section rotated about the centre line. Calculate the design superelevation rate for this curve using the method that considers both friction and superelevation. If the calculated e exceeds the maximum allowable, what design decision would you take? Show the calculation and explain.
[4]

Superelevation Design

Given:

  • Design speed, $V = 70$ km/h
  • Radius, $R = 200$ m
  • Maximum allowable superelevation, $e_{\max} = 7\% = 0.07$
  • Two-lane, two-way road; width = 7.0 m

Step 1: Calculate e + f required for equilibrium

$$e + f = \frac{V^2}{127R} = \frac{70^2}{127 \times 200} = \frac{4900}{25400} = 0.1929$$

Step 2: Determine superelevation using IRC step method (75% design speed, f = 0)

Per IRC:73 / IRC:38, superelevation is first computed for 75% of the design speed assuming friction is fully neglected (f = 0):

$$e = \frac{(0.75V)^2}{127R} = \frac{V^2}{225R} = \frac{70^2}{225 \times 200} = \frac{4900}{45000} = 0.1089 = 10.89\%$$

Step 3: Check against maximum allowable

The calculated $e = 10.89\%$ exceeds $e_{\max} = 7\%$.

Design Decision

Since $e > e_{\max}$, we limit the superelevation to the maximum allowable value:

$$\boxed{e_{\text{design}} = e_{\max} = 7\% = 0.07}$$

The remaining lateral force is to be resisted by friction:

$$f_{\text{required}} = \frac{V^2}{127R} - e = 0.1929 - 0.07 = 0.1229$$

Safety Check

The maximum allowable coefficient of lateral friction is typically $f_{\text{allowable}} = 0.15$ (IRC/AASHTO for 70 km/h).

$$f_{\text{required}} = 0.1229 < f_{\text{allowable}} = 0.15 \quad \checkmark \text{ Safe}$$
∴ Design superelevation = 7% (maximum allowable). Required friction = 0.123, which is within the allowable limit of 0.15. The design is safe.

Relevant Codes

  • IRC:73-1980 — e_max = 7% for plain/rolling terrain; 10% for hilly/snow-free
  • IRC:38-1988 — Step procedure for superelevation design (75% design speed method)
  • NRS 2070 (Nepal) — e_max = 7% for hill roads; lateral friction f = 0.15
  • AASHTO Green Book — Method 5 (distributed e and f)
3b. Determine the extra widening required on this curve. Consider both mechanical widening and psychological widening.
[3]

Extra Widening on Horizontal Curve

Given:

  • Number of lanes, $n = 2$
  • Radius, $R = 200$ m
  • Design speed, $V = 70$ km/h
  • Assume wheelbase of longest vehicle, $l = 6.0$ m (standard truck)

1. Mechanical Widening ($W_m$)

Due to off-tracking of rear wheels on a curve:

$$W_m = \frac{nl^2}{2R} = \frac{2 \times 6.0^2}{2 \times 200} = \frac{72}{400} = 0.18 \text{ m}$$

2. Psychological Widening ($W_{ps}$)

Due to tendency of drivers to shy away from the edge and oncoming traffic on curves:

$$W_{ps} = \frac{V}{9.5\sqrt{R}} = \frac{70}{9.5\sqrt{200}} = \frac{70}{9.5 \times 14.142} = \frac{70}{134.35} = 0.521 \text{ m}$$

3. Total Extra Widening ($W_e$)

$$W_e = W_m + W_{ps} = 0.18 + 0.521 = \boxed{0.70 \text{ m}}$$

The total pavement width on the curve should be:

$$W_{\text{curve}} = W_{\text{tangent}} + W_e = 7.0 + 0.70 = 7.70 \text{ m}$$
∴ Extra widening required = 0.70 m (mechanical: 0.18 m + psychological: 0.52 m). Total width on curve = 7.70 m.

Relevant Codes

  • IRC:73-1980 & IRC:SP:73-2018 — Extra widening on horizontal curves
  • NRS 2070 (Nepal) — Extra widening provisions for hill roads
  • Standard wheelbase $l = 6.0$ m assumed (per IRC:3-1983 design vehicle)
3c. There is a rock cutting on the inside of the curve that obstructs the driver's view. The required setback distance (m) from the centre line of the inner lane to the obstruction needs to be calculated. The stopping sight distance (S) for 70 km/h (using f = 0.35, reaction time 2.5 sec, level grade) is 113.6 m. The length of the curve (L) is 150 m, which is greater than S. Calculate setback distance from the outer edge of the road carriageway. Also, state one critical assumption you are making in applying this formula.
[3]

Setback Distance Calculation

Given:

  • Radius, $R = 200$ m (to centre line of road)
  • Stopping Sight Distance, $S = 113.6$ m
  • Length of curve, $L = 150$ m > $S$ → Use $L > S$ formula
  • Lane width = 3.5 m per lane (two lanes, total 7.0 m)

Step 1: Setback from Centre Line of Inner Lane

When $L \geq S$, the setback distance from the centre of the inner lane is:

$$m = R\left(1 - \cos\frac{S}{2R}\right)$$
$$\frac{S}{2R} = \frac{113.6}{2 \times 200} = \frac{113.6}{400} = 0.284 \text{ radians}$$
$$\cos(0.284) = 0.9599$$
$$m = 200 \times (1 - 0.9599) = 200 \times 0.0401 = 8.02 \text{ m}$$

Step 2: Setback from Outer Edge of Carriageway

The setback calculated above is measured from the centre line of the inner lane. To measure from the outer edge of the carriageway, we add the distance from the outer edge to the centre of the inner lane:

$$d = \text{one full lane width} + \frac{\text{inner lane width}}{2} = 3.5 + \frac{3.5}{2} = 3.5 + 1.75 = 5.25 \text{ m}$$
$$m_{\text{from outer edge}} = m + d = 8.02 + 5.25 = \boxed{13.27 \text{ m}}$$

Critical Assumption

Key Assumption: The sight line is a chord of the circular curve, and the driver's eye and the object are both located on the centre line of the inner lane. The formula assumes the sight distance is measured along the curve (arc length), the obstruction is a continuous wall/rock face parallel to the road, and the radius R = 200 m is approximated as the radius of the inner-lane centreline (small correction of ±1.75 m neglected).

Relevant Codes

  • IRC:66-1976 — Recommended practice for sight distance on rural highways
  • IRC:38-1988 — Setback distance on horizontal curves (formulas for L ≥ S and L < S)
  • NRS 2070 / DoR Nepal — Sight distance and clearance requirements on curves
3d. Explain why a transition curve is essential in this situation, particularly with the computed extra widening and superelevation.
[2]

Necessity of a Transition Curve

A transition curve (spiral/clothoid) is essential between the tangent section and the circular curve for the following reasons:

1. Gradual Introduction of Superelevation

  • The superelevation changes from normal camber (2–2.5%) on the tangent to 7% on the circular curve.
  • Without a transition, this change would be abrupt, causing a sudden jerk and discomfort to passengers.
  • The transition curve allows the superelevation to be gradually developed over its length at a uniform rate.

2. Gradual Introduction of Extra Widening

  • The extra widening of 0.70 m must be introduced gradually, not abruptly.
  • The transition curve provides a length over which the pavement can be progressively widened from 7.0 m to 7.70 m.
  • Abrupt widening creates an unsightly and unsafe kink in the road edge.

3. Gradual Change in Curvature

  • On the tangent, curvature = 0 (radius = ∞). On the circular curve, curvature = 1/R = 1/200.
  • The transition curve provides a gradual change of radius from ∞ to 200 m, allowing the centrifugal force to build up smoothly.
  • Without it, the centrifugal acceleration would change instantaneously, causing lateral jerk and potential loss of control.

4. Driver Comfort and Safety

  • The rate of change of centripetal acceleration is kept within comfortable limits (typically 0.5–0.8 m/s³).
  • Particularly important in hilly terrain at 70 km/h with $R = 200$ m, where lateral forces are significant ($e + f = 0.193$).
∴ A transition curve is essential to gradually introduce the superelevation (0% → 7%), extra widening (0 → 0.70 m), and curvature (∞ → 200 m), ensuring passenger comfort, driver safety, and aesthetic road alignment.

Relevant Codes

  • IRC:38-1988 — Length of transition (spiral) curve: $L_s = \max(L_{\text{comfort}}, L_{\text{rate of SE}}, L_{\text{empirical}})$
  • NRS 2070 / DoR Nepal — Transition curve requirements for hill roads
  • Standard rate of change of centripetal acceleration: $C = 80/(75 + V) = 0.55$ m/s³ for V = 70 km/h

Justification: Design as an Arterial Road

The proposed highway should be designed as a Minor Arterial for the following reasons:

  1. Strategic connectivity: The road is the sole link connecting the new city to the national highway network. This is a classic arterial function — providing mobility between a major urban centre and the national network.
  2. Population growth: The city is projected to grow from 50,000 to 200,000 (4× growth). At 200,000 population, the city qualifies as a sub-metropolitan city in Nepal. Traffic demand on this corridor will be substantial.
  3. Traffic projection: Using typical trip generation rates (0.5–1.0 trips/person/day for intercity), the corridor could see 20,000–40,000 daily trips at build-out, warranting arterial design.
  4. Multi-modal corridor: The road will also serve as a freight corridor (construction materials, goods supply for the growing city), requiring higher design standards.
  5. Hierarchical placement: In the road hierarchy, this road sits directly below the national highway → it is a feeder/arterial, not a collector.
∴ The highway should be designed as a Minor Arterial (with provision for future 4-laning), not a collector road. Its function is primarily mobility — connecting a growing city to the national network.
4b. The authority is debating between multimodal integration (co-locating the airport and railway station) versus keeping them separate. Using the concept of land use-transport interaction, argue for one approach and explain how it would affect travel demand and urban form.
[2]

Multimodal Integration vs Separation

Recommended Approach: Multimodal Integration (Co-location)

The integrated multimodal hub approach — co-locating the airport and railway station — is strongly recommended for the following reasons based on the Land Use-Transport Interaction (LUTI) model:

1. Land Use-Transport Feedback Loop

  • The LUTI cycle states: Transport investment → Accessibility change → Land use change → Activity change → Travel demand change
  • A single integrated hub creates a high-accessibility node, attracting commercial, hospitality, and logistics land uses around it.
  • This concentrates development in a Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) pattern, reducing sprawl.

2. Impact on Travel Demand

  • Reduced inter-modal transfer trips: Passengers transferring between rail and air do not need separate trips across the city.
  • Reduced total VMT (Vehicle Miles Traveled): Concentration of transport facilities reduces private vehicle dependency.
  • Shared last-mile connectivity: One bus/taxi system serves both modes, improving efficiency.

3. Impact on Urban Form

  • Compact urban growth: Development concentrates around the hub, creating a defined urban core rather than scattered development.
  • Efficient infrastructure: Shared road infrastructure, parking, utilities → lower infrastructure cost per capita.
  • Polycentric development: The hub becomes a secondary centre, with the city core and transport hub forming a dual-centre urban structure appropriate for a growing city.
∴ Multimodal integration is recommended. It creates a high-accessibility hub, reduces total travel demand by 15–25%, promotes compact TOD-style urban form, and is more cost-efficient for a new city of 200,000 population.
