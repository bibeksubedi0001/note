3.1 Introduction, types and structural components of pavement208220802073

Past Exam Questions on this Topic

  1. Describe the functions of pavement layers. What are the different types of joints used in the rigid pavement? Explain in brief: [4+4] (2082 Bhadra, Q5)
    Solution

    Functions of flexible-pavement layers:

    • Wearing/surface course: smooth skid-resistant riding surface, resists abrasion, waterproofs.
    • Binder course: transfers load to base, adds stability.
    • Base course: main load-spreading layer.
    • Sub-base: spreads load further, drainage & frost protection, stops subgrade intrusion.
    • Subgrade: compacted natural soil that finally carries the load.

    Joints in rigid pavement: expansion, contraction, construction, warping (hinge) and longitudinal joints.

  2. Describe four types of rigid pavement with simple sketch: Discuss the importance of standard axle load in flexible pavement design; [5+3] (2080 Bhadra, Q6)
    Solution

    Types of rigid (concrete) pavement: Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP), Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP), Continuously Reinforced Concrete Pavement (CRCP), and Prestressed Concrete Pavement (PCP).

    Importance of standard axle load: pavements are designed for a standard 80 kN single axle. Mixed axle loads are converted to equivalent standard axles using the fourth-power law VDF = (axle load/80)4, giving a common basis to accumulate traffic damage (CSA/ESAL).

  3. What are the advantages of rigid pavement over flexible pavement? Explain the concept of cumulative standard axle load. (2073 Shrawan, Q5)
    Solution

    Advantages of rigid over flexible pavement: longer design life and durability; low maintenance cost; slab action spreads load over a wide area (good over weak subgrade); unaffected by oil/fuel spillage; better night visibility (light colour); and lower life-cycle cost.

    Cumulative standard axle load (CSA): mixed traffic is converted to a common 'standard axle' (80 kN) using the equivalent axle-load factor, and the repetitions are accumulated over the design life with traffic growth:

    CSA = 365 × A × D × F × VDF × [(1 + r)n − 1]/r, where A = initial CVPD, D = lane-distribution factor, F = directional factor, VDF = vehicle damage factor, r = growth rate and n = design life; it expresses the total design traffic in millions of standard axles (msa) used for pavement thickness design.

3.2 Factors controlling pavement design20812078

Past Exam Questions on this Topic

  1. Explain the various factors affecting the pavement design. [8] (2081 Bhadra, Q5)
    Solution

    Factors affecting pavement design:

    • Traffic & loading: wheel load, axle configuration, contact pressure, load repetitions (ESAL/CSA), traffic growth.
    • Subgrade: strength (CBR/MR), moisture, drainage, frost.
    • Climate: temperature, rainfall, frost action.
    • Materials: quality and moduli of the pavement layers.
    • Others: gradient, lane width, drainage.
  2. What are the factors that affect in pavement design? How are they considered in AASHTO method? [1+7] (2081 Baishakh, Q5)
    Solution

    Factors: traffic & loading (ESAL), subgrade resilient modulus MR, materials, environment, drainage and reliability.

    AASHTO method: the 1993 equation relates design ESAL (W18) to the structural number SN, reliability (ZR), S0, serviceability loss ΔPSI and MR: log W18 = ZRS0 + 9.36 log(SN+1) − 0.20 + [log(ΔPSI/2.7)]/[0.40 + 1094/(SN+1)5.19] + 2.32 log MR − 8.07. Then SN = a1D1 + a2D2m2 + a3D3m3 gives the layer thicknesses.

  6. A road pavement is to be designed for & stretch of road with the following pavement layers: Minimum thickness of asphalt concrete on the surface course 50 mm. Well graded crushed stone aggregate for base course; CBR value 90% (iii) Fairly graded graved for sub-base course, CBR Value=-20% Giv) Compacted Soil, CBR value 10% 90r percentile sub grade CBR Value ='4% The road has single lane carriage way &,caters present ADT of 1200 commercial vehicle per day with annual growth of 6%.     Solution

    IRC CBR-curve method (single-lane road).

    Design traffic: A = P(1 + r)(n + x) with present P = 1200 cvpd, r = 6%, design life n = 10 yr and x = 0.5 yr up to completion → A = 1200(1.06)10.5 = ≈ 2213 cvpd.     Layer build-up — re-enter curve F at each layer's CBR and take the differences:

    • Over compacted soil (CBR 10%): cover needed ≈ 34 cm → compacted-soil layer = 55 − 34 = 21 cm

    • Over well-graded base (CBR 90%): cover needed ≈ 8 cm → base layer = 21 − 8 = 13 cm

    Section (top→bottom): 8 cm AC / 13 cm WBM base (CBR 90) / 13 cm sub-base (CBR 20) / 21 cm compacted soil (CBR 10) / subgrade (CBR 4); total = 55 cm.

    Same IRC CBR-curve procedure as Marsani's worked example (ADT 1200, curve F, 55 cm over 4% subgrade, cover 21 cm at CBR 20% and 8 cm at CBR 90%); the per-layer covers are chart read-offs from IRC design curve F.

  2. Write down the assumptions and analysis of Westergaard's Theory. How are the warping stress and friction stress developed in the rigid pavement? [8] (2076 Ashwin, Q6)
    Solution

    Westergaard's assumptions: slab is homogeneous, isotropic and elastic; subgrade behaves like a dense liquid (reaction ∝ deflection, Winkler); uniform slab thickness; full slab-subgrade contact; load over a circular area at interior/edge/corner.

    Warping stress comes from restrained temperature differential across the slab (σ = C·E·α·Δt/2 at edge). Frictional stress comes from subgrade resistance to slab expansion/contraction (σf = γ·f·L/(2×104)), and it governs the contraction-joint spacing.

  3. Differentiate between flexible pavement design and rigid pavement design. Describe Westergaard's concept for temperature stresses (2072 Chaitra, Q5)
    Solution

    Flexible vs rigid design: flexible pavement transmits load grain-to-grain to the subgrade (design limits subgrade deformation - CBR/ESAL layered approach); rigid pavement resists load by slab flexural (beam) action spreading it over a wide area (design limits concrete flexural stress vs modulus of rupture - Westergaard/IRC approach).

    Westergaard temperature (warping) stress: interior σ = EαΔt/2·(Cx+μCy)/(1−μ2); edge σ = C·EαΔt/2; corner σ = EαΔt√(a/l)/[3(1−μ)].

  2. Explain the stresses in rigid pavement. Write down the design procedure of dowel bars at expansion joints. [8] (2078 Bhadra, Q10)
    Solution

    Stresses in rigid pavement: wheel-load stresses (interior, edge, corner - Westergaard), warping (temperature) stresses and frictional stresses; the critical combinations are checked against the concrete modulus of rupture.

    Design of dowel bars at expansion joints: compute l; take the effective group within 1.8l; find the capacity of one dowel in shear, bending and bearing (permissible values); balance them for the diameter/embedment (joint width δ enters the bending/bearing length); distribute dowels to carry ~40% of the design wheel load.

  4. What are the factors affecting rigid pavement design? Write down the steps of IRC design guidelines for rigid pavement. (2074 Ashwin, Q5)
    Solution

    Factors affecting rigid-pavement design: wheel load & repetitions, modulus of subgrade reaction k, concrete flexural strength (modulus of rupture), temperature differential, and joint spacing.

    IRC rigid-pavement design steps: (1) select wheel load, k, E, μ, α, flexural strength; (2) estimate design traffic; (3) assume trial slab thickness; (4) compute l and Westergaard edge/corner load stresses; (5) compute warping stresses; (6) check the critical combination ≤ allowable flexural strength; (7) revise thickness; (8) design joints (spacing, dowel & tie bars) and reinforcement.
