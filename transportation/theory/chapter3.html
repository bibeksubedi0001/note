  1. Describe the functions of pavement layers. What are the different types of joints used in the rigid pavement? Explain in brief: [4+4] (2082 Bhadra, Q5)
    Solution

    (a) Functions of the pavement layers (top → bottom):

    LayerFunction
    Wearing / surface courseProvides a smooth, skid-resistant, dust-free riding surface; resists abrasion and the direct wheel load; waterproofs the layers below.
    Binder courseLower part of the bituminous surfacing; takes the high shear from the wheel load and hands it to the base with a coarser, cheaper mix.
    Base courseThe main load-spreading layer — distributes the wheel load over a wider area so that the stress reaching the sub-base is small.
    Sub-baseSpreads the load further, provides drainage, prevents the subgrade from intruding into the base, and acts as a frost/capillary cut-off and a working platform.
    SubgradeCompacted natural soil (top 300-500 mm) that finally carries the load; its CBR / MR governs the total crust thickness.

    (b) Joints in rigid pavement

    • Expansion joint — full-depth transverse gap (20-25 mm) with a compressible filler and sealant; allows the slab to expand in summer. Load transfer by dowel bars (one end free to slide). IRC spacing ≈ 140 m on rough sub-base / 90 m on smooth sub-base.
    • Contraction joint — a groove cut to ≈ ¼ of the depth so that shrinkage/contraction cracks form at a chosen line; spacing ≤ 4.5 m for plain slabs. Load transfer by aggregate interlock (and dowels for thick slabs).
    • Construction joint — provided wherever concreting stops for the day or is interrupted; made to coincide with a contraction joint wherever possible.
    • Warping (hinge) joint — provided to relieve the stresses caused by warping; the slabs are held together by tie bars, so no movement is permitted.
    • Longitudinal joint — provided when the carriageway is wider than 4.5 m; it takes care of longitudinal cracking due to warping and differential settlement. Tie bars are used (deformed bars, fully bonded at both ends).

    Layer functions and joint types & spacings as given in Manual.pdf, Highway Pavement (Ch.2, book pp.113-115) and IRC joint recommendations, book p.168.

  2. Describe four types of rigid pavement with simple sketch: Discuss the importance of standard axle load in flexible pavement design; [5+3] (2080 Bhadra, Q6)
    Solution

    (a) Four types of rigid (cement-concrete) pavement

    TypeDescription / sketch
    JPCP — Jointed Plain Concrete PavementPlain concrete slabs, no temperature steel; closely spaced contraction joints (≤ 4.5 m) with dowel bars for load transfer. Sketch: slab – joint – slab, dowels across the joint.
    JRCP — Jointed Reinforced Concrete PavementLight mesh reinforcement (2-4 kg/m²) holds the intermediate cracks tight so that joints can be spaced wider (10-15 m); dowels at the joints. Sketch: longer slab with mesh at mid-depth.
    CRCP — Continuously Reinforced Concrete Pavement0.6-0.7% continuous longitudinal steel; no transverse contraction joints — fine transverse cracks at 0.6-2 m are held tightly closed by the steel. Sketch: continuous slab with hairline cracks.
    PCP — Prestressed Concrete PavementPre-compressed slab, so the flexural tensile stress under load is largely cancelled; allows a much thinner slab and very long panels (100 m+). Sketch: slab with prestressing tendons.

    (b) Importance of the standard axle load

    • Highway traffic is a mixture of axles of very different magnitude. Pavement damage is not proportional to load — it follows the fourth-power law, so a single heavy axle can do the damage of thousands of car axles.
    • A common yardstick is therefore fixed: the standard axle = 80 kN (8.16 t) single axle with dual wheels. Every other axle is converted with the equivalent-axle-load factor EF = (axle load / 80)4, and per vehicle VDF = Σ(axle load/80)4.
    • The whole mixed traffic then becomes one number — the cumulative standard axles CSA = 365·A·[((1+r)n−1)/r]·LDF·VDF (msa) — which is the single traffic input for the IRC-37/DoR catalogues and for the fatigue and rutting criteria.
    • It also gives a rational basis for legal axle-load limits, for overloading penalties and for comparing the damage caused by different vehicle classes.

    Manual.pdf, Highway Pavement — loads and factors controlling pavement design (book pp.116-121) and the CSA/VDF definition used with the IRC catalogues.

  3. What are the advantages of rigid pavement over flexible pavement? Explain the concept of cumulative standard axle load. (2073 Shrawan, Q5)
    Solution

    (a) Advantages of rigid pavement over flexible pavement

    • Slab (beam) action distributes the wheel load over a very wide area, so a weak subgrade is much less critical — the subgrade stress is only a fraction of that under a flexible pavement.
    • Longer design life (30-40 yr against 15-20 yr) and much lower maintenance.
    • High flexural strength; no rutting, shoving, corrugation or bleeding.
    • Unaffected by oil, fuel and chemical spillage — important at junctions, bus bays, toll plazas and parking areas.
    • Light coloured surface → better night visibility and lower lighting cost.
    • Lower rolling resistance → better fuel economy for heavy vehicles; lower life-cycle cost.
    • Not softened by high temperature and not damaged by standing water.

    (b) Concept of cumulative standard axle load (CSA)

    Mixed traffic cannot be used directly for design, so every axle is first converted to the number of standard 80 kN axles that would cause the same damage:

    EF = (axle load / 80 kN)⁴ VDF = Σ EF summed over all the axles of one vehicle

    The daily commercial traffic is then projected to the year of completion, distributed to the design lane and accumulated over the design life with the growth series:

    A = P (1 + r)ˣ x = years from the count to completion CSA = 365 × A × [((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)/r] × LDF × VDF

    where P = commercial vehicles per day at the last count, r = growth rate, n = design life, LDF = lane distribution factor (single lane 1.0, two-lane single carriageway 0.75, four-lane single carriageway 0.40, dual two-lane 0.75 of each direction) and VDF = vehicle damage factor.

    CSA, expressed in million standard axles (msa), is the traffic input with which the pavement composition is picked from the IRC-37 / DoR design catalogue, and against which the fatigue-cracking and rutting criteria are checked.

    Lane-distribution factors and the design-traffic equation are taken verbatim from Manual.pdf book p.122 (§2.4 — Lane distribution factor) and §2.4.1.

Types of Pavement

Depending on structural behavior:

  • Flexible Pavement
  • Rigid Pavement
  • Semi-Rigid Pavement
  • Composite Pavement

Flexible Pavement

  • Low flexural strength
  • Deflects momentarily under load but rebounds on removal of load
  • Load transmission to the subgrade by lateral distribution with grain to grain point of contacts
  • Designed in layers, empirical method

Rigid Pavement

  • Cement concrete slab
  • High flexural strength
  • Load distribution by slab action
  • Permanent settlements
  • Design by structural analytical techniques

Semi-Rigid Pavement

  • Base, subbase of bonded materials such as pozzolanic cement, lean cement concrete, soil cement
  • Flexible pavement surface course
  • Higher flexural strength than flexible pavement but much lower than that of a rigid pavement

Composite Pavement

  • PCC bottom layer, HMA top layer
  • Ideal pavement
  • Very expensive, rarely used in new construction
  • Mostly rehabilitation of PCC using asphalt overlays

  1. Explain the various factors affecting the pavement design. [8] (2081 Bhadra, Q5)
    Solution

    The design of a pavement is controlled by four groups of factors.

    1. Traffic and loading factors

    • Axle load — the wheel load is transmitted to the surface through the tyre; the heavier the wheel load the thicker the pavement. Design is governed by the commercial vehicles.
    • Wheel configuration — single / dual / tandem wheels change the stress distribution; a dual wheel set is converted to an Equivalent Single Wheel Load (ESWL).
    • Axle configuration — single, tandem, tridem axles carry more load with less damage per tonne; converted by the fourth-power law.
    • Number of load repetitions — accumulated as CSA (= 365·A·[((1+r)n−1)/r]·LDF·VDF).
    • Contact area / tyre pressure — fixes the radius of the loaded area a; for thin pavements the tyre pressure governs, for thick pavements the total load governs.
    • Vehicle speed — slow (creep) traffic loads the layer far longer, lowering the resilient modulus of the bituminous layers and increasing rutting; critical on gradients and at junctions.

    2. Material properties (including the subgrade)

    • Subgrade strength expressed as soaked CBR or resilient modulus MR (MR = 10.3×CBR MPa); the design value is the 87.5 / 90 percentile of the tested samples.
    • Elastic / resilient moduli and Poisson's ratios of the bituminous, granular and cemented layers; for repeated loading the resilient modulus is the correct parameter.
    • Quality, grading and durability of aggregates; binder type and content.

    3. Environmental factors

    • Temperature — changes the modulus of the bituminous layer (softening in summer, low-temperature cracking in winter); in rigid pavements it produces the warping and frictional stresses.
    • Precipitation / drainage — infiltration raises the subgrade moisture, lowers its CBR and causes pumping and loss of support; frost action in cold regions.
    • Road geometry (gradient, curvature) has a small but real effect through speed and drainage.

    4. Failure criteria — the design must satisfy the distress models used by the method:

    • Fatigue cracking — limits the horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the bituminous layer.
    • Rutting — limits the vertical compressive strain on top of the subgrade (or the rut depth).
    • Thermal cracking; and for rigid pavements, fatigue of the concrete under the combined load + warping stress against the modulus of rupture.

    The four-group classification is exactly the one used in Lecture 14 (§3.2 of these notes) and in Manual.pdf §2.3 — "Loads and other factors controlling pavement design", book pp.116-121.

1. Traffic and Loading Factors

  • Axle load
  • Number of load repetitions
  • Contact area
  • Vehicle speed

Axle load

  • Wheel configuration:
    • Most commercial vehicles are provided with dual rear wheels
  • Affects the stress distribution and deflection
    • Design:

Flexible pavement: Wheels on only one side is considered

Rigid pavement: Wheels on both sides are considered

  • Dual wheel load is converted into equivalent single wheel load to simplify the analysis.
  • Axle configuration:
    • Commercial vehicles are provided with multiple axles to further enhance the load carrying capacity.

Number of Load Repetitions

  • Repeated application of the wheel load → pavement distress → pavement failure
    • Total number of repetitions of the axle loads is computed as Cumulative Number of Standard Axles for the design life.

Contact Area

Vehicle Speed

  • Vehicle speed is directly related to duration of loading.
    • The damage to the pavement is much higher if the vehicle is moving at creep speed.
    • The resilient modulus of the pavement layers should be selected carefully considering the combination of loading and vehicle speed.

2. Material Properties

  • Must be specified to determine pavement responses (stresses, strains and displacements)
    • Linearly elastic: Elastic moduli and Poisson’s ratios
    • For consideration of moving load/repetition of load, resilient modulus is a better representative.
    • Nonlinear property of material can be characterized by a stress dependent resilient modulus.

3. Environment Factor

  • Temperature
  • Precipitation
  • Road geometry (small effect)

Temperature

Both temperature and precipitation affect elastic moduli of pavement layers.

Flexible pavement:

  • Affects resilient modulus of asphalt layer.
    • Low temperature can cause asphalt pavement to crack.

Rigid pavement:

  • Create curling stress and affect slab subgrade contact and the stress values.
    • Determine joint and crack openings and affect the efficiency of load transfer.
  • Frost penetration is another temperature effect that affects the subgrade strength in cold climate.

Precipitation

  • Quantity of surface water infiltration affects the moisture content of the subgrade and the depth of GWT.
    • Poor drainage may bring lack of shear strength, pumping, loss of support etc.

4. Failure Criteria

  • Thermal cracking:
    • Low temperature cracking
    • Thermal fatigue cracking
    • Mix stiffness and fracture strength relationship to temperature, tensile strain due to daily temperature cycle

Rigid pavement:

  • Fatigue cracking:
    • Major failure criteria in rigid pavement design.
    • Due to load and temperature stress.
    • Allowable number of load repetitions depends on ratio of flexural tensile stress and concrete modulus of rupture.
  • Pumping, faulting/spalling/joint deterioration
    • Difficult to analyze mechanistically

Theoretical/Mechanistic Design Method

  • Materials forming the flexible pavement layers and the soil supporting the pavement have non-uniform properties.
  • No completely satisfactory method of design based on theoretical considerations
  • Approaches:
    • Boussinesq’s theory
    • Burmister approach

Boussinesq’s Theory

  • A layer theory first derived by Joseph Valentin Boussinesq in 1885
  • First attempt towards analytical solution.
  • Using the layer theory as a structural model, stresses, strains and deflections at any point in the layered system can be obtained.
  • One layered system

Assumptions The soil mass is:

  • Homogenous
    • Isotropic
    • Ideally elastic (constant elastic parameters E and μ for all the pavement layers and the subgrade soil)
    • Semi-infinite

Drawbacks

  • Soils are not perfectly elastic and homogenous.
    • Modulus of Elasticity E of all pavement layers are not constant.
    • Load may not be uniformly distributed.

Burmister’s Theory

  • By Donald M. Burmister 1945
  • Stresses and strains in two layered system (a pavement layer and a subgrade layer)

Assumptions

  • Materials in the layers are homogeneous, isotropic and elastic.
    • Surface layer is infinite in extent in the lateral direction but of finite depth, whereas the underlying layer is infinite in both the horizontal and vertical direction.
    • Layers are in continuous contact and full friction is developed between the interface (perfectly rough interface)
    • Stress solutions are characterized by two material properties E and μ.
    • Surface layer is free of shearing and normal stresses outside the loaded area.

Semi-Empirical Method

  • Basically, theoretical but includes empirical modifications
  • Approach:
    • Triaxial Method

Triaxial Method

  • Employs empirical modifications
    • Traffic coefficient (X)
    • Saturation coefficient (Y)
  • X is based on the ADT of design traffic
  • Y is based on the annual rainfall

Empirical Method

  • Based on Soil Classification (Group Index Method)
  • Based on Arbitrary Soil Strength Test (CBR Methods: IRC:37-1970, Road Note 29, Road Note 31)
  • Based on Pavement Performance (AASHTO Method)

Mechanistic-Empirical Methods

  • Considers mechanics of materials together with observed performance
  • Pavement configuration, structural model (Stress/strain), distress model/failure criteria
  • Asphalt Institute Method, IRC 37-2001 and onwards, DoR guidelines

    Solution

    (a) Legal axle load and standard axle load

    • Legal axle load — the maximum axle load that the law allows a vehicle to carry on a given highway network (in Nepal/India commonly 10.2 t for a single axle with dual wheels). It is a regulatory limit, enforced at weigh stations.
    • Standard axle load — the reference axle of 80 kN (8.16 t), single axle with dual wheels, to which every other axle is converted through (L/80)4. It is a design yardstick, not a legal limit.

    (b) Stresses induced in a rigid pavement slab

    • Wheel-load stresses at the three critical positions — interior, edge and corner (Westergaard)
    • Warping (temperature) stresses from the temperature differential across the slab
    • Frictional stresses from the subgrade restraining the uniform expansion/contraction of the slab
    Si = 0.316 P/h² [4 log₁₀(l/b) + 1.069] interior load Se = 0.572 P/h² [4 log₁₀(l/b) + 0.359] edge load Sc = 3 P/h² [1 − (a√2/l)0.6] corner load σti = (EαΔt/2)(Cx+μCy)/(1−μ²) interior warping σte = C · EαΔt/2 edge warping σtc = EαΔt √(a/l)/[3(1−μ)] corner warping σf = W · L · f/(2×10⁴) frictional

    (c) Spacing of contraction joints in JPCP

    The joint spacing is fixed so that the frictional (tensile) stress developed in the slab as it contracts does not exceed the allowable tensile stress of the concrete (σc ≈ 0.8 kg/cm² for plain concrete):

    σc = W · L · f/(2×10⁴) ⇒ L = 2×10⁴ · σc/(W · f) L = 2×10⁴ × 0.8 / (2400 × 1.5) = 4.44 m

    with W = unit weight of concrete = 2400 kg/m³ and f = coefficient of subgrade friction = 1.5 — which is why IRC limits the contraction-joint spacing of plain slabs to 4.5 m. (For a reinforced slab the steel carries the friction force and the spacing may be increased to 13-14 m.)

Empirical Methods: CBR Methods

  • Based on California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of subgrade soil and subsequent pavement layers
  • Originally developed in California State Highway Department, has been developed and modified by various organizations for different conditions and wheel loads

CBR Method Recommended by

California State Highway Department

  • Based on extensive CBR test data collected on pavement, provided a design chart with curves correlating the soaked CBR value and the total pavement thickness
  • Includes curves for three classes of traffic with different wheel loads: Light (31.75kN), Medium (40.82kN) and Heavy (54.43kN)
  • Higher the load, greater thickness of the pavement is required

IRC (37-1970)

  • Adopts UK based traffic classification based on the number of heavy vehicles
  • Design chart include correlation between soaked CBR values and pavement thickness for 7 curves for different number of commercial vehicles per day
  • Recommendations:
    • CBR test should be performed in the laboratory following standard test procedure.
    • Subgrade soil sample should be compacted at OMC to Proctor density whenever possible or to dry density achievable in the field.
    • CBR test samples may be soaked in water for four days period before testing.
    • At least three samples should be tested for each type of soil at the same density and moisture content.
  • Design steps:
  1. Evaluate the soak CBR value of the subgrade
  2. Choose appropriate curve considering the design traffic
  3. Determine the pavement thickness for the CBR value of the subgrade
  4. To determine thickness of sub-base, base and surface course, use the curves repeatedly

  • Based on research conducted in countries throughout the world by Transport Research Laboratory UK
  • First published in 1962, revised versions: 1966, 1977, 1993, 2024 (Fifth Edition)
  • Cumulative number of standard axles (Road Note 29), catalogue based on full scale tests and empirical observations made in field.
  • Considers variability in material properties, uncertainty in traffic forecast and effect of climate and high axle loads
  • Overseas Road Note 31 (Fifth Edition) in 2024
    • Caters traffic up to 80 million cumulative ESAL in one direction

  2. Write down the assumptions and analysis of Westergaard's Theory. How are the warping stress and friction stress developed in the rigid pavement? [8] (2076 Ashwin, Q6)
    Solution

    (a) Assumptions of Westergaard's theory

    • The cement-concrete slab is a homogeneous, isotropic, thin elastic plate of uniform thickness.
    • The subgrade behaves as a dense liquid (Winkler foundation): the reaction at any point is vertical and proportional to the deflection at that point — p = k·Δ, where k is the modulus of subgrade reaction.
    • The slab remains in full contact with the subgrade throughout.
    • The wheel load is distributed over a circular area of radius a; the load is static.
    • The slab is of infinite extent in plan for interior loading; the three critical load positions are interior, edge and corner.

    Analysis. The stiffness of the slab relative to the subgrade is expressed by the radius of relative stiffness, and the pressure is assumed to be resisted over an equivalent radius b:

    l = [E h³ / (12 k (1 − μ²))]0.25 b = a for a ≥ 1.724 h b = √(1.6a² + h²) − 0.675h for a < 1.724 h
    PositionCritical load stress
    InteriorSi = 0.316 P/h² · [4 log10(l/b) + 1.069]
    EdgeSe = 0.572 P/h² · [4 log10(l/b) + 0.359]
    CornerSc = 3 P/h² · [1 − (a√2/l)0.6]

    IRC uses the versions later modified by Teller & Sutherland (edge) and Kelley (corner).

    (b) How the warping stress is developed

    During the day the top of the slab is heated by the sun while the bottom stays cool, so a temperature differential Δt exists across the depth. The warmer face tries to expand more than the cooler face and the slab tends to warp (curl) — downwards at mid-day and upwards at night. This warping is restrained by the self-weight of the slab and by the subgrade friction, and the restraint produces warping stresses:

    interior : σ = (EαΔt/2) · (Cx + μCy)/(1 − μ²) edge : σ = C · EαΔt / 2 corner : σ = EαΔt √(a/l) / [3(1 − μ)]

    where C, Cx, Cy are Bradbury's coefficients, read from the chart against L/l for the slab length and width. At mid-day the slab warps downwards, so the warping tension at the bottom adds to the load tension — this is the critical combination at the edge.

    (c) How the frictional stress is developed

    A uniform rise or fall of the mean slab temperature makes the whole slab expand or contract. The subgrade resists this movement by friction at the interface, so the slab is put into compression when it expands (summer) and into tension when it contracts (winter). Taking the friction force on half the slab length:

    σf = W · L · f / (2 × 10⁴) kg/cm² W = 2400 kg/m³, L = slab length between joints (m), f ≈ 1.5

    Because σf grows with L, it is this stress that fixes the spacing of the contraction joints (L = 2×104σc/Wf ≈ 4.5 m for plain slabs).

    (b) Westergaard's concept of temperature stresses

    Westergaard showed that temperature produces two distinct stresses in a concrete pavement:

    1. Warping (curling) stress — caused by the differential temperature Δt between the top and the bottom of the slab. The hotter face wants to expand more, so the slab tries to curl; its own weight and the subgrade prevent this and bending stresses appear.     interior : σti = (EαΔt/2)(Cx + μCy)/(1 − μ²) edge : σte = C · EαΔt / 2 corner : σtc = EαΔt √(a/l) / [3(1 − μ)]

    During the day the slab warps down (tension at the bottom, adds to the load stress at the edge); at night it warps up (tension at the top at the corner).

    2. Frictional stress — caused by the uniform change of the mean slab temperature. The subgrade resists the free expansion/contraction of the slab:

    σf = W · L · f / (2×10⁴)

    compressive in summer, tensile in winter, and it is the quantity that fixes the contraction-joint spacing.

    Critical combinations (used for design):

    • Summer mid-day, edge: load + warping − friction
    • Winter mid-day, edge: load + warping + friction
    • Mid-night, corner: load + warping (there is no frictional stress at the corner)

  2. The load at the edge of the slab is distributed uniformly over semi-circular area of contact.

  4. What are the factors affecting rigid pavement design? Write down the steps of IRC design guidelines for rigid pavement. (2074 Ashwin, Q5)
    Solution

    (a) Factors affecting rigid-pavement design

    • Wheel load and its repetitions — design wheel load (5100 kg for IRC charts), contact radius, and the number of commercial vehicles projected to the end of the design life Ad = P(1+r)n+y, which fixes the traffic group and the thickness adjustment.
    • Subgrade / sub-base support — modulus of subgrade reaction k (from a plate-bearing test, corrected for the sub-base); k enters the radius of relative stiffness l = [Eh³/(12k(1−μ²))]0.25.
    • Concrete properties — flexural strength (modulus of rupture, 40-45 kg/cm²), E, μ and the coefficient of thermal expansion α.
    • Temperature differential Δt across the slab (region- and thickness-dependent) which produces the warping stresses; and the seasonal temperature range which, with the coefficient of subgrade friction f, produces the frictional stresses.
    • Slab geometry / joint spacing — Lx, Ly control Bradbury's coefficients and hence the warping stress; the carriageway width decides whether a longitudinal joint is needed.
    • Drainage and shoulder type, and whether a tied concrete shoulder is provided (reduces the edge stress).

    (b) Steps of the IRC design guideline for rigid pavement

    1. Collect design data — P, a, k, E, μ, α, flexural strength, Δt, present traffic, growth rate, design life and construction period.
    2. Compute the design traffic Ad = P(1 + r)(n+y) and identify the traffic group (A … G).
    3. Fix the joint spacings — expansion joint Le = (δ/2)/(αΔT) (≤ 140 m rough / 90 m smooth sub-base) and contraction joint Lc = 2×104σc/(Wf) (≤ 4.5 m plain slab).
    4. Assume a trial thickness h and compute l and b.
    5. Warping stresses — read Bradbury's C from L/l and compute σte = C·EαΔt/2 (edge) and σtc = EαΔt√(a/l)/[3(1−μ)] (corner).
    6. Residual strength = flexural strength − warping stress.
    7. Load stresses — read the edge and corner load stress from the IRC stress charts for h and k (or compute from the Teller & Sutherland / Kelley equations).
    8. Check factor of safety = residual strength / load stress at the edge; if it is much greater or less than 1, revise h and repeat from step 4. Also verify (load + warping) at the corner ≤ flexural strength.
    9. Adjust for traffic intensity — add the thickness increment prescribed for the traffic group (e.g. +2 cm for group G).
    10. Design the joint furniture — dowel bars at the expansion joints (Bradbury's method, 40% load transfer, effective group 1.8 l), tie bars at the longitudinal joint (As = bfhW/Ss, length = dSs/2τ) and, for JRCP, the temperature reinforcement As = LWf/(2Ss).

    Manual.pdf §2.8 — Details of the IRC method of design of rigid pavements for highways, book pp.164-173.

    • The sum of CFDs for the BUC and TDC should be less than 1 to be safe in fatigue cracking.

Design Traffic (BUC)

  • Commercial vehicles with tyre imprint of the outer wheel touching the longitudinal edge during six hours in day (10am to 4pm).
  • For bottom-up cracking, single axle load causes the largest edge stress followed by tandem and tridem axles. So tridem axles are not considered for analysis.
  • It is recommended that 25% of the total two-way commercial traffic may be considered as design traffic for two-lane two-way roads for the analysis of bottom-up cracking.
  • For four lane and other multi-lane divided highways, 25% of the total traffic in the direction of predominant traffic may be considered for design of pavement for bottom-up cracking.

Design Traffic (TDC)

  • Six hour night time (0am to 6am) commercial vehicles with the spacing between the front axle and the first rear axle (single/tandem/tridem) less than the spacing of transverse joints should be considered for top-down cracking analysis.
  • A default value of 50% of the design traffic used for bottom-up cracking analysis may be considered for the analysis of top-down cracking when data availability is limited.

Calculation Fatigue Damage (CFD) Analysis

  • For each axle load group, flexural stress is divided by the design flexural strength or modulus of rupture of the cement concrete to obtain stress ratio (SR).
  • If SR is less than 0.45, allowable number of cycles of axle load (N) is infinity.
  • Otherwise:
  • The cumulative fatigue damage caused to the slab during its service life should be equal to or less than one.
