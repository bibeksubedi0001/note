Question 1

1 (a) TheoryExplain Richter scale and MMI scale. [6]

Richter scale — a MAGNITUDE scale

  • Devised by Charles F. Richter in 1935. It is a quantitative, instrumental measure of the size of an earthquake — of the total energy released at the source.
  • Based on the logarithm (base 10) of the maximum trace amplitude recorded by a standard Wood–Anderson seismograph, corrected to a standard epicentral distance of 100 km: ML = log10A − log10A0.
  • Because it is logarithmic, one unit of magnitude = 10× the wave amplitude and about 31.6 (≈32)× the energy. M7.7 releases ~31× the energy of M6.7 and ~1000× that of M5.7.
  • Energy relation: log10E = 11.8 + 1.5 M (E in ergs).
  • One value per earthquake, independent of the observer's position and of the local soil or buildings. Expressed in Arabic numerals with a decimal.
  • Open-ended and can be negative, but it saturates above about M7 — which is why the non-saturating moment magnitude Mw is now used for large events.
  • Classification: <3 micro/minor, 3–3.9 minor, 4–4.9 light, 5–5.9 moderate, 6–6.9 strong, 7–7.9 major, ≥8 great.

MMI scale — an INTENSITY scale

  • The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale is a qualitative, observational measure of the effects of an earthquake at a particular place — on people, objects, buildings and the ground.
  • It has 12 grades written in Roman numerals, I to XII. No instrument is used; the grade is assigned from damage surveys and human reports.
  • Many values per earthquake — a different value at every location. It is highest near the epicentre and decreases outward.
  • Lines joining points of equal intensity are isoseismals; their map is used to locate the epicentre of historic (pre-instrumental) earthquakes and to prepare seismic hazard maps.
  • It depends on epicentral distance, the local soil, and the type and quality of the buildings.
  • Key grades: VII considerable damage in poorly built masonry; VIII partial collapse of ordinary masonry; X most masonry destroyed with its foundations; XII total damage.
  • Other intensity scales: MSK-64 (also 12 grades, used in Europe and India) and the JMA scale (Japan).

Close with the one-line contrast: the Richter scale measures how big the earthquake was; the MMI scale measures how badly it shook a particular place.

1 (b) TheoryDescribe in brief with sketch major failure mechanism of masonry building during Gorkha earthquake. [6]

The 2015 Gorkha earthquake (Mw 7.8, 25 April 2015, epicentre Barpak, focal depth ~15 km, followed by an Mw 7.3 aftershock on 12 May) damaged or destroyed about 800,000 buildings, the great majority of them unreinforced masonry. The major failure mechanisms observed were:

  1. Out-of-plane collapse of walls — by far the dominant mechanism, caused by unanchored flexible timber floors and roofs, the absence of bands, and slender walls in the upper storeys.
  2. Corner separation and vertical cracking at wall junctions, from poor interlocking of the courses at the corners.
  3. Delamination and collapse of multi-wythe walls — both burnt-brick-in-cement and un-coursed random rubble walls with CGI roofs, because through-stones were missing.
  4. In-plane diagonal (X) shear cracking of piers between openings, and vertical cracking at the corners of openings.
  5. Failure of gable walls, pulling down the top of the end walls and the roof.
  6. Separation of the roof from the walls, followed by collapse of heavy tiled roofs on the occupants.
  7. Soft / open ground storey failure in shop-front buildings, and short-column failures created by mezzanines.
  8. Total collapse of unreinforced stone-in-mud houses in the rural districts around the epicentre.
  9. Widespread failure of temples, monuments and monasteries, several already weakened by earlier earthquakes; plus lifeline failures (e.g. collapse of a penstock in Sindhupalchowk).
The single most quotable field observation: buildings with even one RC lintel band frequently survived with repairable damage while identical unbanded neighbours collapsed — the clearest available evidence for the value of banding.

Sketch to draw

One isometric of a simple masonry building marked with: separation of walls at corners, diagonal (X) cracking in the in-plane wall, out-of-plane overturning of the transverse wall, separation of the roof from the walls, vertical cracking at openings and gable collapse.

2 (a) TheoryDefine masonry structure type based on materials with its advantages and disadvantages. [6]
TypeAdvantagesDisadvantages
Brick masonry
(burnt clay units)		 Uniform shape and size → good bond and thin joints; good strength (3.5–35 N/mm²); easy to form openings and bands; durable, fire and weather resistant; locally manufactured. Needs good clay, fuel and kilns (energy and pollution); quality varies between kilns; heavy; weak in tension.
Stone masonry
(random rubble, coursed rubble, squared, ashlar)		 Very high unit strength; extremely durable; locally available free of cost in hill areas; attractive; good thermal mass. Irregular shapes give poor bond and very thick joints; walls are multi-leaf and delaminate; very heavy; needs skilled masons; the worst-performing type in Nepali earthquakes.

Adobe / earthen masonry Cheapest; zero-energy production; excellent thermal comfort; entirely local material and skills. Very low strength, no tensile capacity, degrades on wetting, erodes; extremely vulnerable in earthquakes (NBC 204).
Composite / faced masonry Combines an economical backing (rubble, block) with a good-quality facing (brick, ashlar). The two leaves have different stiffness and separate unless properly bonded through with headers.

Add the general advantages of masonry (high compressive strength, durability, low-cost local materials, no complicated plant, fire and thermal performance, design flexibility, life > 500 years in lime mortar) and its general disadvantages (negligible tensile strength, heavy so large foundations and large inertia forces, brittle, frost and sulphate spalling, efflorescence, slow and weather-dependent construction).

2 (b) TheoryDescribe affecting factors and relation between unit and mortar on strength of brick masonry. [6]

1. Relation between unit and mortar — the mechanism

Under vertical compression the mortar is softer and has a higher Poisson's ratio than the unit, so it tries to expand sideways more. Friction and bond at the interface prevent it. The result is that the mortar goes into triaxial compression (which strengthens it) while the unit is put into lateral tension combined with vertical compression. Because units are weak in tension, failure begins as vertical splitting cracks through the UNITS, not by crushing of the mortar.

Consequences:

  • Masonry strength is only about 25–50% of the unit strength.
  • Increasing the mortar strength gives a less than proportionate gain — doubling the mortar strength may raise the masonry strength by only 15–25%.
  • A thinner bed joint gives a stronger wall.
  • The mortar should be weaker than the unit — a mortar stronger than necessary is wasteful, cracks the units rather than the joints, and gives brittle unrepairable failure.
  • Empirically fk = K fb0.7 fm0.3 — unit strength matters more than twice as much as mortar strength.

Sketch: a masonry prism under compression with arrows showing the mortar squeezing outwards, the unit in lateral tension, and vertical splitting cracks through the bricks.

If the loads were shared between both leaves (i.e. the slab bears on both), the area would be 230 000 mm², ka = 1.0, fc would be about 0.229 N/mm² and a much weaker combination would do. 5 NumericalDesign an exterior wall of a single storey workshop of height 4 m. The load on the wall is 30 kN/m from the roof and the wind load is 750 N/m². The wall is connected by metal anchor with the roof structure. [14]
Given: single-storey workshop; H = 4.0 m; vertical load from roof = 30 kN/m; wind pressure p = 750 N/m²; wall tied to the roof with metal anchors.
Assume: t = 230 mm; masonry 20 kN/m³. Design a 1 m run of wall.
Vertical load
Load from roof = 30.00 kN/m Self weight of wall = 0.23 × 20 × 4.0 = 18.40 kN/m ------------ Total at the base, W = 48.40 kN/m
Section properties (per metre run)
A = 1000 × 230 = 230 000 mm² Z = b t² / 6 = 1000 × 230² / 6 = 8.817 × 10⁶ mm³
Bending moment due to wind

The wall is anchored at the floor and the roof, so it is treated as partially constrained at both ends; the moment is critical at the base and at mid-height:

M = p H² / 10 = 750 × 4.0² / 10 = 1200 N·m/m = 1.20 × 10⁶ N·mm/m Flexural stress f_cb = M / Z = 1.20 × 10⁶ / 8.817 × 10⁶ = 0.136 N/mm²
Compressive stress at the BASE
f_ca = W / A = 48 400 / 230 000 = 0.210 N/mm² f_c = f_ca + f_cb = 0.210 + 0.136 = 0.346 N/mm²
Tensile stress at MID-HEIGHT (the critical section for tension)
Load present at mid-height W_mid = 30.00 + 0.23 × 20 × 2.0 = 39.20 kN/m f_ca = 39 200 / 230 000 = 0.170 N/mm² f_t = f_cb − f_ca = 0.136 − 0.170 = −0.034 N/mm² Negative → the section is still wholly in COMPRESSION, NO TENSION develops. ✓
Slenderness ratio and eccentricity
Metal anchors → lateral support with PARTIAL restraint h_eff = H = 4000 mm t_eff = 230 mm SR = 4000 / 230 = 17.39 < 27 ✓ Equivalent eccentricity e = M / W = 1.20 × 10⁶ / 48 400 = 24.8 mm e / t = 24.8 / 230 = 0.108 (between 1/12 and 1/6)
Stress reduction factor  IS 1905 Table 9
At SR = 17.39 : e/t = 1/12 : SR 16 → 0.68 , SR 18 → 0.61 k = 0.68 − 0.07 × (1.39/2) = 0.631 e/t = 1/6 : SR 16 → 0.63 , SR 18 → 0.55 k = 0.63 − 0.08 × (1.39/2) = 0.574 Interpolate on e/t for 0.108 : fraction = (0.108 − 0.0833)/(0.1667 − 0.0833) = 0.296 k_s = 0.631 − 0.296 × (0.631 − 0.574) = 0.614
Permissible stress
k_a A = 1.0 × 0.23 = 0.23 m² > 0.2 m² → k_a = 1.0 k_p h/w = 0.50 < 0.75 → k_p = 1.0 TRIAL 1 : unit 5 N/mm² + M1 mortar , f_b = 0.50 f_per = 0.50 × 0.614 = 0.307 N/mm² with the 25% increase for bending f_per,bend = 1.25 × 0.307 = 0.384 N/mm² f_c = 0.346 < 0.384 ✓ but only 90% utilised TRIAL 2 : unit 7.5 N/mm² + M1 mortar , f_b = 0.74 f_per = 0.74 × 0.614 = 0.454 N/mm² f_per,bend = 1.25 × 0.454 = 0.568 N/mm² f_c = 0.346 < 0.568 ✓ utilisation 61 % ADOPT

Summary of checks
Compression at base : 0.346 < 0.568 ✓ Tension at mid-height: none develops ✓ Slenderness : 17.39 < 27 ✓
SAFE. Adopt a 230 mm (one brick) thick exterior wall, 4 m high, built with bricks of crushing strength 7.5 N/mm² in M1 (1:5) cement mortar, anchored to the roof structure with metal anchors at not more than 1 m centres.
