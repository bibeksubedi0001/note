Question 1

1 (a) TheoryDefine elastic rebound theory. Explain the origin of earthquakes in Himalayas. [8]

1. Elastic rebound theory

Proposed by H. F. Reid after studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Rocks on either side of a fault are slowly deformed (bent) by tectonic forces and store elastic strain energy. When the accumulated strain exceeds the strength of the rock (or the frictional resistance of the fault), the rock suddenly ruptures and the two blocks “snap back” — rebound — to a new, unstrained position. The stored elastic energy is released in a few seconds as heat, fracturing and seismic waves; those waves are the earthquake.

Draw the four stages: (a) original unstrained state, with a fence or road crossing the fault straight; (b) strain build-up over decades — the fence bends; (c) rupture when the strength is exceeded — the fence snaps; (d) rebound — the two halves are straight again but permanently offset. The cycle then restarts.

2. Origin of earthquakes in the Himalayas

The Himalaya is the classic continent–continent convergent margin:

  1. About 225 million years ago the Indian plate separated from Gondwanaland and drifted northwards at roughly 10 cm/yr, the intervening Tethys oceanic crust subducting beneath Eurasia.
  2. Around 55 million years ago the Tethys closed and India collided with Asia. Because both are continental crust of the same low density, neither could subduct beneath the other.
  3. The collision pressure could only be relieved by crustal thickening and thrusting upward, raising the Himalayan range. This is why the Himalaya has great earthquakes but no volcanoes.
  4. Convergence continues today; about 2 cm/yr of the ~4–5 cm/yr India–Eurasia convergence is absorbed across the Himalaya, so Nepal shortens by roughly 2 m per century.
  5. The shortening is taken up on a system of north-dipping thrusts that all sole into one gently north-dipping detachment, the Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT): MFT (Main Frontal Thrust, southernmost and active), MBT (Main Boundary Thrust), MCT (Main Central Thrust) and STDS (South Tibetan Detachment System, a normal fault to the north).
  6. The shallow part of the MHT is locked by friction between earthquakes. Strain accumulates for decades to centuries and is then released suddenly as a great earthquake — exactly the elastic rebound mechanism. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake (Mw 7.8) was a rupture of the locked MHT that propagated eastward from Barpak, Gorkha, at a focal depth of about 15 km.
  7. Seismic gap: no great earthquake has ruptured the western Nepal segment of the MHT since 1505, so more than five centuries of strain is stored there. The 2015 event did not release it.

Draw a simplified north–south section: Indian plate underthrusting, the Himalayan wedge, the MHT with the MFT / MBT / MCT splays branching off it, and the typical focus at 10–20 km depth.

1 (b) TheoryExplain the magnitude and intensity of the earthquake. Compare the MMI scale with the Richter scale. [8]

1. Magnitude

Magnitude is a quantitative, instrumental measure of the size of an earthquake — of the total energy released at the source. It is a single number for a given earthquake, independent of where it is measured.

  • Richter scale (local magnitude ML), 1935: based on the logarithm (base 10) of the maximum trace amplitude recorded by a standard Wood–Anderson seismograph, corrected to a standard epicentral distance of 100 km.
  • An increase of one unit of magnitude = 10 times larger wave amplitude and about 31.6 (≈32) times more energy. So M7.7 releases ~31 times the energy of M6.7 and ~1000 times that of M5.7.
  • Open-ended, can be negative, but it saturates above about M7.
  • Moment magnitude Mw is derived from the seismic moment M0 = µAD; it does not saturate and is now used for all large earthquakes (Gorkha 2015 = Mw 7.8).
ML = log10 A − log10 A0      Energy–magnitude (Gutenberg–Richter):   log10 E = 11.8 + 1.5 M  (E in ergs)

2. Intensity

Intensity is a qualitative, observational measure of the actual effects of an earthquake at a particular place — on people, objects, buildings and the ground. Because the effects change from place to place, one earthquake has many intensity values but only one magnitude. It is highest near the epicentre and decreases outward; lines joining points of equal intensity are isoseismals, used to locate the epicentre of historic (pre-instrumental) earthquakes and to prepare seismic hazard maps. The scale used is the Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale with 12 grades, I to XII (others: MSK-64, JMA).

Question 2

2 (a) TheoryExplain load-bearing masonry walls, infill masonry, and confined masonry. [8]

These are the three structural systems in which masonry is used. The distinguishing features are the load path and whether the wall is a structural element.

(A) Load-bearing masonry

  • The masonry walls themselves are the main supporting elements — there is no separate frame.
  • Load path: roof / floor slab → main (load-bearing) walls → plinth → foundation → soil. Both gravity and lateral loads travel through the walls.
  • Walls parallel to the shaking act as shear walls (in-plane); walls perpendicular are loaded out-of-plane.
  • Economical for 1–3 storeys; thickness increases rapidly with height. Unreinforced load-bearing masonry is brittle, so earthquake resistance must be supplied by bands, vertical bars, corner bonding and box action.

(B) Confined masonry

Load-bearing masonry wall panels confined on all four sides by lightly reinforced concrete tie-columns (vertical ties) and tie-beams (horizontal ties).

The defining construction sequence: the masonry wall is built FIRST, with toothed vertical edges, and the tie-columns are then cast against the completed wall.
  • Tie-columns (“practical columns”) are of much smaller cross-section than RC frame columns. They are not a moment frame — the masonry, not the ties, carries the load.
  • The confining members are effective in: (1) enhancing the stability and integrity of the wall for in-plane and out-of-plane earthquake loads — they contain the damaged masonry; (2) enhancing the lateral strength; and (3) reducing the brittleness, thereby improving earthquake performance.
  • Components: masonry walls, confining elements, floor/roof slabs acting as diaphragms, plinth band and foundation.
  • It has emerged over the last 100 years as an economical alternative between unreinforced masonry and RC frame construction.

(C) Infill masonry

  • Masonry panels enclosed by RC or steel frame members on all four sides. The frame, not the wall, is the designed structure.
  • Load path: slab → beam → column → foundation. The infill is nominally non-structural.
  • In reality it greatly increases the lateral stiffness of the frame, and under lateral load acts as a diagonal compression strut, so the infilled frame can be analysed as a truss.
  • Dangers: a sudden loss of stiffness when the infill cracks can create a soft-storey mechanism; irregular distribution in elevation (the open ground storey) concentrates drift in one storey; partial-height infill produces the short-column effect.
  • Conversely, continuous well-distributed infill has been shown to reduce the vulnerability of an RC structure.

The key sentence: because the earthquake shakes the building in both horizontal directions and reverses continually, every wall is an in-plane wall at one instant and an out-of-plane wall the next. A wall is only safe if it is adequate in both roles — and it is the out-of-plane role that governs survival.

Question 3

3 (a) TheoryDefine repair, restoration, and retrofitting. [4]
TermDefinitionAim & typical work
Repair Cosmetic and functional works carried out on a damaged building to bring it back to a usable condition, without altering its structural strength. Aim: restore serviceability and appearance only. Work: patching and re-plastering, filling fine cracks, repairing doors, windows, wiring, plumbing, whitewashing, rebuilding non-structural partitions, re-fixing roof tiles. The strength is NOT restored.
Restoration
(rehabilitation)		 Structural works that bring the damaged building back to its original strength — i.e. to the condition it was in before the earthquake. Aim: recover the lost strength and stiffness. Work: grouting and injection of cracks, stitching of wide cracks with dowels, re-pointing eroded joints, dismantling and rebuilding shattered portions, replacing damaged lintels. No more than the original capacity is provided.
Retrofitting
(seismic strengthening)		 Structural works that upgrade the building beyond its original strength, so that it can resist a future earthquake better than it resisted the last one. Aim: increase strength, stiffness, ductility and integrity to meet current code requirements. Work: adding horizontal bands and vertical bars, splint-and-bandage, wire-mesh / ferrocement or FRP jacketing, through-stones, corner stitching, stiffening the diaphragm, reducing openings, lightening the roof, adding buttresses or shear walls. Applied to undamaged buildings too.
The one-line distinction to write: Repair → looks better; Restoration → as strong as it was; Retrofitting → stronger than it ever was.

Question 4

4 (a) TheoryWhat are the steps involved in retrofitting strategies? [4]
  1. Data collection and condition survey. Collect the as-built drawings; survey the building from outside and inside; record the structural system, materials, wall thicknesses, openings, roof type and the site conditions; note existing cracks and past interventions.
  2. Seismic vulnerability assessment. Rapid visual screening first (e.g. FEMA 154 / NBC type checklists), then a detailed evaluation — material testing (NDT), damage grade classification (EMS G1–G5), and analysis of the existing capacity.
  3. Identify the deficiencies. List exactly what is wrong: no bands, no through-stones, large or badly placed openings, weak mortar, heavy roof, soft storey, torsional irregularity, poor corner bonding, inadequate diaphragm.
  4. Define the performance objective. Life safety, immediate occupancy or collapse prevention, and the corresponding target earthquake level.
  5. Select the retrofitting strategy. Choose between increasing strength/stiffness (jacketing, new shear walls, bands), increasing ductility and integrity (vertical bars, corner stitching, confinement), reducing demand (lightening the roof, removing storeys, base isolation), or a combination.
  6. Analyse and design the intervention, checking that the retrofitted structure meets the performance objective and that the new elements are compatible with the old.
  7. Cost–benefit and feasibility check against the option of demolition and reconstruction; consider heritage value, disruption to occupants and available skills.
  8. Prepare drawings, specifications and a construction sequence (shoring first, then integrity, then strength, then demand reduction).
  9. Execute with strict quality control and supervision, followed by inspection and monitoring.
See Chapter 3 §3.7.6; full treatment is Chapter 6.

Question 5

5 (a) TheoryWhat is reinforced masonry? Explain. [4]
Reinforced masonry is masonry designed on the assumption that the flexural tensile stresses cannot be resisted by the masonry and are resisted by reinforcement only. Shear stresses may be resisted by the masonry or by the reinforcement, singly or in combination. Design may be by allowable stress design or by strength design.
  • Why: brickwork and blockwork have high compressive strength but very low tensile resistance, so steel is introduced to carry the tension — exactly as in reinforced concrete.
  • Where the steel goes: vertical bars grouted into the voids created by particular bonds or into the hollow cores of blocks, and horizontal bed-joint reinforcement (ladder or truss mesh) laid in the mortar. Bed-joint reinforcement enhances the capacity of walls to resist lateral loading.
  • Contrast with URM: unreinforced masonry is designed assuming the flexural tensile stress is resisted by the masonry, and any reinforcement present is neglected; the design is in the linear elastic range. So “unreinforced masonry” may actually contain steel — the term describes the design assumption, not the absence of bars.
  • Benefits: real flexural and shear capacity, genuine ductility and energy dissipation, much better out-of-plane performance, taller walls and larger openings possible.
  • Applications: shear walls, retaining walls, tanks, footbridges, reinforced masonry columns, beams and bands. Prestressed (post-tensioned) masonry adds precompression through vertical tendons.
  • History: used in India and Japan since after the First World War, in America since after the Second.
See Chapter 2 §2.4.2(D); full treatment is Chapter 5.
