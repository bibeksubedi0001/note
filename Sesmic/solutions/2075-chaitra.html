Question 1

1 TheoryDiscuss the design criteria and the design steps involved in the design of masonry shear walls. [10]

Part A — Design criteria

A masonry shear wall is the element that carries the in-plane horizontal storey shear down to the foundation. It must satisfy four criteria:

  1. Shear. The developed shear stress τ = V/A must not exceed the permissible shear stress, which for masonry increases with the precompression: τper = 0.1 + fd/6  ≤ 0.5 N/mm² where fd is the compressive stress due to the dead load only. Note that gravity load helps — a heavily loaded wall has more shear capacity.
  2. Compression. The maximum combined stress fc = fca + fcb must not exceed the permissible compressive stress. For combined axial and bending stress the permissible value may be increased by 25%, i.e. facb = 1.25 ks fb. The axial stress alone must also satisfy fca ≤ fac = ks fb.
  3. Tension. The flexural tensile stress ft = fcb − fca must be within the permissible tensile stress (0.07 N/mm² vertical, 0.14 N/mm² horizontal, for M1 or richer cement mortar). Ideally the design should show that no tension develops at all, i.e. that the precompression exceeds the bending stress.
  4. Stability — overturning and sliding. The restoring moment from the gravity load must exceed the overturning moment with a factor of safety of at least 1.5, and the base friction must exceed the base shear.

To these are added the general design principles of masonry buildings: a symmetric and regular plan; shear walls in both orthogonal directions; walls continuous from foundation to roof; the centre of rigidity as close as possible to the centre of mass to limit torsion; openings restricted in size and position; and a rigid diaphragm to distribute the shear.

Part B — Design steps

StepOperationKey relations
1Fix the trial geometry and section properties A = L t  ·  Z = t L²/6  (bending is in the plane, so L is the depth)
2Compute the vertical (gravity) load W on the wall self weight + tributary floor / roof load
3Compute the lateral force F on the wall F = AhW for a single wall, or the share of the storey shear Vi = V · Ri/ΣR plus the torsional share
4Check the shear stress τ = F/A  ≤  τper = 0.1 + fd/6
5Compute the in-plane bending moment M = F·H/2 for a load distributed over the height; M = F·H for a load concentrated at the roof
6Compute the axial and flexural stresses fca = W/A  ·  fcb = M/Z  ·  fc = fca + fcb  ·  ft = fcb − fca
7Compute the slenderness ratio and the equivalent eccentricity SR = heff/teff  ·  e = M/W  ·  e/L
8Read the stress reduction factor ks from IS 1905 Table 9, interpolating on both SR and e/t
9Select the unit strength and mortar grade fb from IS 1905 Table 8; apply ka and kp
10Final checks — if any fails, increase t, increase L, or use a stronger unit/mortar and repeat fca ≤ ksfb  ·  fc ≤ 1.25 ksfb  ·  τ ≤ τper  ·  ft ≤ ft,per
Remember the reversal. Earthquake load reverses direction, so both ends of the wall must be checked for maximum compression and for tension; the design must be symmetric.

Question 2

2 TheoryExplain the different modes of failure in brick masonry structures with sketches for both static load and earthquake load. [10]
The question has two halves. Give the static (gravity / service) failure modes first, then the earthquake failure modes. Most candidates write only the seismic modes and lose half the marks.

A. Failure modes under STATIC (gravity and service) load

ModeCauseAppearance / sketch
Vertical splitting (the classic compression failure) The mortar expands laterally more than the unit; the unit is put into lateral tension and splits. Continuous vertical cracks running through the bricks, roughly parallel to the load. This is the mode on which the basic compressive stress fb is based.
Local crushing / bearing failure A concentrated load (a beam or joist end) applied over a small area. Crushed and spalled masonry directly under the bearing, with cracks radiating downward.
Buckling of a slender wall High slenderness ratio; secondary moments amplify the deflection. The wall bows out of plane and fails by combined compression and bending — controlled by ks and the SR limits.
Flexural tension failure from eccentricity e > t/6, so the resultant falls outside the middle third. Horizontal crack opening on the tension face; only part of the section remains effective, fc = 2P/[3L(t/2 − e)].
Differential settlement cracking Non-uniform bearing capacity, soft spot, adjacent excavation. Diagonal cracks widening upward away from the settled portion; sloping stepped cracks over openings.
Shrinkage and thermal cracking Drying shrinkage of the mortar or blocks; thermal movement of a slab over a wall. Vertical cracks at regular spacing; horizontal crack under the roof slab at the top of the wall.
Sliding at the DPC A smooth membrane forms a slip plane with very low friction. Horizontal offset at plinth level. No tension whatever may be assumed across a DPC.
Arch / vault thrust failure The horizontal thrust of an arch or vault pushes the supporting wall outwards. The wall leans outward with a hinge crack; the arch drops.
Durability failure Frost, sulphate attack, efflorescence, dampness. Spalling of the brick face, powdering mortar, white salt deposits.

B. Failure modes under EARTHQUAKE load

B1 — In-plane modes (the wall acting as a shear wall):

ModeWhen it governsCrack pattern
Rocking (flexural) Slender pier (high h/L), low vertical load Horizontal crack at the heel; the pier rotates about the compressed toe; crushing at the toe. Ductile — the best mode to have.
Sliding shear Very low vertical load, weak mortar, low friction A single horizontal crack along a bed joint with a visible offset.
Diagonal shear — bed-joint sliding Mortar weaker than the unit Stair-stepped diagonal crack following the joints; the bricks are undamaged.
Diagonal tension + toe crushing Mortar stronger than the unit, high precompression, squat pier Straight diagonal crack cutting through the bricks; under reversal the two directions form the characteristic “X” crack. Brittle — the dangerous mode.

B2 — Out-of-plane modes (the wall loaded normal to its plane):

  • Simple overturning — the wall rotates about its base as a rigid body and falls outward. Occurs when there is no connection to the return walls or the diaphragm.
  • Composed overturning — the falling wall drags a wedge of the return walls with it, so the failure surface is diagonal on the side walls.
  • Vertical flexure — the wall is held at the top and bottom but spans too far; a horizontal hinge crack forms at mid-height and the wall folds outward.
  • Horizontal flexure — the wall spans horizontally between cross walls; a vertical crack forms at mid-span and the two halves rotate outward.

B3 — System / connection modes: corner separation; delamination of multi-leaf walls; gable and parapet collapse; roof or floor separation; soft-storey and short-column failure; pounding; torsional failure; and liquefaction or settlement of the foundation.

Sketches to draw

Draw two rows of small labelled sketches:

  • Static row: a prism under compression with vertical splitting cracks through the bricks; a beam bearing on a wall with local crushing; a slender wall bowing; a wall with settlement cracks widening upward.
  • Seismic row: four piers showing rocking, sliding, stair-stepped diagonal and X-shaped diagonal cracking; then a building isometric with the out-of-plane wall overturning, corner separation and the roof pulling away.

Influence on the strength of the wall — this is what the question asks for:

  1. The basic compressive stress fb of Table 8 is measured on a short prism. A tall, slender wall buckles: it deflects laterally, the load becomes eccentric with respect to the deflected shape, and secondary (P–Δ) moments develop that add to the stress. The wall therefore fails at a load far below the short-prism strength.
  2. IS 1905 accounts for this by the stress reduction factor ks of Table 9, which is a function of SR and the eccentricity ratio: fper = fb ks ka kp.
  3. The strength falls steeply as SR rises. With e = 0: SR 6 → ks = 1.00; SR 12 → 0.84; SR 18 → 0.67; SR 24 → 0.51; SR 27 → 0.43. Roughly, doubling the slenderness from 12 to 24 halves the permissible stress. With eccentricity present the fall is even steeper — at SR 27 and e/t = 1/6, ks is only 0.22, i.e. barely a fifth of the short-wall value.
  4. SR interacts with eccentricity: a slender wall is far more sensitive to an eccentric load than a stocky one, because the initial eccentricity is amplified by the lateral deflection.
  5. Out of plane, the h/t ratio is the single most important parameter governing whether a wall survives an earthquake — hence the code puts an absolute ceiling on SR (Table 7): 27 for a load-bearing wall in cement mortar, 20 or 13 in lime mortar, 30 for a panel or curtain wall, 12 for a column, and only 10 for a free-standing wall or parapet.
  6. Design consequence: the way to reduce SR is to increase the thickness, or to reduce the effective height or length by adding cross walls, piers, buttresses or bands. Adding piers is usually the most economical, but only if their spacing satisfies Sp/Wp < 20.

Influence on the strength of the wall — and why:

  1. The physical reason. Masonry fails because the softer mortar squeezes out laterally and puts the unit into lateral tension. A taller unit means fewer bed joints per metre of wall height, so there is less mortar to squeeze and the lateral tension induced in the unit is smaller. The wall is therefore stronger for the same unit and mortar.
  2. Also, a taller unit has a larger height-to-joint ratio, so the proportion of the wall volume that is weak mortar falls, and the restraint the unit provides to the mortar improves.
  3. Magnitude of the effect. It can be very large — for a low-strength unit (5 N/mm²) laid with h/w = 2 to 4, the permissible stress is increased by 80% (kp = 1.8). This is why hollow concrete blocks and stone blocks are far more efficient than thin bricks.
  4. The bonus shrinks as the unit gets stronger. At 15 N/mm² the same shape gives only kp = 1.2. A strong unit is already able to resist the lateral tension, so there is less to gain.
  5. The ordinary Nepali/Indian brick gets nothing. A 230 × 115 × 57 mm brick laid flat has h/w = 57/115 = 0.50, which is less than 0.75, so kp = 1.0. A modular brick 190 × 90 × 90 mm laid flat has h/w = 90/90 = 1.0, so it earns kp = 1.1 to 1.2 — a genuine 10–20% bonus for the same materials.
  6. Restriction. IS 1905 limits the factor to kp ≤ 1.5 for masonry in which the units are laid in a bond, and it applies only to units laid so that the stated height/width ratio is actually achieved — laying a brick on edge changes the ratio and therefore changes kp.
  7. Design consequence: kp is the one factor in fper = fbkskakp that can be increased free of cost — simply by choosing a taller unit or by laying the same unit differently.

Question 6

6 NumericalDetermine the equivalent eccentricity and the eccentricity ratio of a 230 mm thick and 3 m high wall. The wall carries an axial load of 20 kN/m and an eccentric load of 12 kN/m acting at 6 mm from the centre of the wall. [12]
Given: t = 230 mm; H = 3.0 m; axial load P1 = 20 kN/m at the centre line (e1 = 0); eccentric load P2 = 12 kN/m at e2 = 6 mm from the centre.
Assume: masonry 20 kN/m³; RCC slab bearing top and bottom → full restraint. Work per metre run.
Principle — what “equivalent eccentricity” means
When several vertical loads act on a wall at different eccentricities, they are replaced by a single resultant load W = ΣPi acting at the equivalent eccentricity e, found by taking moments about the centre line of the wall:

  e = Σ Pi eiΣ Pi     and     eccentricity ratio = et
Resultant load
W = P₁ + P₂ = 20.00 + 12.00 = 32.00 kN/m
Equivalent eccentricity
Taking moments of the loads about the CENTRE LINE of the wall : W · e = P₁·e₁ + P₂·e₂ 32.00 × e = (20.00 × 0) + (12.00 × 6) 32.00 × e = 72.00 kN·mm/m e = 72.00 / 32.00 = 2.25 mm
Eccentricity ratio
e / t = 2.25 / 230 = 0.00978 = 1 / 102.2 ≈ 1 / 102
Interpret the result  IS 1905 cl. 5.4.1
Range of eTreatment required by IS 1905
e ≤ t/24 The eccentricity is so small that it may be IGNORED; ks is read from the e = 0 column of Table 9.
