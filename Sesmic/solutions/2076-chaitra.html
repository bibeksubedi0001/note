Question 1

1 (a) TheoryState and explain briefly the factors affecting the compressive strength of brick masonry. [8]

Group the answer into four heads — this structure alone earns most of the marks.

GroupFactorHow it affects the strength
A. Properties
of the unit		 Crushing strength of the unit The single most important factor. Masonry strength is roughly proportional to fb0.7, but is only 25–50% of the unit strength.
Shape — height/width ratio as laid A taller unit means fewer bed joints per metre and less lateral tension, so the masonry is stronger. Allowed for by the shape modification factor kp (IS 1905 Table 10).
Frogs, perforations, voidsReduce the net bearing area and cause stress concentrations.
Water absorption / initial rate of suction A dry, absorbent brick sucks water out of the mortar and destroys the bond — bricks must be soaked before laying.
Uniformity of size and squarenessIrregular units force thick, uneven joints.
B. Properties
of the mortar		 Grade / strength of the mortar Increases the masonry strength, but much less than proportionately: doubling the mortar strength may gain only 15–25%. Masonry strength ∝ fm0.3.
Deformability of the mortar A softer mortar expands laterally more under load and puts more lateral tension into the unit → earlier vertical splitting.
Workability, water retentivity, mix proportion Richer than 1:3 shrinks and cracks; leaner than 1:6 is harsh and does not fill the joints.
C. Geometry
of the masonry		 Thickness of the mortar joint Very significant. A 10 mm joint is standard; a 20 mm joint can lose 20–30% of the strength.
Slenderness ratio Higher SR → buckling and secondary moments → permissible stress reduced by ks (Table 9). SR 27 gives only ~43% of the stress allowed at SR 6.
Eccentricity of the load Produces bending; the permissible stress is reduced through the same factor ks.
Cross-sectional area Small sections have no redundancy: ka = 0.7 + 1.5A for A < 0.2 m².
D. Workmanship Filling of the vertical (head) joints The commonest defect on site; unfilled perpends can lose a third of the strength.
Bond pattern and corner interlocking Continuous vertical joints split a wall into independent slender leaves.
Verticality and uniformity of courses An out-of-plumb wall introduces an unintended eccentricity.
Curing, age and rate of construction Building too fast loads green mortar; masonry gains strength for months.
All of this is condensed by IS 1905 into   fper = fb · ks · ka · kp   where fb covers the unit and mortar (Table 8), ks covers slenderness and eccentricity (Table 9), ka covers small area, and kp covers the unit shape (Table 10).

Finish with the mechanism: under compression the mortar tries to expand laterally more than the unit; friction restrains it, putting the mortar into triaxial compression and the unit into lateral tension, so failure starts as vertical splitting through the units. This is why the unit strength matters far more than the mortar strength, and why the mortar should be weaker than the unit.

Full treatment: Chapter 2 §2.3.4–2.3.5 (Fig. 2.8).

Given: single room, one storey; plan 4 m × 6 m (outer); ceiling height 3.0 m; wall t = 220 mm; lintel band at 2.1 m above the floor; RCC flat slab roof; design earthquake coefficient αh = 0.36; openings neglected.
Assume: masonry 20 kN/m³; concrete M20, steel Fe 415; band cast integrally with the wall over the full thickness (220 mm wide).
Critical band: the one on the 6 m long wall, which spans between the 4 m cross walls.
What loads act on a lintel band?
A seismic band is a horizontal beam bent about TWO axes at the same time:
• Vertically — by the weight of the masonry it supports (the lintel action).
• Horizontally — by the out-of-plane inertia of the wall strip tributary to it; here the band bends in its own plane, using the wall thickness as the depth.
It must be checked for biaxial bending.

Question 2

2 (a) TheoryExplain the design criteria of walls subjected to transverse loading. [8]

“Transverse loading” means load acting perpendicular to the plane of the wall — wind, earthquake inertia, earth pressure or water pressure. IS 1905 lays down the following criteria.

  1. Slenderness ratio. The SR of the wall must not exceed the limits of Table 7 (27 for a load-bearing wall in cement mortar, 30 for a panel or curtain wall, 10 for a free-standing wall or parapet). This is the first and most important control on out-of-plane behaviour, because the out-of-plane capacity depends primarily on h/t.
  2. Flexural tensile stress must be within the permissible value. IS 1905 cl. 5.4.2 permits, for cement mortars M1 or richer, 0.07 N/mm² for bending in the vertical direction (tension across the bed joints, i.e. when the wall spans between floor and roof) and 0.14 N/mm² for bending in the horizontal direction (tension across the head joints, i.e. when the wall spans between cross walls). No tension whatever is allowed in an earthquake combination in lime or mud mortar, and no tension is permitted in the direction normal to the bed joint in the case of a DPC or a similar slip plane.
  3. Precompression must be used first. The correct design approach is to show that the flexural tensile stress fcb is cancelled by the axial compressive stress fca, i.e. fcb − fca ≤ ft,per. If fcb < fca no tension develops at all and the wall is safe by inspection. The critical section for tension is usually at mid-height, where the moment is high but the precompression is only about half its base value.
  4. Maximum compressive stress. On the compression face, fca + fcb must not exceed the permissible compressive stress, which may be increased by 25% for combined axial and bending stresses.
  5. Increase in permissible stresses for wind or earthquake. When the transverse load is wind or earthquake, all permissible stresses may be increased by 33⅓% (alternatively, design for 0.75 times the combined load).
  6. Effective span and the moment coefficient. The moment depends on the support conditions and must be stated: M = pH²/8 for a wall simply supported top and bottom; M = pH²/10 for a wall with partial restraint, e.g. anchored to the roof with metal anchors; M = pH²/12 for full continuity; M = pH²/2 for a wall free at the top (a parapet or free-standing wall — the worst case). If the wall is supported on all four sides it may be designed for two-way bending using the yield-line coefficients.
  7. Arching action. Where the wall is built tight between rigid supports so that it cannot move away from them, arching action may be relied on to carry the transverse load, and the wall is then checked in compression instead of tension.
  8. Provision of lateral supports. Cross walls, piers, buttresses, bands and a rigid diaphragm must be provided at a spacing that keeps the effective span — and hence the SR — within limits. Anchoring the wall to the floor and roof is mandatory; an unanchored wall behaves as a free cantilever with five times the moment.
  9. Stability against overturning and sliding at the base and at the DPC must be checked separately, with a factor of safety of at least 1.5 (or 2.0 for a free-standing wall).
  10. If the tension cannot be eliminated, the options are: increase the thickness, reduce the span by adding cross walls or piers, add precompression, or provide reinforcement — i.e. change from unreinforced to reinforced masonry.
Full treatment: Chapter 2 §2.6.9 and Chapter 4 §4.8.

3 (a) TheoryDiscuss in brief the failure mechanism of masonry structures and remedial measures. [10]

Answer this in the form of a table that pairs each failure mechanism with the measure that prevents it — that is what earns full marks.

Failure mechanismCauseRemedial / preventive measure
Out-of-plane overturning of walls (the dominant killer) No connection to the floor/roof; no bands; slender wall; flexible diaphragm Horizontal bands at plinth, sill, lintel, floor and roof; anchor the roof and floor to the walls; reduce the span with cross walls; stiffen the diaphragm
Corner separation, vertical cracks at wall junctions Poor interlocking of courses at the corner; walls built at different times Proper corner interlocking of alternate courses; L-shaped band bars lapped 60φ at corners; vertical bars at every corner and T-junction
Delamination of multi-leaf stone or rubble walls Two independent leaves with a rubble core, no cross ties Through-stones or S-shaped/hooked ties every 600 mm vertically and 1.2 m horizontally; grout injection; ferrocement or wire-mesh jacketing
In-plane diagonal (X) shear cracking of piers Storey shear exceeds the diagonal tensile strength; low precompression; weak mortar Increase the wall length or thickness; restrict the openings; improve the mortar; add splint-and-bandage or a shotcrete/ferrocement overlay; add vertical reinforcement
Rocking / toe crushing of slender piers High aspect ratio, low vertical load Widen the pier; add vertical steel and confining tie-columns to develop tension at the heel
Sliding shear along a bed joint or DPC Low friction, weak mortar, smooth DPC membrane Improve the mortar; add dowels / vertical bars across the joint; avoid a smooth slip plane at the DPC
Cracking at openings (corners of doors/windows) Stress concentration; openings too large, too close to a corner, or unbanded Follow the NBC/IS 4326 opening rules: total width of openings ≤ 50% of the wall length; a pier of at least 600 mm between openings; openings at least 600 mm from a corner; sill and lintel bands and vertical bars at the jambs
Gable and parapet collapse Free-standing cantilever with no top restraint Gable band along the slope tied to the roof band; limit the parapet height; add vertical bars
Roof / floor separation and collapse Joists simply resting on the wall; heavy tiled or earth roof Anchor the joists to the wall or band; brace the roof; replace a heavy roof with a light CGI roof (reduces the demand)
Soft storey / short column Open ground storey; partial-height infill; mezzanines Add infill or shear walls at ground level; full-height or fully separated infill; strengthen the ground-storey columns
Pounding between adjacent buildings Inadequate separation gap Provide the code separation gap; align the floor levels
Torsional failure Eccentricity between the centre of mass and the centre of rigidity Symmetric plan; distribute the walls uniformly; add walls to move the centre of rigidity towards the centre of mass
Foundation failure / differential settlement Weak or liquefiable soil, sloping ground Continuous plinth band; ground improvement; uniform bearing; avoid a split-level foundation
The four principles behind every one of these measures: (1) Integrity — tie the whole building into a single box; (2) Strength — enough wall area, good mortar, no oversized openings; (3) Ductility — reinforcement and confinement so it cracks instead of collapsing; (4) Reduced demand — light roof, regular symmetric plan, limited height.
Full treatment with figures: Chapter 3 §3.6 and §3.7.

4 (b) TheoryExplain the following as applied to masonry structures: (i) Stress reduction factor  (ii) Confined masonry. [4×2]

(i) Stress reduction factor, ks

The stress reduction factor is the factor by which the basic compressive stress fb of a masonry element must be multiplied to allow for the combined effect of its slenderness ratio and the eccentricity of the load acting on it.

fper = fb · ks · ka · kp     IS 1905 Table 9
  • Why it is needed: the basic stress fb of Table 8 is measured on a short, axially loaded prism. A real wall is slender, so it buckles and develops secondary moments, and the load on it is usually eccentric, so the stress block is not uniform. Both effects reduce the load the wall can carry.
  • How it is used: enter Table 9 with the slenderness ratio (rows, SR = 6 to 27) and the eccentricity ratio e/t (columns, 0, 1/24, 1/12, 1/6, 1/4, 1/3) and interpolate linearly between the tabulated values.
  • Range: ks = 1.00 at SR = 6 with e = 0, falling to 0.43 at SR = 27 with e = 0 and to only 0.22 at SR = 27 with e/t = 1/6. A slender eccentrically loaded wall may be worth less than a quarter of a short axially loaded one.
  • Companion rules: if e ≤ t/24 the eccentricity may be ignored; if t/24 < e ≤ t/6 the stress is reduced through ks; if e > t/6 tension develops, part of the section becomes ineffective, and the stress must be found from fc = 2P/[3L(t/2 − e)].
  • Blank entries in Table 9 (e.g. SR 24 with e/t = 1/3) mean that the combination is not permitted.

(ii) Confined masonry

Confined masonry consists of load-bearing masonry wall panels confined on all four sides by lightly reinforced concrete tie-columns (vertical) and tie-beams (horizontal).

The defining feature is the construction sequence: the masonry wall is built FIRST, with toothed vertical edges, and the tie-columns are cast afterwards against the completed wall. (In an infilled RC frame it is the opposite — frame first, wall afterwards.)
  • Components: masonry walls (the main load-carrying element), confining tie-columns and tie-beams, floor and roof slabs acting as diaphragms, a plinth band, and the foundation.
  • The ties are NOT a moment frame. They are much smaller than RC frame columns (typically 230 × 230 mm with 4 nos 12 mm bars) and are designed only to confine the masonry, not to carry the gravity load by themselves.
  • The confining elements are effective in: (a) enhancing the stability and integrity of the wall under both in-plane and out-of-plane earthquake loads — they hold the cracked masonry together; (b) enhancing the lateral strength and stiffness of the wall; (c) reducing the brittleness of the masonry and hence improving its earthquake performance.
  • Where the ties are placed: at every wall intersection, at both sides of every large opening, at free ends of walls, and at a spacing not exceeding about 4 m or 1.5 times the wall height.
  • Position in the spectrum: confined masonry is an economical intermediate system between unreinforced masonry and RC frame construction, well suited to 1–4 storey buildings, and it uses the same materials and skills as ordinary masonry.
