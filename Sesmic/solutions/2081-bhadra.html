Question 1

1 (a) TheoryExplain an earthquake as a lateral force in a building. [8]

Solution

An earthquake does not apply a force to a building the way wind or a live load does. The ground merely moves; the force is generated inside the building itself as an inertia force.

Earthquake force is an INERTIA force developed because of ground shaking:

FI = mass × acceleration = m × a = (W/g) × a

The imposed acceleration is largest in the horizontal direction, so the resulting inertia force acts essentially horizontally — that is, laterally on the building.

1. Why a lateral force develops

  1. When the ground shakes, the foundation moves with the ground.
  2. The mass of the roof and floors, by inertia (Newton's first law), resists this motion and tends to stay where it was, so the upper part lags behind the base.
  3. This relative displacement between base and mass distorts the walls and sets up internal forces in them.
  4. Because the ground reverses direction many times a second, the building swings back and forth and the lateral force reverses in sign every cycle — unlike gravity load, which acts in one direction only.
  5. If the building were perfectly rigid, every point would move exactly with the ground and the force would be simply m × PGA. Real buildings are flexible, so different parts move by different amounts and the force also depends on the building's own dynamic properties (period, damping, stiffness).

2. Flow of the inertia force — the seismic load path

Mass of roof / floor slab → roof / floor diaphragm → walls parallel to the shaking (shear walls, acting in-plane) → plinth / bands → foundation → soil
  • Walls parallel to the shaking act as shear walls and carry the force efficiently by in-plane shear — they are strong and stiff in that direction.
  • Walls perpendicular to the shaking are loaded out-of-plane, i.e. as vertical slabs spanning between floor and roof. Unreinforced masonry is very weak in this mode and overturns — the classic cause of collapse of Nepali masonry houses.
  • If any link is missing (no band, no wall-to-roof anchorage, no corner interlocking) the building loses box action and the walls fail individually.

3. Horizontal versus vertical shaking

  • Shaking has three components, but the two horizontal components govern design, because buildings have a large factor of safety against vertical load and very little inherent resistance to horizontal load.
  • The vertical component is normally taken as about two-thirds of the horizontal. It matters for cantilevers and long spans, and for masonry because it momentarily reduces the compressive pre-load and hence the frictional shear capacity of the wall.

4. Sketch to draw

Draw two panels: (a) building at rest; (b) during shaking — base displaced in the direction of ground motion, roof lagging behind, and the inertia force FI = m a marked at roof level acting opposite to the ground motion. Then add the load-path arrows slab → wall → foundation.

1 (b) TheoryDescribe the earthquake force parameters. [8]

Solution

The magnitude of the seismic force a building must resist depends on three families of parameters — those of the ground motion, those of the structure, and the code coefficients that combine them.

(A) Ground motion parameters

ParameterWhy it matters
Peak ground acceleration (PGA)Directly fixes the inertia force F = m a. Expressed in g, m/s² or Gal (1 g = 9.81 m/s² = 981 Gal).
Peak ground velocity (PGV)Best single indicator of damage to medium-period structures; governs the energy input.
Peak ground displacement (PGD)Governs long-period structures, bridges, pipelines and separation gaps.
Duration of strong motionNumber of load reversals — masonry degrades cumulatively, so long duration is very damaging.
Frequency content / predominant periodDecides whether resonance with the building occurs.
Magnitude and focal depthControl the energy released and how much of it reaches the surface.
Epicentral / hypocentral distanceAmplitude attenuates with distance; near-field records contain damaging velocity pulses.
Local soil stiffnessAcceleration is amplified in soft soil and the predominant period lengthens.
Topographic amplificationRidges and hill tops amplify base-rock acceleration by resonance.

(B) Structural parameters

ParameterWhy it matters
Seismic weight / mass WForce is proportional to mass. Heavy masonry and heavy roofs attract large forces — reducing mass is the cheapest seismic improvement.
StiffnessGoverns how the force distributes among the walls, and the drift.
Fundamental natural period TaFixes Sa/g read from the spectrum. More flexibility → longer T; more mass → longer T. Range 0.05–2.0 s for 1–20 storeys; T < 0.4 s for low-rise masonry.
DampingEnergy dissipation; codes assume 5% of critical. Higher damping reduces response.
DuctilityCapacity for inelastic deformation without collapse; permits the design force to be reduced by R.
Configuration & regularityPlan/vertical irregularity, torsion, soft or weak storey and mass irregularity all raise the local demand.
Foundation and soil–structure interactionModify the motion actually transmitted to the building.

(C) Code parameters — the design shorthand

VB = AhW      Ah = Z2 · IR · Sag

Z zone factor (regional seismicity, 0.36 for Zone V)  |  I importance factor (occupancy, consequence of failure)  |  R response reduction factor (ductility; I/R ≤ 1.0)  |  Sa/g spectral acceleration coefficient (period + soil)  |  W seismic weight

Question 2

2 (a) TheoryDiscuss the failure mechanisms of the stone masonry buildings with mud mortar during the 2080 Jajarkot Earthquake. [8]

Solution

Background. The 2080 BS (3 November 2023) Jajarkot earthquake was only M 6.4 — a moderate event — yet it destroyed a very large number of houses and killed about 154 people, because the building stock of Jajarkot and Rukum West is almost entirely random rubble stone masonry laid in mud mortar, with heavy mud-and-slate roofs and no seismic features whatever.

#Failure mechanismWhy it happened in stone-in-mud construction
1Delamination of the wall through its thicknessThick two- or three-leaf walls with a loose rubble core and no through-stones. The leaves have no connection, so the outer leaf peels off and the wall loses most of its effective section. The dominant mechanism in this earthquake.
2Out-of-plane overturning of wallsFlexible timber/slate roofs simply resting on the walls, no anchorage, no bands, and heavy walls with a high height-to-thickness ratio. Once a wall is unsupported at the top it topples as a rigid block.
3Corner separation and disintegration at junctionsRounded and irregular stones give no interlocking at the corners, and mud mortar has essentially zero tensile and bond strength — so the corner simply comes apart, leaving each wall unsupported at its ends.
4Bulging and buckling of the wallRandom rubble with thick mud joints behaves as loose stacked stone once shaking begins; the wall bulges outwards and buckles.
5Diagonal / stepped shear cracking of piersWith weak mortar the diagonal crack steps around the stones through the joints rather than passing through them (bed-joint sliding mode).
6Collapse of the heavy mud-and-slate roofVery large mass high up, unanchored — a large inertia force applied to walls that cannot resist it; the roof then falls in on the occupants.
7Gable and parapet collapseNo gable band and almost no vertical load to stabilise them.
8Loss of strength of the mud mortarRain before and after the event further weakened an already negligible bond.
9Total (pancake) collapseOnce the walls disintegrated the floors and roof lost all support — giving a very high fatality rate for a moderate earthquake.

Sketches to draw

(i) Section through a wall showing delamination of the outer leaf from the rubble core; (ii) elevation showing corner separation with vertical cracks at the junction; (iii) section showing simple out-of-plane overturning about the base with the roof dropping in.

One-line conclusion worth writing: the primary causes were the mud mortar (no bond), the absence of through-stones (no monolithic wall), the absence of bands and anchorage (no box action) and the heavy unanchored roof (large inertia force) — not the size of the earthquake.
Full treatment: Chapter 3 §3.9.2, with the failure-mode figures in §3.6 and §3.7.
2 (b) TheoryExplain the strengthening procedure for the damaged masonry buildings. [8]

Solution

Answer in two parts — first the procedure (the sequence of activities), then the techniques.

A. The procedure

  1. Post-earthquake damage assessment. Survey the building from outside, identify and map the cracks, examine the site for geotechnical hazard, inspect from inside and at critical locations, then interpret. Classify the damage on the five-grade EMS scale (G1 negligible → G5 destruction).
  2. Decide the strategy from the damage grade. G1–G2 → architectural repair plus seismic strengthening; G3 → grouting of cracks + repair + strengthening; G4–G5 → vacate, then either extensive restoration or demolish and rebuild.
  3. Emergency shoring and propping of unstable walls before any work starts, and removal of dangerous debris, parapets and overhangs.
  4. Identify the deficiency that caused the damage — no bands, no through-stones, large openings, weak mortar, heavy roof, soft storey — and select measures that address that deficiency.
  5. Execute in the right order: restore integrity first (crack repair, corner stitching), then add strength and ductility (bands, vertical bars, jacketing), then reduce demand (lighten the roof, reduce openings).
  6. Quality control and supervision throughout, followed by re-inspection.

B. The techniques

PurposeTechnique
Restore continuity across cracksGrouting / injection of cement or polymer grout into fine cracks; stitching of wide cracks with steel or reinforced-concrete dowels laid across the crack; re-pointing of eroded joints.
Rebuild badly damaged masonryDismantle and rebuild the shattered portion in good mortar, toothed into the sound masonry.
Stop delamination of stone wallsInsert through-stones (bond stones), or substitutes — S-shaped steel ties, wooden dowels, concrete bond blocks — at about 1.2–1.5 m spacing in both directions.
Prevent corner separationCorner stitching with RC or timber elements connecting the two orthogonal walls at 0.5–0.7 m vertical spacing; add vertical bars at all corners and T-junctions, from foundation to roof.
Create box actionAdd horizontal bands (RC, steel or timber) at plinth, sill, lintel and eave level in every storey, and a gable band for a sloped roof.
Increase in-plane and out-of-plane strengthSplint-and-bandage (vertical splints at corners and jambs plus horizontal bandages at band levels); wire-mesh and cement (ferrocement) jacketing on one or both faces; FRP / textile-reinforced mortar overlays; shotcrete jacketing.
Stiffen the diaphragmBrace the timber floor/roof with diagonals or plywood sheathing; anchor it to the walls with metal anchors.
Reduce demandReplace a heavy roof with a light one; reduce and re-position openings by infilling parts of large openings; remove heavy parapets and projections; reduce the number of storeys.
Strengthen the foundationUnderpinning or widening where differential settlement contributed.
Mitigation table: Chapter 3 §3.7.6 and Fig. 3.18 (containment reinforcement); repair and retrofit techniques belong to Chapter 6.

3 (a) TheoryWhat are the physical properties of bricks? [4]

Solution

The physical properties are those that can be observed or measured without loading the brick to failure (as opposed to the mechanical properties such as compressive strength and modulus).

PropertyRequirement / typical value
Size (Nepal Standard NS 1:2035)240 × 115 × 57 mm with a 10 mm mortar joint. Modular rule: length = 2 × width + one vertical joint. (IS 1077 modular brick 190×90×90 mm; common site brick often 230×110×55 mm.)
ShapeRegular, truly rectangular, sharp square edges, parallel faces, free from warpage, distortion and bowing.
ColourDeep cherry red or copper colour, uniform throughout.
TextureEven, fine-grained and compact; free from grit, lumps and nodules of lime.
SoundnessEmits a clear ringing (metallic) sound when two bricks are struck together; should not break when dropped flat from about 1 m.
HardnessNo impression left when scratched with a finger nail.
Water absorptionNot more than 15% of its own weight after 24 hours immersion at normal temperature (hand-made bricks with a frog, up to 25%).
EfflorescenceNil to slight — no white salt deposit after immersion and drying.
Density / unit weightAbout 1800–2000 kg/m³; brick masonry taken as 19–20 kN/m³.
Structural qualityThoroughly burnt, free from cracks, flaws and organic matter.

Question 4

4 (a) TheoryDescribe the nature of the masonry structure. [4]

Solution

Masonry is a composite structural material formed by bonding individual units (brick, stone, block, adobe) with layers of mortar. Its essential character can be summed up in nine points:

CharacteristicExplanation and structural consequence
Composite / two-phaseNot one material but two — stiff strong units bedded in a softer weaker mortar. Behaviour is governed by their interaction and by the unit–mortar bond, not by either alone.
Heterogeneous, highly variableProperties scatter from point to point in the same wall, so design values are conservative “minimum assured” values.
Anisotropic (directional)Bed and head joints form continuous planes of weakness, so strength and stiffness depend on the direction of loading relative to the joints.
Strong in compression, very weak in tension and shearSuits walls, columns, arches and domes; cannot be used for beams or slabs, and cracks as soon as flexural tension appears.
Brittle, little ductilityFails suddenly with little warning and dissipates very little energy — the opposite of what cyclic earthquake loading demands.
Heavy — large massLarge self weight → large inertia force F = m a and heavy foundations. Mass is both its stabiliser (gravity friction) and its enemy (seismic demand).
ModularThe unit fixes the module; wall thicknesses, lengths and opening sizes are multiples of the unit dimension plus a joint.
Extremely sensitive to workmanshipFull even bed joints, filled perpends, correct bond, corner interlocking and curing govern the real strength far more than the nominal design.
Non-engineered by traditionMost masonry worldwide is still built by artisans without any structural calculation.
Full treatment: Chapter 2 §2.2.2.

Question 5

5 (a) TheoryExplain reinforced masonry. [4]

Solution

Reinforced masonry is masonry designed on the assumption that the flexural tensile stresses cannot be resisted by the masonry and are resisted by reinforcement only. Shear stresses may be resisted by the masonry or by reinforcement, singly or in combination. Design may be by allowable stress design or by strength design.
  • Why it is needed. Brickwork and blockwork, like concrete, have high compressive strength but very low tensile resistance. Steel bars are therefore introduced to carry the tension, exactly as in reinforced concrete.
  • Where the steel goes. Advantage is taken of the voids created by particular bonds and/or of the bed joints — vertical bars grouted into the hollow cores of blocks or into pockets, and horizontal bed-joint reinforcement (ladder or truss mesh) laid in the mortar. Bed-joint reinforcement in particular enhances the capacity of walls to resist lateral loading.
  • Contrast with unreinforced masonry (URM). URM is designed assuming the flexural tensile stress is resisted by the masonry, and any reinforcement present is neglected in design; design is in the linear elastic range. So “unreinforced masonry” may actually contain steel — the term refers to the design assumption, not to the absence of bars.
  • Benefits: genuine flexural and shear capacity, real ductility and energy dissipation, much better out-of-plane performance, and the ability to build taller and with larger openings.
  • Applications: shear walls, retaining walls, tanks, footbridges, reinforced masonry columns, beams and RC/reinforced-masonry bands. Prestressed (post-tensioned) masonry adds precompression through vertical tendons.
  • History: used in India and Japan since after the First World War, and in America since after the Second.
See Chapter 2 §2.4.2(D); the full treatment is Chapter 5 (Reinforced Masonry).
