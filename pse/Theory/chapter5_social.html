Social Engineering
The deliberate, planned effort to influence and shape social behaviour, attitudes and structures on a large scale — using policy, technology, education and institutions to guide society toward desired goals such as inclusion, development and equity.

For engineers, social engineering means designing not just physical structures but also the social processes around them — ensuring projects are accepted, equitable and sustainable. Its scope covers community mobilization, behaviour change (e.g. sanitation, safety), inclusion of marginalized groups and managing planned social change.

Social Change

Social change is the alteration of social structure, cultural patterns and behaviour over time; society is never static. Eight factors drive it: technological (the most powerful), cultural, physical/environmental, demographic, economic, social conflict, social movements and human ideas.

Figure 5.1: Four Theories of Social Change
Conflict theory in Nepal: Marx’s class-struggle model explains the Maoist insurgency (1996–2006), the shift from monarchy to republic (2008) and the Dalit/Janajati/Madhesi movements — changes driven by inequality between the “haves” and “have-nots.”

Society vs Community
Society (Latin socius, companionship) is an abstract web of social relationships — not a mere collection of people. A community is a concrete group of people living in a definite locality with a shared “we-feeling.”
BasisSocietyCommunity
NatureAbstract (relationships)Concrete (people in a place)
GeographyNo fixed areaDefinite locality

Criteria & Elements of Society

Four criteria of society: population, common territory, government/political authority and common culture. Characteristics/elements: plurality of individuals, web of social relationships, likeness and difference, interdependence, cooperation and conflict, permanence, dynamic nature, abstract nature, social control and culture.

Elements of community: a group of people, definite locality, community sentiment (we-feeling), cultural similarity, permanency, naturality, no legal status and a particular name.

Parsons’ six fundamental social needs: organizing activities, protection, replacement (reproduction), transmission of knowledge, motivation and conflict resolution. The five basic social institutions are Family, Religion, Economy, Education and the State.

Why society matters to an engineer: engineers serve society’s needs, draw resources and labour from it, and their works (roads, water, power) reshape it — making the man–society relationship reciprocal and essential to the profession.

Social Inclusion
The process of ensuring that all people — especially marginalized groups (women, Dalits, Janajatis, Madhesis and persons with disabilities) — can participate in and benefit from development decisions on an equal footing.

Ladder of Participation

Figure 5.2: Ladder of Citizen Participation (non-participation → tokenism → citizen power)

How participation empowers: it creates ownership and responsibility, builds skills and capacity, gives a voice to marginalized groups (e.g. the 50% women requirement in community-forestry committees), delivers economic empowerment and strengthens local institutions. Nepal’s inclusive development rests on the “3 I’s”: Inclusion, Investment, Infrastructure.

Advantages of participation
  • Ownership & sustainability
  • Demand-driven, relevant design
  • Local knowledge & transparency
  • Conflict minimization
Disadvantages
  • Time-consuming & costly
  • Risk of elite capture
  • Conflicting interests
  • Needs skilled facilitation

Stakeholder
Any individual, group or organization that can affect, or is affected by, a project — including clients, users, communities, government, contractors, funders and civil society.

Identification and Analysis

Identification lists everyone with a stake. Analysis assesses each stakeholder’s power (influence) and interest (stake). To understand social dynamics on the ground, engineers use ethnographic methods: participant observation, in-depth interviews, focus group discussions (FGD), field notes, case studies, surveys and content analysis — which reveal power relations, inequality and marginalization.

Stakeholder Mapping: the Power–Interest Grid

Figure 5.3: Stakeholder Power–Interest Grid (Mendelow’s Matrix)

Engagement & communication is then tailored to each quadrant — manage high-power/high-interest stakeholders closely, keep others satisfied, informed or merely monitored. Participatory tools such as PRA (Participatory Rural Appraisal), KII (Key Informant Interview), beneficiary feedback and outcome mapping support ongoing engagement.

5.5 CORPORATE SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY (CSR)

LIKELY / PRACTICE QUESTIONS:

  1. Define Corporate Social Responsibility. Explain Carroll’s CSR pyramid. [4]
  2. Discuss the benefits of CSR for an engineering company. [4]
  3. Write short notes on: CSR provisions in Nepal. [2]
Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)
A company’s commitment to operate ethically and contribute to economic development while improving the quality of life of its workforce, the local community and society at large — going beyond profit and legal compliance.

Carroll’s CSR Pyramid

Figure 5.4: Carroll’s Four-Level CSR Pyramid

The pyramid builds from the base upward: be profitable (economic), obey the law (legal), be ethical (do what is right) and be a good corporate citizen (philanthropic).

Benefits and Nepal Context

  • Improved reputation, brand value and stakeholder trust.
  • Better community relations and a “social licence to operate.”
  • Employee motivation and retention; long-term sustainability.
Nepal: the Industrial Enterprises Act requires medium and large industries to allocate at least 1% of annual net profit to CSR activities — typically education, health, disaster relief and local infrastructure. Hydropower and cement companies commonly fund local schools, roads and drinking water.

QUICK REFERENCE: KEY FACTS

  • Society = abstract web of relationships; Community = concrete group in a locality with “we-feeling.”
  • 4 theories of social change: Evolutionary, Functionalist, Conflict (Marx), Cyclical.
  • Criteria of society: population, territory, government, culture.
  • Participation ladder: Manipulation → Information → Consultation → Partnership → Delegated Power.
  • Nepal 3 I’s: Inclusion, Investment, Infrastructure.
  • Power–Interest grid: Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor.
  • CSR pyramid: Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic; Nepal 1% of net profit.
