6.1 ENGINEER AS AN AGENT OF CHANGE 2080 2079 2078 2075 2073 2071 2070

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS (mapped from EPP & TES):

  1. Explain in brief the role of engineers in social change. [3] (2080 Chaitra, Q1)
  2. Explain why society is important for engineers. What are the key roles an engineer plays in society? [4] (2078 Chaitra, Q1a)
  3. Why is society necessary for engineers? What roles can an engineer play in society? [5] (2070 Bhadra, Q1a)
  4. Discuss the participatory approach for community management and the engineer’s role as facilitator in development activities. [4+4] (2079 Shrawan, Q2)
  5. Discuss the role of engineers as facilitators in a community-led development project. [4] (2080 Chaitra, Q2)
  6. Discuss the role of engineer in community development. [8] (2073 Bhadra, Q4)

Engineers are among the most powerful agents of change in society: they translate scientific knowledge into the roads, water systems, power, buildings and networks that transform how people live.

Five Key Roles of Engineers in Society

Figure 6.1: Five Key Roles of Engineers in Society

Role in social change: technology introduction, infrastructure development, innovation (e.g. earthquake-resistant construction after the 2015 Gorkha earthquake), knowledge transfer (training communities) and positive environmental impact (solar, hydropower). Engineers created mass production, automation, faster transport and mass communication — reshaping the pace and structure of life.

Engineer as Facilitator

In community-led development, the engineer acts not as a boss but as a facilitator who keeps a neutral view and makes processes easier. The eight facilitator roles are: technical expert, scope definition, process facilitation, activity management, meeting leadership, communication hub, conflict resolution and capacity building. In developmental activities the engineer creates a vision → prepares a mission → executes → monitors and evaluates → trains new engineers.

Community management is the management of Common Pool Resources by the community itself, guided by the 6 C principles: Content, Cross-promotion, Creating relationships, Cultivating relationships, Collaboration and Consistency.

  1. Explain the perception of the general public towards engineers and their professional work in the context of Nepal. [2] (2081 Shrawan, Q2a)
  2. Define professional ethics. Why do engineers behave unethically? Explain the role of the NEC to control immoral acts of engineers in Nepal. [1+1+2] (2078 Kartik, Q3a)
  3. Why is it necessary for a professional to follow a code of conduct? [Part of 4] (2078 Chaitra, Q2a)
  4. How do NEA and NEC contribute to keeping the profession ethical? [Part of 5] (2079 Chaitra, Q1b)
  5. Write short notes on: Impact of the engineering profession. [2.5] (2069 Bhadra, Q5a)
Public Accountability
The obligation of engineers to answer to the public for the safety, quality and consequences of their work — because engineering is “public property, subject to public evaluation.”

The NSPE Fundamental Canons place public welfare first: hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public; serve only within one’s competence; make objective, truthful public statements; act as faithful agents; avoid deception; and conduct oneself honourably and lawfully. The NEC code (Act 2055) reinforces personal responsibility — each engineer is individually accountable for all their professional work.

Ethical citizenship means the engineer acts not only as a technician but as a responsible citizen: opposing corruption, protecting public resources, being transparent and using professional skill for the common good. An accountability gap appears when structures fail — the collapses in the 2015 earthquake exposed the cost of weak accountability.

Public perception in Nepal: engineers are generally respected as experts, but trust is dented by project delays, cost overruns and corruption. Rebuilding trust requires competence, transparency, integrity and visible public service.

6.3 BALANCING TECHNICAL AND SOCIAL EXPECTATIONS 2073 2070

PAST EXAM QUESTIONS (mapped from EPP):

  1. Explain how individual freedom balances societal goals. [4] (2073 Bhadra, Q1b)
  2. Why does conflict exist between an individual’s freedom and the social goal? [5] (2070 Magh, Q1a)
  3. Globalization transfers technology and development but also a culture that loosens confidence in nationality. How can people work for development within their own culture, with others as a guide? Explain. [10] (2070 Bhadra, Q4)

Every engineering decision sits between technical optimality (what is cheapest, fastest, most efficient) and social expectation (what is safe, fair, culturally acceptable and environmentally sound). The engineer must balance the two.

Individual Freedom vs Societal Goals

Why conflict exists: self-interest competes with the collective good, resources are scarce, and values differ. In professional practice this shows as cost-cutting vs safety standards, or client interest vs public interest.

The classic principle of balance: “Your liberty to swing your fist ends just where my nose begins.” Balance is achieved through social control (laws, ethics, norms), placing public welfare above personal gain, and designing innovation that serves society.

Global Technology vs Local Culture — “Glocalization”

Think globally, act locally. Nepal should adopt technology selectively rather than imitate blindly — integrating indigenous knowledge, using foreign expertise as a guide (not a master), building local capacity, and strengthening local identity through supportive policy (national standards benchmarked to international ones, protection of local industry).

6.4 CONTRIBUTION TO SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT GOALS (SDGs)

LIKELY / PRACTICE QUESTIONS:

  1. What are the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)? How can engineers contribute to achieving them? [6]
  2. Explain the role of civil engineers in achieving SDG 6 (Clean Water), SDG 7 (Clean Energy) and SDG 9 (Infrastructure). [4]
  3. “Construction is destruction to environment.” Do you agree? Relate your answer to sustainable development. [4] (mapped from EPP 2081 Shrawan, Q6b)
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
A set of 17 global goals adopted by the United Nations in 2015 (the 2030 Agenda) to end poverty, protect the planet and ensure prosperity for all — the successor to the Millennium Development Goals.

Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations. Engineers are central to delivering the SDGs because most goals require physical infrastructure and technology.

In Nepal, engineers advance the SDGs through the LEP approach (Labour-based, Environment-friendly, Participatory), the mandatory EIA/IEE process (EPA 2053) and inclusion of marginalized communities in planning and benefit-sharing. On “construction is destruction,” the balanced view is that construction does disturb the environment, but through EIA, mitigation and green design it can proceed sustainably rather than destructively.

QUICK REFERENCE: KEY FACTS

  • 5 roles of engineers: Problem Solver, Innovator, Visionary/Planner, Guardian of Safety, Agent of Economic Growth.
  • Engineer as facilitator: neutral guide who makes community processes easier (8 roles).
  • Public accountability: engineering is public property; NSPE canon — public safety paramount.
  • Balance principle: “liberty to swing your fist ends where my nose begins.”
  • Glocalization: think globally, act locally; foreign expertise as guide, not master.
  • SDGs: 17 goals (UN 2015); engineers key to SDG 6, 7, 9, 11, 13.
  • 3 pillars: Environment, Economic, Social.
