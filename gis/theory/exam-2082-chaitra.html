(b) Positional accuracy of a map digitised from a 1:25,000 source [5]

Given: Smallest mark drawable on map = 0.5 mm; source scale = 1:25,000.

Concept: The minimum detectable distance on the paper map (0.5 mm) translates to a real-world distance equal to 0.5 mm × scale denominator. This is the best positional accuracy that can ever be achieved from that source.

RMS planimetric error = 0.5 mm × 25,000 = 12,500 mm = 12.5 m

Interpretation: Any feature digitised from a 1:25,000 source map cannot be located on the ground better than ±12.5 m. Even with a perfect digitiser and operator, the inherent map accuracy limits the result. Therefore the digitised dataset is unsuitable for engineering work requiring sub-metre precision (e.g. cadastral survey, road centre-line setting-out) but acceptable for regional planning, watershed studies and small-scale infrastructure mapping.

Question 2: Infrastructure Development Map — Kailali District [4 + 3 Marks]

(a) Choice of map projection and coordinate system [4]

Kailali lies in the far-western Tarai of Nepal, between approximately 28°15′–29°15′N and 80°30′–81°30′E, falling within UTM Zone 44N.

Recommended projection: Modified Universal Transverse Mercator (MUTM) Zone 81 (the official projection of the Survey Department of Nepal) or standard UTM Zone 44N on the WGS-84 datum.

Why not Geographic (lat/long)? Latitude/longitude is angular — one cannot directly compute area or length in metres. For construction quantities, alignment, and overlay analysis, a projected metric system is mandatory.

(b) Effect of scale & projection errors on area and distance [3]

  • Scale distortion: Every projection has a line of true scale; away from it the scale factor (k) deviates from 1. In UTM, k = 0.9996 along the central meridian and grows to ~1.001 at the zone boundary. Distances measured on the map differ from ground distance by this factor; areas differ by k². In a district as wide as Kailali this can introduce errors of 0.04–0.1 % in distance and up to 0.2 % in area.
  • Projection error: Transverse Mercator is conformal but not equal-area. If land-acquisition area is calculated directly from UTM coordinates without applying the area scale-factor, the computed area can be slightly larger or smaller than the true ground area, potentially causing financial/legal disputes.
  • Mitigation: apply the scale-factor correction for distance (true distance = grid distance / k); for areas use either an equal-area auxiliary projection (e.g. Albers) or apply the local area scale-factor (k²).

Question 3: Raster vs Vector & Raster Compression [3 + 4 Marks]

(a) Raster vs Vector for satellite imagery and cadastral maps [3]

DatasetRecommended modelReason
Satellite imagery Raster Imagery is naturally a regular grid of pixels recording reflectance values per band. The raster model preserves continuous spectral information, supports band-math, classification, NDVI, filtering and texture analysis. Vectorising every pixel would explode storage and discard the radiometric content.
Cadastral maps Vector Cadastral parcels are discrete polygons with sharp boundaries, owner attributes, and exact geometry. Vector preserves topology (shared edges between adjoining plots), supports precise area/perimeter computation, enables attribute joins (owner, tax, land-use) and clean plotting at any scale — all critical for legal land records.

For land-use mapping the two are combined: the satellite raster is classified to produce a thematic raster, then converted to vector polygons that can be overlaid on cadastral parcels.

(b) Run-length and Quadtree compression for raster data [4]

1. Run-Length Encoding (RLE)

RLE compresses a raster by scanning row-by-row and storing each consecutive sequence of identical cell values as a pair: (value, run-length).

Example — one row of a land-use raster (F = Forest, A = Agriculture, U = Urban):

Original (16 cells): F F F F F A A A A A A U U U U U
RLE encoded:         (F,5) (A,6) (U,5)        →  6 numbers instead of 16

Best when: raster has large homogeneous patches (land-use, soil maps). Poor when: raster is highly heterogeneous (a satellite image at full resolution) — RLE may even increase the file size.

2. Quadtree

The raster is recursively subdivided into four equal quadrants. Any quadrant that is uniform (all cells the same value) is stored as a single leaf node; non-uniform quadrants are subdivided further until uniformity is reached or the single-cell limit is hit.

Best when: the raster contains both large uniform regions and small detailed regions (land-cover, soil). Trade-off: traversal is more complex than RLE but compression is far better for two-dimensional clusters.

Question 4: GPS Positioning & DGPS [4 + 4 Marks]

(a) How GPS determines location using satellite signals [4]

GPS positioning is based on the principle of trilateration using precise time measurements of radio signals transmitted by satellites.

  1. Signal transmission: Each GPS satellite broadcasts a unique pseudo-random code together with its own ephemeris (precise orbit) and the exact time of transmission, generated by an on-board atomic clock.
  2. Travel-time measurement: The receiver matches the incoming code with an internally generated replica and measures the time-shift Δt between transmission and reception.
  3. Pseudo-range: The distance from satellite i to the receiver is Ri = c × Δti, where c ≈ 3×108 m/s. It is called a pseudo-range because the receiver clock is biased by an unknown offset dt.
  4. Trilateration: Each pseudo-range defines a sphere of radius Ri centred on the satellite. With three spheres the receiver lies on a unique point in space; a fourth satellite is needed to solve for the clock bias dt. The four equations are: 
    (Xi−x)² + (Yi−y)² + (Zi−z)² = (Ri − c·dt)²    i = 1…4
    Solved simultaneously for the unknowns x, y, z, dt.
  5. Output: Geocentric (ECEF) coordinates are converted to lat/long/height on a chosen datum (typically WGS-84).

(b) Major sources of GPS error and how DGPS improves accuracy [4]

Error sourceMagnitudeCause
Ionospheric delay1–10 mFree electrons in ionosphere slow the signal.
Tropospheric delay0.5–2 mWater vapour and pressure in lower atmosphere.
Satellite clock & orbit (ephemeris)1–3 mImperfect clock model and predicted orbit.
Multipath0.5–5 mSignal reflects off buildings, water surfaces.
Receiver noise0.3–1 mHardware thermal noise, code-tracking jitter.
Geometry (PDOP)multiplierPoor satellite distribution magnifies all above.

Differential GPS (DGPS) improves accuracy from typical 5–15 m down to 0.5–3 m (or sub-cm with carrier-phase RTK) using these steps:

  1. A Reference (Base) station is set up at a precisely-known surveyed point.
  2. The base receives the same satellite signals and computes its own position. The difference between observed and known position is the error correction — atmospheric, satellite-clock and ephemeris errors are common to nearby receivers.
  3. Corrections are broadcast in real time (radio, GSM, NTRIP) to the Rover receiver.
  4. The rover applies the corrections to its own pseudo-ranges, eliminating the spatially-correlated errors.

Why DGPS works: If the base and rover are within ~30 km, both observe nearly identical atmospheric and satellite errors; subtraction cancels them out. Multipath and receiver noise are not corrected (site-specific) but their effect is small.

Question 5: RDBMS for GIS [4 + 3 Marks]

(a) Concept of RDBMS & suitability for spatial / non-spatial data [4]

A Relational Database Management System (RDBMS) stores data in two-dimensional tables (relations) consisting of rows (tuples) and columns (attributes). Tables are linked through primary keys and foreign keys, and queried using a declarative language — SQL. Examples: PostgreSQL/PostGIS, Oracle Spatial, SQL Server, MySQL.

Key characteristics:

  • Tabular structure with a fixed schema (defined column names & data types).
  • Primary & foreign keys enforce uniqueness and referential integrity between tables.
  • ACID transactions (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) guarantee data reliability under concurrent edits.
  • SQL query language — declarative, optimisable, standardised.
  • Indexing (B-tree for attributes, R-tree/GiST for geometry) for fast retrieval.
  • Multi-user, role-based security.

Why RDBMS suits GIS data:

  • Modern spatial RDBMS (PostGIS, Oracle Spatial) extends standard SQL with a geometry / geography data type plus spatial functions (ST_Intersects, ST_Buffer, ST_Within) so spatial & attribute data live in one system.
  • R-tree spatial indexing makes "find all parcels within 500 m of this river" execute in milliseconds even on millions of features.
  • Multiple GIS clients (ArcGIS, QGIS, web maps) can simultaneously read/write through the same RDBMS — supporting enterprise GIS workflows like the Survey Department, road authority, utility companies.
  • ACID transactions prevent half-finished edits from corrupting cadastral or utility datasets.
  • Backup, replication, and version-control mechanisms of RDBMS protect long-term institutional data.

(b) Database structuring & normalisation reduce redundancy and maintain consistency [3]

Normalisation is the systematic decomposition of tables so that each attribute depends only on the whole primary key. The first three normal forms (1NF, 2NF, 3NF) eliminate repeating groups, partial dependencies and transitive dependencies respectively.

Un-normalised parcel table:

ParcelIDOwnerNameOwnerPhoneOwnerAddressLandUseLandUseRate
P-101Ram Bahadur9841…Dhangadhi-5AgricultureNPR 200/m²
P-102Ram Bahadur9841…Dhangadhi-5AgricultureNPR 200/m²

If Ram changes phone number, two rows must be updated → update anomaly.

Normalised (3NF):

  • Owner(OwnerID, Name, Phone, Address)
  • LandUse(LandUseCode, Description, Rate)
  • Parcel(ParcelID, Geometry, OwnerID FK, LandUseCode FK, Area)

Benefits:

  • Redundancy removed — owner details stored once.
  • Consistency — change phone in one place, all parcels reflect it.
  • Insertion / deletion anomalies eliminated — a new owner can be entered before any parcel is assigned; deleting a parcel does not lose owner info.
  • Easier to maintain & extend — adding a new attribute (e.g. owner email) touches only the Owner table.
  • Combined with topological structuring on the spatial side, the geodatabase remains internally consistent (no overlapping parcels, no dangling road segments).

Question 6: Vector Overlay & Buffer Analysis (Road Expansion) [5 + 5 Marks]

Scenario: Existing 7 m two-lane road is to be widened to a 20 m four-lane highway. The corridor passes through agricultural land, residential areas, commercial zones and small forest patches.

(a) Vector overlay operations to determine impacts [5]

Overlay analysis combines two or more vector layers so that the resulting layer carries the geometry and the attributes of all inputs.

Input layers: proposed alignment (line) → buffered to a 20-m corridor polygon (and original 7-m corridor for the "land already in road" subtraction); land-use (polygon: agriculture, residential, commercial, forest); cadastral parcels (polygon).

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Buffer the proposed centre-line by 10 m (each side) → Proposed_Corridor polygon (20 m wide).
  2. Buffer the existing centre-line by 3.5 m → Existing_RoW; Erase Existing_RoW from Proposed_Corridor → New_Acquisition polygon (the strip that must be acquired).
  3. Intersect(New_Acquisition, LandUse) → Acquisition_by_LandUse: every new piece of acquisition split by land-use class with attributes from both layers.
  4. Intersect(New_Acquisition, Parcels) → Acquisition_by_Parcel: each parcel clipped to the corridor with owner attributes preserved.
  5. Calculate Geometry on the result polygons (area in m²).
  6. Summarize / Dissolve by land-use to obtain totals.
Question to answerGIS toolOutput
Total area of agricultural land acquiredIntersect → Select "Agriculture" → Sum(Area)Total ha of farmland lost
Number of residential / commercial parcels affectedIntersect(Corridor, Parcels) → Select by attribute LandUse IN ('Residential','Commercial') → CountParcel count + owner list
Extent of forest impactedIntersect → Select "Forest" → Sum(Area)m² / ha of forest cleared

Key tools: Buffer (creates corridor), Erase / Difference (excludes existing RoW), Intersect (combines geometries & attributes), Clip (alternative when only geometry of one input is needed), Dissolve (aggregates by category), Summary Statistics (totals per class), Spatial Join (counts of parcels within corridor).

(b) Multi-ring buffer for environmental & social impact zones [5]

Two concentric buffers around the proposed alignment are created:

  • Inner 50-m buffer — Direct construction-impact zone: physical occupation by structures, side drains, slope-protection works, displaced land-uses, dust generation, blasting, vegetation clearance.
  • Outer 30-m buffer beyond the 50-m line (i.e. 50–80 m from centre) — Noise & air-pollution assessment zone: traffic noise (typically >55 dB(A) within 80 m of a four-lane highway), vehicle exhaust (NOx, PM2.5) and visual impact.

How these buffers help impact-mitigation planning

BufferUse in mitigation planning
50 m direct-impactQuantifies land-acquisition area, number of structures to demolish, trees to be felled, cost of compensation; identifies parcels needing resettlement; basis for the Land Acquisition Plan.
30 m additional (noise/air)Identifies sensitive receptors (schools, hospitals, religious sites) within 80 m where noise barriers, plantation belts, speed limits or pollution monitoring stations should be installed; informs the EIA report.

Overlaying the two buffers with land-use, settlements, water bodies and forest layers produces an Environmental & Social Impact Map that decision-makers can use to choose alternative alignments, plan compensation, and design mitigation measures (sound barriers, green belts, drainage controls).

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Pre-processing — ensure all rasters share the same projection, cell size and extent.
  2. Slope generation: Surface → Slope on the DEM → Slope_deg. Reclassify (≤8°)→1, (>8°)→0 → S1_slope.

  4. Land-cover suitability: Reclassify Land-Cover → S3_lcov (forest, built-up = 0; rest = 1).
  5. Soil suitability: Reclassify Soil-type → S4_soil (clay/silty-clay = 1; permeable = 0).

Question 9: Electromagnetic Spectrum, Spectral Signatures & Sensor Resolution [4 + 4 Marks]

(a) Electromagnetic spectrum & spectral signatures in land-cover classification [4]

Electromagnetic (EM) spectrum: the continuum of all EM wavelengths, from gamma rays (10⁻¹² m) to radio waves (10² m). Remote sensing of land cover uses mainly visible (0.4–0.7 μm), NIR (0.7–1.3 μm), SWIR (1.3–3 μm), thermal IR (8–14 μm) and microwave (1 mm–1 m) regions.

Role of the spectrum: Different ground objects interact differently with each wavelength — absorbing some, reflecting others. By measuring reflectance in several discrete wavelength bands, the sensor produces a multi-band raster from which surface materials can be discriminated.

Spectral signature: the unique reflectance-vs-wavelength curve of a feature. It is the "fingerprint" used by classification algorithms (Maximum Likelihood, Random Forest, SVM) to assign each pixel to a land-cover class.

Use in classification:

  • Vegetation has very high NIR reflectance and low red reflectance → enables NDVI = (NIR − R)/(NIR + R) for healthy-vegetation mapping.
  • Water is dark in NIR/SWIR → easily masked using band ratios (NDWI).
  • Built-up areas reflect strongly in SWIR with low NIR → detected via NDBI.
  • Soils, snow, minerals each show distinct curves enabling supervised classification.

(b) Spatial, spectral and temporal resolution — importance for an urbanisation study [4]

ResolutionDefinitionTypical examplesImportance for urbanisation study
Spatial The ground size of one pixel — i.e. the smallest object the sensor can resolve. Landsat 8: 30 m; Sentinel-2: 10–20 m; WorldView-3: 0.31 m. Urban features (buildings, roads, individual plots) are small. High spatial resolution (≤5 m) is needed to map roof footprints, road networks, and impervious surfaces accurately.
Spectral Number and width of wavelength bands recorded. Landsat 8: 11 bands; Sentinel-2: 13 bands; Hyperion: 220 bands. More and narrower bands separate land-cover classes that look similar in colour: concrete vs asphalt vs metal roofs, vegetated vs bare soil. SWIR and thermal bands help map heat-island intensity (LST).
Temporal Revisit interval — how often the same area is re-imaged. Landsat: 16 days; Sentinel-2: 5 days; PlanetScope: ~1 day. Urban growth is dynamic. Frequent revisits enable change-detection on a yearly basis, monitoring of new construction, illegal encroachments, and cloud-free imagery during the dry season.

Combined importance: An ideal urbanisation study uses multi-sensor fusion — high-resolution commercial imagery (e.g. WorldView at 0.5 m) for one-time detailed mapping, plus Sentinel-2 (10 m, 5-day, 13 bands) for periodic change monitoring. Trade-offs: increasing spatial detail usually means smaller swath and less frequent coverage, so the GIS analyst chooses a sensor mix that satisfies all three resolutions for the given urban question.

Question 10: Choropleth Maps & Misleading Design [4 Marks]

Definition: A choropleth map is a thematic map in which predefined geographical units (countries, districts, wards, census tracts) are shaded or coloured in proportion to the value of a statistical variable measured for each unit (e.g. population density, literacy rate, COVID cases per 100 000 population). It is the most common technique for visualising area-aggregated quantitative data.

Essential design rules:

  • Map only derived/normalised values (rates, densities, percentages) — not raw counts.
  • Choose an appropriate classification scheme (equal-interval, quantile, natural breaks, standard deviation) and number of classes (typically 4–7).
  • Use a logical colour scheme: sequential for ordered data, diverging when data has a meaningful midpoint, qualitative only for nominal data.
  • Provide a clear legend, scale bar, north arrow, title and data source.

Critical takeaway: A choropleth map is persuasive; planners therefore must look at the legend, classification, normalisation and source before making a decision. A well-designed choropleth turns data into insight; a poorly-designed one turns the same data into propaganda. Best practice is to publish maps together with an interactive version that lets users change classification and colour ramps to verify that the conclusion is robust.
