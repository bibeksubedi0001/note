  1. Why valuation of property is required? Differentiate between obsolescence and depreciation. [5] (2067 Ashadh, Q.4(a))
    Solution

    a) Why valuation of property is required

    Purposes of valuation — why it is required

    1. Buying and selling — to fix a fair price for both parties.
    2. Rent fixation — standard rent is normally 6–10% of the value.
    3. Security of a loan / mortgage — a bank lends only a fraction of the valuated amount.
    4. Taxation — property tax, house tax, wealth tax and municipal rates are assessed on value.
    5. Compulsory acquisition by government, to fix the compensation payable.
    6. Insurance — to fix the sum insured and the premium.
    7. Probate, partition and inheritance — to divide property among heirs.
    8. Court cases and arbitration — to settle disputes.
    9. Auction — to fix the upset (reserve) price.
    10. Speculation and investment decisions — to judge whether a purchase is worthwhile.
    11. Betterment charges, when a public work raises the value of adjoining land.

    b) Obsolescence distinguished from depreciation

    DepreciationObsolescence
    CausePhysical — wear and tear, decay, age, weatheringFunctional — the property becomes out of date, out of fashion or unsuitable for present needs
    Depends onThe age and condition of the property itselfChanges outside the property — new design, new technology, changed taste, changed town planning
    RateSlow and steady; can be calculated year by yearSudden and unpredictable; cannot be forecast

    A building can be physically sound and still lose most of its value through obsolescence — which is why a valuer must consider both.

    a) Valuation, and how a computer helps the engineer in valuation

    a) Why valuation is done, and the parameters affecting the value of a property

    Purposes of valuation — why it is required

    1. Buying and selling — to fix a fair price for both parties.
    2. Rent fixation — standard rent is normally 6–10% of the value.
    3. Security of a loan / mortgage — a bank lends only a fraction of the valuated amount.
    4. Taxation — property tax, house tax, wealth tax and municipal rates are assessed on value.
    5. Compulsory acquisition by government, to fix the compensation payable.
    6. Insurance — to fix the sum insured and the premium.
    7. Probate, partition and inheritance — to divide property among heirs.
    8. Court cases and arbitration — to settle disputes.
    9. Auction — to fix the upset (reserve) price.
    10. Speculation and investment decisions — to judge whether a purchase is worthwhile.
    11. Betterment charges, when a public work raises the value of adjoining land.

    Factors affecting the value of a property

    Physical factors

    • Location — the single most important factor; a central or commercial location commands a far higher value.
    • Size, shape and frontage of the plot; a regular plot with good road frontage is worth more than an irregular one.
    • Type, age and condition of the building, and its remaining useful life.
    • Quality of construction, specification and workmanship.
    • Availability of services — water supply, drainage, electricity, telephone, road access.
    • Soil condition and topography, and liability to flooding or landslide.

    Economic factors

    • Supply and demand for property in that locality.
    • Rental income the property can fetch, and the security of that income.
    • Prevailing rate of interest — a lower rate raises the year's purchase and hence the value.
    • Cost of construction at the present time.
    • Inflation and the general state of the economy.

    Legal and social factors

    • Clear title, freehold or leasehold tenure, and the unexpired lease period.
    • Encumbrances — mortgage, easement, right of way, tenancy.
    • Town planning restrictions — land use zoning, setback, floor area ratio, height limit.
    • Neighbourhood and social environment, proximity to schools, hospitals and markets.
    • Government policy — taxes, rent control, planned public works.

  5. Define value of any property and what is to be satisfied by any property to have its value. A person desires to sell his property having details as: Gross rent = Rs. 5000 per month, total outgoings = 25% of gross rent, estimated future life = 35 yrs, area of land = 200 m², estimated value of land = Rs. 2000 per square meter.     Solution

    a) Value of a property, and what a property must satisfy to have value

    Value is the present worth of the benefits that will arise from the ownership of a property — what it is worth today to its owner or in the market. It is not the same as its cost.

    For a property to possess value, four conditions must all be satisfied:

    1. Utility — it must be capable of satisfying some want or need. A thing of no use to anybody has no value.
    2. Scarcity — it must be limited in supply relative to demand. Air is useful but free because it is not scarce.
    3. Transferability — the ownership must be capable of being transferred, with a clear and marketable title free of dispute.
    4. Effective demand — there must be a purchaser who not only wants it but has the means and the willingness to pay for it. Desire without purchasing power creates no value.

    ENCE 351 §7.1 and §7.4.

Valuation is the art of estimating the fair present value of a property — land, a building, a factory or any other engineering asset. Estimating asks what a thing will cost to build; valuation asks what it is worth now, in its present condition and location.

Purposes of valuation

  1. Buying or selling a property — to fix a fair price.
  2. Mortgage or security — a bank lends against the valued worth.
  3. Rent fixation — standard rent is normally 6–10% of the valuation.
  4. Taxation — property tax, wealth tax, house tax and municipal rates are levied on value.
  5. Compulsory acquisition — compensation when land is taken for a public project.
  6. Insurance — to fix the sum insured and to settle a fire or earthquake claim.
  7. Partition of property among co-owners or heirs.
  8. Court cases and arbitration, and compensation for damage caused by fire, flood or war.
  9. Auction and speculation, and to judge an investment.

Principles of valuation

A valuer must be more than an estimator: the work calls for knowledge of planning and design, of building construction, of the local market, and of the administrative law — town planning rules, building by-laws, rent control and local taxes. The following principles are observed:

  1. Value depends on supply and demand for that kind of property in that locality.
  2. Value depends on the design, specification and quality of the materials used.
  3. Value depends on the location of the property.
  4. Value varies with the purpose for which the valuation is made — the figure for insurance is not the figure for sale.
  5. Value varies with time; a valuation is valid only on its stated date.
  6. The valuer must be impartial and must inspect the property personally.

    Solution

    i) Value and cost

    For a property to have value it must satisfy four conditions: it must have utility (be capable of satisfying a want), be scarce (limited in supply), be transferable (have a clear, marketable title), and command effective demand (there must be a buyer able and willing to pay).

    ii) Salvage value and scrap value

    Scrap value. The value of the dismantled material of a property at the end of its useful life — the broken bricks, steel, timber and other debris sold as material, after deducting the cost of demolition. It is usually taken as about 10% of the cost of construction. A building at the end of its life has scrap value only.

    Salvage value. The value of a property or machine at the end of its useful life to its present owner, when it is sold without being dismantled — it can still be used by somebody else for the same or another purpose. A machine sold second-hand at the end of its economic life to the present user has salvage value.

    Answer any three. All four are given below.

    i) Approximate estimate. A rough estimate prepared at the proposal stage, before detailed drawings exist, from a single overall parameter — plinth area, cube content, cost per bed, per student, per km of road. It is used to judge whether the scheme is worth pursuing, to obtain administrative approval and to provide the amount in the budget. It is supported by a report, a site plan and line drawings, and is accurate only to about ±15–20%.

    ii) Revised estimate. A fresh detailed estimate prepared for a work already sanctioned, when the expenditure exceeds or is likely to exceed the sanctioned amount by more than 5%, or when the rates or the design change materially. It shows the original and the revised quantity and cost of every item side by side, with the reason for each variation, and must be sanctioned afresh by the competent authority.

    iii) Centre line method. A method of taking out quantities in which the total centre-line length of all the walls is found once and then multiplied by the breadth and the depth of each item in turn. Where walls meet, half the breadth of the item is deducted for each junction, because that corner would otherwise be counted twice.

    deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item quantity = (total centre-line length − deduction) × breadth × height

    It is the quickest method and is best suited to buildings in which all the walls have the same thickness.

    iv) Capitalized value. The amount of capital whose interest, at the prevailing rate, would yield the net annual income actually received from the property. It is the value of the property on an income basis:

    capitalized value = net annual income × year's purchase year's purchase (perpetuity) = 100 / rate of interest = 1 / i net annual income = gross annual income − all outgoings

    For example, a property yielding a net income of Rs 1,20,000 a year, capitalised at 8%, has a capitalized value of 1,20,000 × (100/8) = 1,20,000 × 12.5 = Rs 15,00,000.

  6. Write short notes on (any three):
    • i) Contingency
    • ii) Bill of quantities
    • iii) Distress value
    • iv) Depreciation (Items i and ii also belong to Chapter 3.)
    [3×2] (2065 Shrawan, Q.2(b))
    Solution

    Answer any three. All four are given below.

    i) Contingency. A lump-sum provision, normally 3–5% of the estimated cost, added to every estimate to meet unforeseen and petty expenses and minor omissions that cannot be itemised at the time of estimating — small changes at site, small variations in rates, incidental charges. It is not meant to pay for a change in the scope of the work, which requires a supplementary or revised estimate, and it can be spent only with the sanction of the competent authority.

    ii) Bill of quantities. A tabulated schedule of every item of work in a project, giving a brief description, the estimated quantity, the unit, the rate and the amount. It is issued with the tender documents so that all contractors price the same quantities and their offers can be compared item by item, and it is afterwards used to measure and pay for the work actually executed and to value variations.

    iii) Distress value. The value that a property fetches when it is sold under compulsion or unfavourable conditions instead of in an open, willing market — for instance in a forced auction to recover a debt, in an urgent sale to raise money, during a partition dispute, or where the title is doubtful or the property is encumbered. It is always appreciably lower than the fair market value, typically by 15–30%, because the seller has neither the time nor the freedom to wait for the right purchaser.

    iv) Depreciation.

    Depreciation. The gradual loss in the value of a property due to its use, wear and tear, decay and age. It is a physical deterioration, and it is recovered by charging a part of the cost against the income each year.

    1. Straight line method — equal amount every year:
    D = ( C − S ) / n per year value after m years = C − m × D C = original cost, S = scrap value, n = useful life in years

    2. Sinking fund method — depreciation is the annual sinking fund instalment plus interest.
    3. Quantity survey method — the property is examined in detail and the loss in each part is assessed. The most accurate and the most laborious.

Cost, price and value

TermMeaning
CostThe actual expenditure incurred in producing or constructing the property — materials, labour, plant, overheads.
PriceThe cost plus the producer's profit — what is actually paid in a transaction.
ValueThe present worth of the property to its owner, depending on its utility, condition, location and the demand for it.

Cost and value are independent. A house that cost Rs 1 crore to build in a remote village may be worth Rs 40 lakh; an old house in central Kathmandu that cost Rs 5 lakh decades ago may be worth Rs 5 crore because of the land beneath it.

    Value of the property = Rs 17,21,042.53; the amount recommended for advancing as a loan against the mortgage ≈ Rs 11,47,400 (two-thirds of the valuation).

    The rate of interest, the redemption rate and the total life are not given in this question, so they must be assumed and the assumption stated. The cubic content of 1,600 m³ would be used if the building were valued by the cube rate method; here the rental method is used, so it is not needed.

    Building built 20 years ago, plinth area 500 m²
    Land area 1,000 m², current market value Rs 25,000 per m²
    Rate of building = Rs 50,000 per m²
    Assumed: life of the building 70 years, scrap value 10%, straight line depreciation

    Value of the land

    land = 1,000 m² × Rs 25,000 = Rs 2,50,00,000

    Replacement cost of the building

    Depreciation — straight line method

    scrap value S = 10% of 2,50,00,000 = Rs 25,00,000 D = ( C − S ) / n = ( 2,50,00,000 − 25,00,000 ) / 70 = 2,25,00,000 / 70 = Rs 3,21,428.57 per year depreciation in 20 years = 3,21,428.57 × 20 = Rs 64,28,571.43

    ENCE 351 §7.4.

Methods of valuation

  1. Rental method (capitalization of net rent). The net annual rental income is found by deducting outgoings from the gross rent, and multiplied by the year's purchase. Suitable where reliable rents exist and rent control does not distort them.
    capitalized value = net annual income × YP
  2. Direct comparison with the capital value. The property is compared with recent sales of similar properties nearby, with adjustments for differences in size, frontage, age and condition. The usual method for land.
  3. Valuation based on profit. The net profit the property generates is capitalized. Used for commercial properties whose value derives from the business — hotels, cinemas, restaurants, petrol pumps.
  4. Valuation based on cost (cost method). The actual cost of construction is worked out and depreciation deducted for age and condition. The usual method for a building, and the one used for insurance.
    value = replacement cost − depreciation ( + present value of the land )
  5. Development method. The value of land with development potential is found from the value of the finished scheme less the cost of developing it. Used for large plots ripe for building, and for a building to be converted.
  6. Depreciation method of valuation. The building is divided into four parts — walls, roof, floors and finishings — and each is depreciated according to its own life and condition before the values are added.

Valuation of land

  • Comparative method — from recent transactions of comparable plots.
  • Belting method — a deep plot is divided into belts parallel to the road; the front belt takes the full rate, the second belt about ½ and the rear belt about ⅓ of it, because frontage carries the value.
  • Hypothetical building scheme — the land is valued from what a building erected on it could earn.

Which method to use

Open landDirect comparison; belting for deep plots
Residential buildingCost method (replacement less depreciation) plus land value
Rented / tenanted propertyRental method — capitalization of net income

    1. Title and reference — who ordered the valuation, and for what purpose (sale, loan, tax, acquisition).
    2. Particulars of the property — owner, plot number, area, location, boundaries.
    3. Title and tenure — ownership documents examined, tenure, and any encumbrance.

    ENCE 351 §7.5.

A valuation report is the valuer's signed statement of the value of a property, with the reasoning that supports it. It has to stand up in a bank, a tax office or a court, so its form is fairly standard.

Components of a valuation report

  1. Introduction — who instructed the valuation, the purpose for which it is made, and the date of valuation.
  2. Identification of the property — owner's name, plot / kitta number, ward, municipality or VDC, district, and boundaries on all four sides.
  3. Legal particulars — title deed, tenure, encumbrances, tenancy, litigation.
  4. Description of the land — area, shape, frontage, level, approach road and its width, land use permitted.
  5. Description of the building — year of construction, number of storeys, plinth area of each floor, structural system, specification of foundation, walls, roof, floors, doors and windows, finishes, and the services provided.
  6. Present condition and the estimated remaining life; observed defects.
  7. Method of valuation adopted, with reasons, and the rates assumed with their source.
  8. Detailed calculation — land value, replacement cost of the building, depreciation, and the value of any other improvements (boundary wall, gate, well, landscaping).
  9. Summary of value and the valuer's opinion of the fair market value, in figures and in words.
  10. Declaration and signature of the valuer with registration number.
  11. Annexures — site plan, floor plans, photographs, copy of the title deed and of the approved drawing.

The valuation must state clearly that it is valid as on a particular date and for a particular purpose. A valuation made for a bank mortgage cannot be reused for an insurance claim.
