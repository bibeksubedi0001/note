8.1 Purpose of specifications

    The second part of this question also belongs to Section 8.4.
    Solution pending.

A drawing shows the shape, size and position of the work. It cannot show the quality — what grade of cement, what class of brick, how many coats of paint, how long to cure. That is what the specification supplies. Drawing and specification together define the work completely; neither is sufficient alone.

Purpose of specifications

  1. To describe the nature, quality and class of materials and the standard of workmanship required.
  2. To make the rate meaningful — a rate for brickwork means nothing until the mortar mix and class of brick are stated.
  3. To enable a fair comparison of tenders, because every bidder prices the same thing.
  4. To serve as an instruction to the contractor on how the work is to be executed.
  5. To give the engineer a basis for inspection, testing and rejection of sub-standard work.
  6. To form part of the legal contract, so that disputes can be settled by reference to it.
  7. To fix the mode of measurement and hence the basis of payment.

Chakraborti, Estimating, Costing and Specification ch.XIII; Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual ch.8.

The two types

General (brief) specificationDetailed specification
What it givesA short, general description of the class of work in each trade — enough to form a general idea of the building.A full description of each item: the materials, their proportions and quality, the method of preparation and execution, the workmanship, and the mode of measurement.
LengthA paragraph per tradeA page or more per item
Used forApproximate estimates; giving the client an idea of the class of buildingDetailed estimates, tender documents and execution
Legal statusNot usually a contract documentPart of the contract document
Example"Foundation: cement concrete 1:4:8 over well-consolidated earth. Superstructure: first-class brickwork in 1:6 cement mortar. Roof: RCC slab with weathering course.""Brickwork shall be of first-class bricks of crushing strength not less than … laid in English bond in cement mortar 1:6, joints not exceeding 10 mm, raked and cured for 10 days …"

Specifications are also classified by how they prescribe: a material and method (prescriptive) specification states exactly what to use and how; a performance specification states the result required — a concrete of 25 N/mm² at 28 days — and leaves the means to the contractor.

Importance of specifications

  1. The cost depends on it. The same item can vary in price several fold depending on the specification; without it no rate can be analysed.
  2. Tenders become comparable. Every bidder quotes for the same quality, so the lowest bid is genuinely the lowest.
  3. Quality is enforceable. The engineer can reject work by pointing to a clause, rather than arguing about opinion.
  4. Disputes are reduced, and those that arise can be settled by reading the document.
  5. It is a legal document forming part of the contract, admissible in arbitration and in court.
  6. It guides execution — the site engineer and the contractor's foreman both work to the same written standard.
  7. It protects the client from substitution of inferior materials.

Chakraborti, Estimating, Costing and Specification ch.XIII.

How a specification is written

Each item is written under a standard sequence of headings. Keeping to the sequence is what makes a specification complete:

  1. Materials — the class, source, grade and the relevant IS or NS code; the tests the material must pass.
  2. Proportion of the mix, and how it is to be measured (by volume in gauge boxes, or by weight).
  3. Preparation — storing and handling of materials, mixing, and preparation of the surface or bed.
  4. Workmanship — how the item is to be laid, bonded, compacted, finished and jointed; the tolerances allowed.
  5. Curing and protection — how long, and by what means.
  6. Tests and acceptance — what is to be tested, how often, and the acceptance criteria.
  7. Mode of measurement and payment — the unit, what is included in the rate, and what is deducted.

Rules for good drafting

  • Use simple, unambiguous language; avoid words like "good", "proper" or "best" that cannot be measured.
  • Describe the work in the order of construction — foundation upward.
  • Give quantities and figures wherever possible: mix, thickness, number of coats, days of curing.
  • Refer to standard codes rather than repeating them.
  • Do not contradict the drawing; state which prevails in case of conflict.
  • Do not repeat the same requirement in two places — it will eventually be amended in only one.
  • Cover every item in the bill of quantities; an unspecified item is an invitation to a claim.

Chakraborti, Estimating, Costing and Specification ch.XIII; the drafting rules follow standard departmental practice.

Site works

Site clearance. The site shall be cleared of all trees, bushes, stumps, roots, rubbish and structures. Stumps and roots shall be grubbed out to the full depth and the hollows filled and consolidated. Useful material shall be stacked as directed; the rest removed from site.

Earthwork in excavation. Excavation shall be carried out to the lines, levels and dimensions shown, in the class of soil described. Sides shall be kept vertical and shored where necessary; the bed shall be levelled, watered and well rammed. Excavated earth shall be deposited at least 1 m clear of the edge. Water met with shall be bailed or pumped out. Excavation beyond the specified depth shall be made good with concrete at the contractor's cost. Measured in m³, separately for each class of soil and for work below water level.

Earth filling. Filling shall be in layers not exceeding 15–20 cm, each layer watered to optimum moisture and consolidated by rammer or roller before the next is laid. Filling shall be free from rubbish, roots and organic matter.

Structural works

Plain cement concrete. Cement shall conform to the relevant standard and be stored clear of the ground and damp. Sand shall be clean, sharp, free from clay, silt and organic matter. Coarse aggregate shall be hard, dense, well graded, of the nominal size specified. Materials shall be measured by volume in gauge boxes, the cement by bag. Mixing shall be in a mechanical mixer for not less than 2 minutes; hand mixing on a watertight platform only where permitted, with 10% extra cement. Concrete shall be placed within 30 minutes of adding water, in layers not exceeding 15 cm, and compacted by vibrator. It shall be cured for at least 7 days for PCC. Measured in m³.

Reinforced cement concrete. As above, with the mix and grade specified. Formwork shall be rigid, true to line and level, and tight against leakage of grout; it shall not be struck before the periods specified (sides 24–48 hours, slab soffit 7 days, beam soffit 14–21 days). Reinforcement shall be free from loose rust, oil and grease, cut and bent cold to the shapes shown, and placed with the specified cover maintained by cover blocks. Laps and hooks shall be as detailed. Concrete shall be cured for at least 14 days. Concrete measured in m³, formwork in m², reinforcement in kg.

Brick work. Bricks shall be first class, well burnt, of uniform colour, regular in shape, emitting a clear ringing sound when struck, with water absorption not exceeding 20% and the crushing strength specified. Bricks shall be soaked in water for at least one hour before use. Brickwork shall be laid in English bond unless otherwise specified, in the mortar mix stated, with joints not exceeding 10 mm, fully filled. No part shall be raised more than 1 m above another. Joints shall be raked where the surface is to be plastered. Work shall be kept wet and cured for 10 days. Measured in m³.

Stone masonry. Stone shall be hard, durable, free from cracks and weathering. Random rubble shall be laid on its natural bed, well bonded, with through (bond) stones at not more than 1.5 m apart in every course. Hearting shall be well packed with spalls and mortar; no dry packing. Measured in m³.

Damp proof course. DPC shall be of cement concrete of the mix specified with an approved waterproofing compound, laid over the full width of the wall at plinth level, with the top surface left rough for the masonry above. Measured in m².

Finishing works

Cement plaster. The surface shall be raked, cleaned and thoroughly wetted. Plaster shall be of the mix and thickness specified, applied in one or two coats, finished true and even with a wooden float, with corners and junctions truly vertical and rounded where directed. It shall be cured for 7 days. Measured in m²; deductions as in §1.4.

Pointing. Joints shall be raked out to a depth of 20 mm, cleaned and wetted; mortar pressed into the joints and finished flush, ruled, or weathered as specified. Cured for 7 days. Measured in m².

Flooring. The base shall be prepared, watered and consolidated. Concrete or tiles shall be laid to the thickness and pattern specified, true to level and slope, joints close and even, and the surface finished as directed. Marble and mosaic floors shall be ground and polished in the number of cuts specified. Measured in m².

Wood work. Timber shall be well seasoned, of the species specified, free from large or loose knots, sapwood, shakes and warping, with moisture content not exceeding that stated. Joints shall be true and close; frames shall be given one coat of primer before fixing and embedded with hold-fasts. Frames in m³, shutters in m².

Painting. The surface shall be cleaned, rubbed down and made free from dust and grease; knots sealed and the surface primed and stopped. Paint of the approved brand and shade shall be applied in the number of coats specified, each coat allowed to dry hard before the next. The finished surface shall be uniform and free from brush marks. Measured in m², with the multiplying factors of §1.4.

White washing and distempering. The surface shall be cleaned of dirt and loose material and any defects made good. White wash shall be of fresh, well-slaked fat lime, applied in the number of coats specified, each coat covering the surface uniformly. Measured in m².
