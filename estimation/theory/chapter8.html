8.1 Purpose of specifications

Past Exam Questions on this Topic

  1. State the purpose of specification and write the techniques of specification writing. [equal marks] (CM 2072 Ashwin, Q.7(a))
    The second part of this question also belongs to Section 8.4.
    Solution

    Specification. A written statement describing in detail the quality of the materials to be used, the workmanship expected, the method of doing the work and the method of its measurement and payment. The drawing shows the size and shape of the work; the specification states its quality. Only together do the two define the work completely.

    Purpose / role of specifications

    1. To describe the quality and class of materials and the workmanship required, which a drawing cannot show.
    2. To form part of the contract document, and so be legally binding on both parties.
    3. To provide the basis for the analysis of rates — a rate cannot be worked out until the item is defined.
    4. To guide the contractor in executing the work and the engineer in supervising and accepting it.
    5. To put every tenderer on the same footing, so that quoted rates are comparable.
    6. To fix the method of measurement and payment of each item.
    7. To prevent disputes, by removing ambiguity about what was intended.
    8. To lay down the tests to be carried out and the standards to be met.
    9. To specify the equipment, tools and plant to be engaged on the work, so that they can be arranged beforehand.
    10. To fix the responsibility of the contractor for quality and for defects.

    Techniques / principles of writing a specification

    1. Be clear and unambiguous. Use simple, direct language; one sentence should carry one requirement. Avoid words such as "good", "suitable", "proper", "best quality" — they are unenforceable.
    2. Be complete. Cover material, proportion, workmanship, method, tolerance, testing, curing, protection, and the mode of measurement and payment.
    3. Be brief. Say a thing once. Repetition creates contradiction.
    4. Use the imperative or the word "shall" — "bricks shall be first class". "Should" and "may" are advisory and unenforceable.
    5. Follow the sequence of construction — materials first, then workmanship, then measurement and payment.
    6. Refer to standards (IS / NS codes) rather than reproducing them, but quote the number and the year.
    7. Do not contradict the drawing or the conditions of contract; if there is a conflict, state which prevails.
    8. Quantify wherever possible — give numbers, tolerances and limits, not adjectives. "Joints not exceeding 10 mm", not "thin joints".
    9. Do not specify the impossible or the unavailable; the specification must be practicable at that site.
    10. Do not encroach on the contractor's means unless the method genuinely matters — over-specifying transfers the risk back to the owner.
    11. Write in a standard order and number the clauses, so that they can be referred to.
    12. Check it against the bill of quantities, so that every item priced is specified and every item specified is priced.

    ENCE 351 §8.1 and §8.4.

A drawing shows the shape, size and position of the work. It cannot show the quality — what grade of cement, what class of brick, how many coats of paint, how long to cure. That is what the specification supplies. Drawing and specification together define the work completely; neither is sufficient alone.

Purpose of specifications

  1. To describe the nature, quality and class of materials and the standard of workmanship required.
  2. To make the rate meaningful — a rate for brickwork means nothing until the mortar mix and class of brick are stated.
  3. To enable a fair comparison of tenders, because every bidder prices the same thing.
  4. To serve as an instruction to the contractor on how the work is to be executed.
  5. To give the engineer a basis for inspection, testing and rejection of sub-standard work.
  6. To form part of the legal contract, so that disputes can be settled by reference to it.
  7. To fix the mode of measurement and hence the basis of payment.

  3. Define and differentiate between general and detailed specification. Write a detailed specification of cement plaster of 1:4 in brick wall. [2+4] (CM 2077 Chaitra, Q.7)
    The second part of this question also belongs to Section 8.5.
    Solution

    a) General and detailed specification defined and distinguished

    General specification gives a brief, overall description of the class and nature of the work as a whole, without going into individual items. It conveys the general idea of the building and is used in the report and for approximate estimates.

    Detailed specification describes each item of work separately and in full — the materials, their proportions, the workmanship, the method of execution, the curing, the tests and the mode of measurement and payment. It forms part of the contract document and governs execution and payment.

    b) Detailed specification of 1:4 cement plaster on brick wall

    Detailed specification — 12 mm cement plaster 1:4 on brick wall

    1. Scope. Twelve millimetre thick cement plaster in cement sand mortar 1:4 on internal or external brick wall surfaces.

    2. Materials. Cement shall be ordinary Portland cement conforming to the relevant IS / NS standard, fresh and free from lumps. Sand shall be clean, sharp, well-graded river sand passing a 2.36 mm sieve, free from clay, silt, salt and organic matter, with silt content not exceeding 4%; it shall be screened and washed if necessary. Water shall be clean and fit for drinking.

    3. Mortar. Cement and sand shall be measured by standard boxes in the proportion 1:4, mixed dry on a clean watertight platform until uniform in colour, then mixed with the minimum water needed for workability. Only as much mortar as can be used within 30 minutes shall be prepared; re-tempering is not permitted.

    4. Preparation of the surface

    • All joints of the brickwork shall be raked out to a depth of 12–15 mm while the mortar is green, to provide a key.
    • The surface shall be brushed clean of dust, loose mortar, efflorescence, oil and grease, and any projecting burr removed.
    • The surface shall be thoroughly wetted and kept damp for at least 6 hours before plastering, but shall not be soaking wet at the moment of application.
    • Any unevenness exceeding 12 mm shall be made good in advance with mortar; plaster shall not be used to correct bad brickwork.

    5. Application

    • Screeds (gauge patches) about 15 × 15 cm shall be applied at not more than 2 m apart, horizontally and vertically, to fix the thickness and the plane.
    • Mortar shall be applied with a trowel in a single coat 12 mm thick, forced well into the joints, and brought to a true surface by working a wooden straight edge over the screeds with a small upward and sideways motion.
    • The surface shall then be finished with a wooden float to a true, even and smooth surface, free from cracks and undulations. A steel trowel finish shall be used only where specified.
    • Plastering shall be started from the top and worked downwards.
    • Work shall be closed at the end of a wall or at a natural break, never in the middle of a panel, and the day's joint shall not be visible.
    • All corners, arrises and junctions shall be true, sharp and rounded as directed; internal corners shall not be rounded unless specified.

    6. Curing. Curing shall start 24 hours after the plaster is laid, once it has hardened sufficiently not to be damaged, and shall continue by keeping the surface constantly moist for at least 7 days. The plaster shall be protected from rain, direct sun and frost during this period.

    7. Defects. Plaster that sounds hollow when tapped, or shows cracks, blisters or efflorescence, shall be cut out in complete rectangular patches and redone at the contractor's cost.

    8. Measurement and payment. Plastering shall be measured in square metres (m²) of the finished surface, separately for each thickness and each mix, and separately for internal and external work. Deductions for openings shall follow IS 1200:

    Area of openingDeductionJambs and soffits
    up to 0.5 m²no deductionnot measured
    0.5 to 3 m²one face onlynot measured
    above 3 m²both facesmeasured and added

    No deduction shall be made for the ends of beams, posts or rafters. Payment shall be per m², the rate including materials, labour, scaffolding, curing and protection.

    Importance of specification in construction

    1. It completes the drawing. A drawing gives dimensions only. Two buildings of identical drawings can differ enormously in cost and quality; the specification is what distinguishes them.
    2. It is the legal backbone of the contract. In any dispute, the specification is the document to which both parties and the arbitrator turn.

    5. It is the standard of acceptance. The engineer accepts or rejects work by comparing it with the specification.
    6. It protects the owner against the use of inferior materials and poor workmanship.
    7. It protects the contractor too, by defining exactly what he is obliged to do — and no more.
    8. It fixes measurement and payment, and so avoids disputes over billing.
    9. It records the intention for future maintenance, repair and valuation.
    10. A tender paper without a specification is baseless — the contractor has nothing on which to build his rate, and the owner nothing against which to judge it.

    b) Detailed specification for brick work in 1:4 cement sand mortar

    Detailed specification — first class brickwork in cement sand mortar

    1. Scope. This specification covers first class brickwork in walls, piers and similar work, laid in cement sand mortar of the proportion stated in the item.

    2. Materials

    • Bricks shall be first class, table moulded, thoroughly burnt in a kiln, of uniform deep red, cherry or copper colour, free from cracks, chips, flaws and lumps of lime or organic matter. They shall have sharp, straight and square edges, plane faces and uniform size, and shall give a clear metallic ringing sound when struck against one another. The minimum crushing strength shall be 105 kg/cm² (10.5 N/mm²), and a dry brick shall not absorb more than one-sixth of its weight after one hour's immersion (about 20% of the dry weight after 24 hours). Bricks shall not break when dropped flat on hard ground from about 1 m.
    • Cement shall be ordinary Portland cement conforming to the relevant IS / NS standard, fresh, free from lumps, and stored clear of the ground and damp.
    • Sand shall be clean, sharp, coarse river sand, free from clay, silt, salt and organic matter; silt content not more than 4%. It shall be screened and washed if necessary.
    • Water shall be clean and fit for drinking, free from oil, acid, alkali and organic matter.

    3. Mortar. Cement and sand shall be measured by boxes of standard size in the proportion stated in the item, mixed dry on a clean watertight platform until the mixture is of uniform colour, and then mixed with the minimum water necessary to give a workable consistency. Only so much mortar shall be prepared as can be used within 30 minutes of adding water. Mortar left unused beyond its initial setting time shall be rejected; re-tempering is not permitted.

    4. Soaking. Bricks shall be fully soaked in clean water for at least two hours — and for first class work four hours — before use, stacked on clean wooden planks clear of mud, and allowed to become surface-dry before laying, so that they neither absorb water from the mortar nor float on it.

    5. Workmanship

    • Brickwork shall be laid in English bond unless otherwise specified, this being the strongest bond, with alternate courses of headers and stretchers and a queen closer next to the quoin header.
    • Bricks shall be laid with their frogs upward, on a full bed of mortar, and well pressed so that the mortar is squeezed out and the joint is completely filled.
    • Joints shall be full and shall not exceed 6 mm in cement mortar or 10 mm in lime mortar. Vertical joints must break — that is, they shall not come one above the other in consecutive courses.
    • No bats shall be used except where they are absolutely required to obtain the specified bond or the dimensions of a course.
    • Each course shall be truly horizontal and the face truly vertical, checked with a spirit level and plumb bob. The work shall be taken up in level courses all round.
    • The height raised in one day shall not exceed 1.0 m, to avoid unequal settlement of the fresh work.
    • Where the work has to be left, it shall be stepped or raked back at an angle not steeper than 45°; toothing shall not be allowed.
    • The face of the wall shall be kept clean of mortar droppings. Where the surface is to be plastered, the joints shall be raked out to a depth of 12–15 mm while the mortar is green, to give a key to the plaster; where it is to be pointed, the joints shall be raked as required for pointing.
    • Openings, chases, holdfasts and embedded fixtures shall be provided as the work proceeds; cutting afterwards is not permitted.

    6. Curing. The work shall be kept constantly moist on all faces for at least 10 to 14 days, by sprinkling water at least twice a day and by covering with wet gunny bags. At the end of each day's work a trough at least 2.5 cm deep shall be formed on the top of the wall with weak mortar or mud edging and kept flooded with water. Freshly laid work shall be protected from rain, sun and frost; during rain no brickwork shall be carried out unless it can be protected for 24 hours.

    7. Scaffolding. Single scaffolding may be used for ordinary work; double scaffolding shall be used for important work and where the wall face is to be exposed, so that no hole is left in the finished face.

    8. Measurement and payment. Brickwork shall be measured in cubic metres (m³), the dimensions being taken to the nearest 0.01 m. No deduction is made for the bearings of lintels and beams up to 0.1 m³ in volume, nor for openings up to 0.1 m² in area. Brickwork of each thickness, each class of brick and each mortar mix shall be measured separately, and separately in foundation, in plinth and in superstructure. Payment shall be made per m³ at the rate quoted, which shall include all materials, labour, scaffolding, curing and protection.

    The mix here is 1:4, which is richer than the 1:6 normally used for ordinary walls; it is specified for load-bearing walls, for work below plinth level and in wet situations. For 1 m³ of wet 1:4 mortar the quantities are cement 1.30 × (1/5) = 0.26 m³ = 374.4 kg = 7.49 bags, and sand 1.30 × (4/5) = 1.04 m³.

    9. Do not specify the impossible or the unavailable; the specification must be practicable at that site.
    10. Do not encroach on the contractor's means unless the method genuinely matters — over-specifying transfers the risk back to the owner.
    11. Write in a standard order and number the clauses, so that they can be referred to.
    12. Check it against the bill of quantities, so that every item priced is specified and every item specified is priced.

    b) (i) Detailed specification of RCC with M20 concrete in columns

    Detailed specification — RCC with M20 concrete in columns

    1. Scope. Reinforced cement concrete of grade M20 (nominal mix 1:1½:3) in columns, including formwork, placing, compaction, finishing and curing. Reinforcement is measured and paid separately.

    2. Materials

    • Cement — ordinary Portland cement conforming to the relevant IS / NS standard, fresh and free from lumps.
    • Fine aggregate — clean, sharp river sand, free from clay, silt, salt and organic matter, silt content not exceeding 4%, conforming to grading zone II or III.
    • Coarse aggregate — hard, dense, durable crushed stone, angular, free from dust, flaky and elongated particles, of nominal maximum size 20 mm (and not exceeding one-fourth of the least dimension of the member, nor the clear cover, nor the minimum clear spacing between bars).
    • Water — clean and fit for drinking; pH not less than 6.
    • Steel — deformed bars of the specified grade, free from rust, scale, oil and grease.

    3. Proportioning and mixing. Cement, fine and coarse aggregate shall be proportioned 1 : 1½ : 3 by volume using standard boxes, cement being taken by the bag. The water cement ratio shall not exceed 0.50, and shall be adjusted for the moisture in the aggregate. Mixing shall be done in a mechanical mixer for not less than 2 minutes after all the materials are in, until the mix is uniform in colour and consistency. Hand mixing is permitted only for small quantities and with 10% extra cement. The slump shall be 25–75 mm for columns.

    4. Formwork. Formwork shall be of steel or well-seasoned timber, sufficiently rigid to retain its shape, with tight joints to prevent leakage of grout. It shall be truly vertical, properly braced and supported, and its inner surface treated with an approved mould oil before the reinforcement is placed. Formwork to the sides of columns shall not be removed earlier than 24–48 hours after casting.

    5. Reinforcement. Bars shall be cut, bent and fixed strictly to the bar bending schedule, tied at every intersection with 18 SWG annealed binding wire, and held in position by cover blocks of the same mix. The clear cover to the main reinforcement in a column shall be 40 mm, and not less than the diameter of the bar. Laps shall be staggered and shall be as specified; laps in adjacent bars shall not occur at the same section.

    6. Placing and compaction

    • Concrete shall be placed within 30 minutes of mixing and before the initial set has begun.
    • It shall be deposited as near as possible to its final position, and shall not be dropped from a height exceeding 1.5 m, to prevent segregation.
    • It shall be placed in horizontal layers not exceeding 300 mm and compacted with a needle (immersion) vibrator until air bubbles cease to rise and the surface shows a thin film of mortar. Over-vibration causing segregation shall be avoided.
    • Concreting of a column shall be carried out in one continuous operation up to the underside of the beam. Where a construction joint is unavoidable, it shall be made at a position approved by the engineer, roughened, cleaned of laitance and wetted, and a coat of cement slurry applied before fresh concrete is placed.

    7. Curing. Curing shall begin as soon as the concrete has hardened sufficiently, and shall continue for at least 14 days by wrapping with wet gunny bags or hessian kept continuously moist. The concrete shall be protected from rain, direct sun, running water and mechanical damage during this period.

    8. Tests. Cube tests shall be carried out on 150 mm cubes as specified; the characteristic compressive strength at 28 days shall be 20 N/mm². Slump shall be tested at the point of placing. The frequency of sampling shall be as required by the code.

    9. Measurement and payment. RCC work shall be measured in cubic metres (m³) of the finished concrete. No deduction is made for the volume of the reinforcement, nor for openings up to 0.1 m² or embedded pipes up to 100 cm² in sectional area. Formwork is measured separately in m² of the surface in contact with the concrete, and reinforcement separately in kg (weight per metre = d²/162). Payment shall be per m³ at the rate quoted, which includes materials, mixing, placing, compaction, finishing and curing.

    b) (ii) Detailed specification of brick work in 1:6 cement sand mortar

    Detailed specification — first class brickwork in cement sand mortar

    1. Scope. This specification covers first class brickwork in walls, piers and similar work, laid in cement sand mortar of the proportion stated in the item.

    2. Materials

    • Bricks shall be first class, table moulded, thoroughly burnt in a kiln, of uniform deep red, cherry or copper colour, free from cracks, chips, flaws and lumps of lime or organic matter. They shall have sharp, straight and square edges, plane faces and uniform size, and shall give a clear metallic ringing sound when struck against one another. The minimum crushing strength shall be 105 kg/cm² (10.5 N/mm²), and a dry brick shall not absorb more than one-sixth of its weight after one hour's immersion (about 20% of the dry weight after 24 hours). Bricks shall not break when dropped flat on hard ground from about 1 m.
    • Cement shall be ordinary Portland cement conforming to the relevant IS / NS standard, fresh, free from lumps, and stored clear of the ground and damp.
    • Sand shall be clean, sharp, coarse river sand, free from clay, silt, salt and organic matter; silt content not more than 4%. It shall be screened and washed if necessary.
    • Water shall be clean and fit for drinking, free from oil, acid, alkali and organic matter.

    3. Mortar. Cement and sand shall be measured by boxes of standard size in the proportion stated in the item, mixed dry on a clean watertight platform until the mixture is of uniform colour, and then mixed with the minimum water necessary to give a workable consistency. Only so much mortar shall be prepared as can be used within 30 minutes of adding water. Mortar left unused beyond its initial setting time shall be rejected; re-tempering is not permitted.

    4. Soaking. Bricks shall be fully soaked in clean water for at least two hours — and for first class work four hours — before use, stacked on clean wooden planks clear of mud, and allowed to become surface-dry before laying, so that they neither absorb water from the mortar nor float on it.

    5. Workmanship

    • Brickwork shall be laid in English bond unless otherwise specified, this being the strongest bond, with alternate courses of headers and stretchers and a queen closer next to the quoin header.
    • Bricks shall be laid with their frogs upward, on a full bed of mortar, and well pressed so that the mortar is squeezed out and the joint is completely filled.
    • Joints shall be full and shall not exceed 6 mm in cement mortar or 10 mm in lime mortar. Vertical joints must break — that is, they shall not come one above the other in consecutive courses.
    • No bats shall be used except where they are absolutely required to obtain the specified bond or the dimensions of a course.
    • Each course shall be truly horizontal and the face truly vertical, checked with a spirit level and plumb bob. The work shall be taken up in level courses all round.
    • The height raised in one day shall not exceed 1.0 m, to avoid unequal settlement of the fresh work.
    • Where the work has to be left, it shall be stepped or raked back at an angle not steeper than 45°; toothing shall not be allowed.
    • The face of the wall shall be kept clean of mortar droppings. Where the surface is to be plastered, the joints shall be raked out to a depth of 12–15 mm while the mortar is green, to give a key to the plaster; where it is to be pointed, the joints shall be raked as required for pointing.
    • Openings, chases, holdfasts and embedded fixtures shall be provided as the work proceeds; cutting afterwards is not permitted.

    6. Curing. The work shall be kept constantly moist on all faces for at least 10 to 14 days, by sprinkling water at least twice a day and by covering with wet gunny bags. At the end of each day's work a trough at least 2.5 cm deep shall be formed on the top of the wall with weak mortar or mud edging and kept flooded with water. Freshly laid work shall be protected from rain, sun and frost; during rain no brickwork shall be carried out unless it can be protected for 24 hours.

    7. Scaffolding. Single scaffolding may be used for ordinary work; double scaffolding shall be used for important work and where the wall face is to be exposed, so that no hole is left in the finished face.

    8. Measurement and payment. Brickwork shall be measured in cubic metres (m³), the dimensions being taken to the nearest 0.01 m. No deduction is made for the bearings of lintels and beams up to 0.1 m³ in volume, nor for openings up to 0.1 m² in area. Brickwork of each thickness, each class of brick and each mortar mix shall be measured separately, and separately in foundation, in plinth and in superstructure. Payment shall be made per m³ at the rate quoted, which shall include all materials, labour, scaffolding, curing and protection.

How a specification is written

Each item is written under a standard sequence of headings. Keeping to the sequence is what makes a specification complete:

  1. Materials — the class, source, grade and the relevant IS or NS code; the tests the material must pass.
  2. Proportion of the mix, and how it is to be measured (by volume in gauge boxes, or by weight).
  3. Preparation — storing and handling of materials, mixing, and preparation of the surface or bed.
  4. Workmanship — how the item is to be laid, bonded, compacted, finished and jointed; the tolerances allowed.
  5. Curing and protection — how long, and by what means.
  6. Tests and acceptance — what is to be tested, how often, and the acceptance criteria.
  7. Mode of measurement and payment — the unit, what is included in the rate, and what is deducted.

Rules for good drafting

  • Use simple, unambiguous language; avoid words like "good", "proper" or "best" that cannot be measured.
  • Describe the work in the order of construction — foundation upward.
  • Give quantities and figures wherever possible: mix, thickness, number of coats, days of curing.
  • Refer to standard codes rather than repeating them.
  • Do not contradict the drawing; state which prevails in case of conflict.
  • Do not repeat the same requirement in two places — it will eventually be amended in only one.
  • Cover every item in the bill of quantities; an unspecified item is an invitation to a claim.

    a) Importance of specification in construction work

    Importance of specification in construction

    1. It completes the drawing. A drawing gives dimensions only. Two buildings of identical drawings can differ enormously in cost and quality; the specification is what distinguishes them.
    2. It is the legal backbone of the contract. In any dispute, the specification is the document to which both parties and the arbitrator turn.
    3. It governs the rate. The analysis of rates begins from the specification; change the mix from 1:2:4 to 1:1½:3 and the rate changes at once.
    4. It ensures fair competition. All tenderers price the same quality, so the lowest bid is genuinely the lowest.
    5. It is the standard of acceptance. The engineer accepts or rejects work by comparing it with the specification.
    6. It protects the owner against the use of inferior materials and poor workmanship.
    7. It protects the contractor too, by defining exactly what he is obliged to do — and no more.
    8. It fixes measurement and payment, and so avoids disputes over billing.
    9. It records the intention for future maintenance, repair and valuation.
    10. A tender paper without a specification is baseless — the contractor has nothing on which to build his rate, and the owner nothing against which to judge it.

Site works

Site clearance. The site shall be cleared of all trees, bushes, stumps, roots, rubbish and structures. Stumps and roots shall be grubbed out to the full depth and the hollows filled and consolidated. Useful material shall be stacked as directed; the rest removed from site.

Earthwork in excavation. Excavation shall be carried out to the lines, levels and dimensions shown, in the class of soil described. Sides shall be kept vertical and shored where necessary; the bed shall be levelled, watered and well rammed. Excavated earth shall be deposited at least 1 m clear of the edge. Water met with shall be bailed or pumped out. Excavation beyond the specified depth shall be made good with concrete at the contractor's cost. Measured in m³, separately for each class of soil and for work below water level.

Earth filling. Filling shall be in layers not exceeding 15–20 cm, each layer watered to optimum moisture and consolidated by rammer or roller before the next is laid. Filling shall be free from rubbish, roots and organic matter.

Structural works

Plain cement concrete. Cement shall conform to the relevant standard and be stored clear of the ground and damp. Sand shall be clean, sharp, free from clay, silt and organic matter. Mixing shall be in a mechanical mixer for not less than 2 minutes; hand mixing on a watertight platform only where permitted, with 10% extra cement. Concrete shall be placed within 30 minutes of adding water, in layers not exceeding 15 cm, and compacted by vibrator. It shall be cured for at least 7 days for PCC. Measured in m³.

Formwork shall be rigid, true to line and level, and tight against leakage of grout; it shall not be struck before the periods specified (sides 24–48 hours, slab soffit 7 days, beam soffit 14–21 days). Cement plaster. The surface shall be raked, cleaned and thoroughly wetted. Plaster shall be of the mix and thickness specified, applied in one or two coats, finished true and even with a wooden float, with corners and junctions truly vertical and rounded where directed. It shall be cured for 7 days. Measured in m²; deductions as in §1.4.

Pointing. Joints shall be raked out to a depth of 20 mm, cleaned and wetted; mortar pressed into the joints and finished flush, ruled, or weathered as specified. Cured for 7 days. Measured in m².

Flooring. The base shall be prepared, watered and consolidated. Concrete or tiles shall be laid to the thickness and pattern specified, true to level and slope, joints close and even, and the surface finished as directed. Marble and mosaic floors shall be ground and polished in the number of cuts specified. Measured in m².

Wood work. Timber shall be well seasoned, of the species specified, free from large or loose knots, sapwood, shakes and warping, with moisture content not exceeding that stated. Joints shall be true and close; frames shall be given one coat of primer before fixing and embedded with hold-fasts. Frames in m³, shutters in m².

Painting. The surface shall be cleaned, rubbed down and made free from dust and grease; knots sealed and the surface primed and stopped. Paint of the approved brand and shade shall be applied in the number of coats specified, each coat allowed to dry hard before the next. The finished surface shall be uniform and free from brush marks. Measured in m², with the multiplying factors of §1.4.

White washing and distempering. The surface shall be cleaned of dirt and loose material and any defects made good. White wash shall be of fresh, well-slaked fat lime, applied in the number of coats specified, each coat covering the surface uniformly. Measured in m².
