An approximate estimate — also called a rough, preliminary or budgetary estimate — finds the probable cost of a work quickly, before the detailed drawings exist. It is prepared at the proposal stage so that the authority can decide whether the scheme is worth pursuing and how much money to provide.

When and why it is prepared

  • To decide the financial policy — can the department afford it?
  • To obtain administrative approval and provide for the work in the budget.
  • To compare alternative proposals and choose between them.
  • When there is no time or no detailed drawing for a full estimate.

It is supported by a report explaining the necessity of the work, a site or index plan, and the rate assumed per unit, with the basis on which that rate was taken.

Methods of preparing an approximate estimate

MethodBasisSuitable for
Plinth area methodPlinth area × plinth area rate. The plinth area is measured to the outer face of the walls at floor level.Buildings — the commonest method of all
Cubical content methodPlinth area × height × a rate per m³, the height measured from floor level to the top of the roof.Buildings where height matters — halls, cinemas, godowns. More accurate than plinth area.
Unit base (service unit) methodCost per functional unit × number of units.School per classroom or per student, hospital per bed, cinema per seat, irrigation per hectare, water tank per litre, road per kilometre
Approximate quantity methodThe work is divided into two blocks — foundation and superstructure — and the running metre of wall in each is priced.Boundary walls, small buildings; more accurate than the plinth area method
Bay methodCost of one bay × number of bays.Repetitive structures — sheds, barracks
Cost comparison with a similar structureThe known cost of a completed similar work, corrected for size, time and locality.Any work for which a close precedent exists
plinth area method : cost = plinth area (m²) × rate per m² cubical content : cost = plinth area × height × rate per m³ unit base method : cost = number of units × cost per unit

Accuracy improves down the table: plinth area is the crudest, approximate quantity the closest to a detailed estimate. Whichever is used, a contingency of 3–5% is added, and the estimate is expected to be within about 5–10% of the detailed figure.

The detailed estimate is the accurate estimate, prepared after the drawings and specifications are complete. Every item of work is measured from the drawings, entered in a measurement (quantity) sheet, priced at the rate obtained by analysis of rates, and totalled in an abstract of cost.

How it is prepared

  1. Study the drawings and specifications and list the items of work, grouped under sub-heads (§3.6).
  2. Take out the quantities of each item from the drawings by one of the methods of Chapter 2, entering number, length, breadth and depth in the measurement sheet.
  3. Analyse the rate of each item (§6) from the norms and the district rates.
  4. Abstract the cost — quantity × rate for every item, summed by sub-head.
  5. Add contingencies, work-charged establishment and any other allowances.
  6. Attach the report, detailed specifications, drawings, calculation sheets and the analysis of rates.

What a detailed estimate must contain

  • Report and general abstract of cost
  • Detailed measurement (quantity) sheet
  • Abstract of cost
  • Analysis of rates
  • General and detailed specifications
  • Complete set of drawings — plans, elevations, sections, details
  • Site (index) plan and design calculations

Contingencies and work-charged establishment

Two allowances are added at the end of every detailed estimate, and the examiners regularly ask for the difference:

ContingenciesWork-charged establishment
What it coversSmall unforeseen items and incidental expenses that cannot be foreseen at the time of estimatingThe pay of staff employed specifically to supervise this work — site engineer, overseer, work sarkar, chowkidar
Usual allowance3–5% of the estimated cost1.5–2% of the estimated cost
Charged toThe workThe work (not the department's regular establishment)

Contingencies are not the same as overhead cost. Contingencies are a provision inside the estimate for the unforeseen; overheads are the contractor's own cost of running a business — office rent, staff salaries, insurance, depreciation of plant — and are recovered inside the rate (§6), not added to the estimate.

Dawadi, chapter 3 estimating §3.2; Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual §3.1.4.

A revised estimate is a fresh detailed estimate prepared for a work already sanctioned, when the original sanction is no longer adequate.

When a revised estimate is required

  • When the expenditure exceeds, or is likely to exceed, the sanctioned amount by more than about 5%.
  • When the rates adopted have changed materially.
  • When there is a material deviation from the original proposal — a change of design, of specification, or of the structural system.
  • When the work is abandoned part way and the sanction has to be adjusted.

It is prepared in the same form as the original detailed estimate, but with a comparative statement showing, item by item, the original quantity and cost, the revised quantity and cost, the difference, and the reason for it.

Example. A school building is sanctioned at Rs 80 lakh. During construction the sub-soil proves weaker than assumed and the isolated footings must be replaced by a raft, while cement rates rise. The cost rises to Rs 92 lakh — 15% over sanction — so a revised estimate is prepared and fresh sanction obtained before the extra work proceeds.

Dawadi, chapter 3 estimating §3.2.1.

3.4 Supplementary estimate

A supplementary estimate is a detailed estimate for additional work found necessary while the original work is in progress. The original work is not disturbed; the new work is estimated separately and sanctioned in addition.

When it is required

  • When an altogether new item or new structure becomes necessary.
  • When further work is needed to make the original work fully useful.

It is prepared exactly like a detailed estimate, and its abstract shows both the amount of the original sanctioned estimate and the amount now proposed, so that the total commitment is visible.

Example. A hostel block is under construction to sanction. It is then decided that a separate generator room and an overhead water tank are needed for the block to function. These were never in the original estimate, so a supplementary estimate is prepared for them.

Revised or supplementary?

Revised estimateSupplementary estimate
ReasonThe same work now costs more, or the design has changedAdditional work is needed
Original estimateSuperseded by the revised oneStands; the new estimate is in addition
Presented withA comparative statement against the originalAn abstract showing both sanctions

3.5 Complete estimate2075

A complete estimate covers not only the structure itself but every cost that has to be incurred before the work can be used. An estimate limited to the building alone always understates the money required.

Components of a complete estimate

  1. Cost of land — purchase or acquisition, with compensation.
  2. Legal expenses — registration, stamp duty, land revenue, legal charges.
  3. Cost of the structure from the detailed estimate.
  4. Water supply and sanitary installation — usually taken at 8–10% of the building cost.
  5. Electrical installation — usually 8–10% of the building cost.
  6. Approach road, boundary wall, gate, landscaping and other site development.
  7. Architectural and consultancy fees, and supervision charges.
  8. Contingencies (3–5%) and work-charged establishment (1.5–2%).
  9. Furniture and equipment, where the building cannot function without it.

Related estimates

  • Annual repair and maintenance estimate — prepared each year to keep the property in proper condition (white washing, minor repairs, replacement of fittings). Usually 1–2% of the capital cost for a building.
  • Extension and improvement estimate — for enlarging or upgrading an existing property.
  • Maintenance and special repair estimate — for periodic heavy repairs beyond ordinary annual maintenance.

Dawadi, chapter 3 estimating §3.2.3–3.2.5; Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual §3.2.

A detailed estimate is presented in a fixed set of forms. Knowing which form does what — and how the bill of quantities differs from the abstract of cost — is worth several marks nearly every year.

1. Detail quantity (measurement) sheet

Where the quantities are taken out from the drawings. Its columns never change:

S.N.Description of itemNo.LengthBreadthHeight / depthQuantityRemarks
1Earthwork in excavation in foundation119.200.900.9015.55 m³
2PCC 1:3:6 in foundation119.200.900.101.73 m³

2. Analysis of rates

The rate of each item built up from materials, labour, plant, overheads and profit (§6).

3. Abstract of cost

Quantity × rate for every item, grouped by sub-head and totalled, with contingencies and work-charged establishment added at the foot.

S.N.Description of itemQuantityUnitRate (Rs)Amount (Rs)
1Earthwork in excavation in foundation15.55m³425.006 608.75

Total22 092.25

Grand total23 196.87

4. Bill of quantities (BOQ)

The tender document: the same list of items with their quantities and units, but with the rate and amount columns left blank for the bidder to fill in. Priced and signed, it becomes part of the contract and the basis of every running bill.

BOQ and abstract of cost compared

Bill of quantitiesAbstract of cost
PurposeTo invite and compare tenders, and to pay the contractorTo arrive at the estimated cost and obtain sanction
Prepared byThe employer; priced by the bidderThe estimator
Rates shownBlank when issued; the contractor's own rates when pricedThe estimator's analysed rates
StatusA contract documentAn internal costing document
When usedAt tendering, and throughout execution for paymentBefore tendering, for sanction and budgeting

The two share the same items, units and quantities — that is the relationship. They differ in whose rates fill the last two columns and in what the document is for.

5. Summary (general abstract) of cost

One line per sub-head or per sub-work, carrying the totals forward to the grand total, so a sanctioning authority can see the shape of the cost on a single page.

Dawadi, Estimating ch.2 §5.3 and chapter 3 estimating; Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual §3.4.
