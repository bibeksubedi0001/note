  1. What are the different methods of preparing approximate estimate? Write the suitability of each method. [6] (2073 Shrawan, Q.2(a))
    Solution

    Methods of preparing an approximate estimate, and the suitability of each

    MethodHow the cost is foundSuitable for
    1. Plinth area methodPlinth area (measured to the outer face of the walls) × plinth area rate of a recently built similar buildingThe commonest method for buildings of all kinds, where a comparable recent building exists
    2. Cubical content methodPlinth area × height (plinth level to half the roof height) × rate per m³Buildings where the height differs from the norm — halls, theatres, godowns — because it accounts for storey height, which the plinth area method ignores. More accurate than method 1
    3. Unit base / service unit methodNumber of functional units × cost per unit — per bed, per student, per seat, per vehicleBuildings defined by capacity: hospitals (per bed), schools (per student), cinemas (per seat), hostels (per resident)
    4. Approximate quantity methodThe work is split into a few major items (foundation, superstructure) and the running metre or bulk quantity of each is pricedBoundary walls, roads, canals, water lines — long structures of uniform section. The most accurate of the approximate methods
    5. Typical bay methodCost of one typical bay × number of bays, end bays taken separatelyLong repetitive structures — sheds, barracks, factory buildings
    6. Cost comparison / per-km methodCost of a completed similar work, scaled by length, capacity or a cost indexRoads (per km), pipelines, transmission lines

    Accuracy rises down the table and so does the effort. Methods 1–3 need only one overall figure; method 4 needs a preliminary layout. All of them are used before detailed drawings exist, which is exactly why none can be as accurate as a detailed estimate.

  2. Explain why approximate estimate of any structure is done before the detailed estimate and final cost is worked out. [4] (2068 Baishakh, Q.1(c))
    Solution

    Why an approximate estimate is made before the detailed estimate

    1. At the proposal stage the detailed drawings and specifications do not yet exist, so a detailed estimate is simply impossible. Only an overall parameter — plinth area, cube content, number of beds — is known.
    2. An approximate estimate is quick and cheap; a detailed estimate takes weeks of work. There is no sense in spending that effort on a scheme that may never be approved.
    3. It allows the competent authority to decide whether the scheme is worth pursuing and to grant administrative approval, and allows the amount to be provided in the budget.
    4. It allows alternative schemes, sites and designs to be compared on cost before one is selected for detailed design.
    5. It fixes the order of magnitude, so the detailed estimate prepared later can be checked against it — a large discrepancy signals an error in one of them.
    6. It lets the funding, land acquisition and clearances be started early, in parallel with the detailed design.

    The relationship is one of successive refinement: rough cost → approximate estimate (administrative approval) → detailed estimate (technical sanction) → tendered cost → actual cost. Each stage narrows the band of uncertainty.

    ENCE 351 §3.1.

    • a) What do you understand by approximate estimate? When do you need revised estimate?
    • b) Prepare a preliminary estimate of six storied framed structure office building having a total carpet area
    [4+4] (2076 Chaitra, Q.3)
    Solution

    a) Approximate estimate, and when a revised estimate is needed

    Approximate estimate. A rough estimate of the probable cost of a work, prepared at the proposal stage before detailed drawings and specifications exist, from a single overall parameter such as the plinth area, the cube content or the number of service units. It is supported by a report, a site plan and line drawings.

    It is prepared in order to:

    • judge whether the scheme is financially feasible and worth pursuing at all;
    • obtain administrative approval from the competent authority;
    • provide the amount in the annual budget;
    • compare alternative schemes and sites on cost before one is chosen; and
    • fix the order of magnitude, against which the detailed estimate prepared later can be checked.

    It is accurate to about ±15–20% only, and cannot be used for technical sanction or for inviting tenders.

    Revised estimate. A fresh detailed estimate prepared for a work that has already been sanctioned. It is needed when:

    1. the expenditure exceeds, or is likely to exceed, the sanctioned amount by more than 5%;
    2. the rates adopted in the original estimate have changed materially;
    3. there is a substantial change in the design, specification or scope of the work;
    4. site conditions turn out to differ from those assumed — rock instead of soil, a high water table, a longer lead; or
    5. the work was abandoned and is being restarted after a long interval.

    It shows the original and the revised quantity and cost of every item side by side, with the reason for each variation, and must be sanctioned afresh by the competent authority before the extra expenditure is incurred.

    b) Preliminary estimate of a six-storeyed framed office building

    The carpet area printed in the question paper is not legible in the available copy. A total carpet area of 3000 m² is assumed below; substitute the figure given in your paper — the method is unchanged.

    Convert carpet area to plinth area

    plinth area = carpet area / 0.60 = 3000 / 0.60 = 5000 m² (total, all six floors) per floor = 5000 / 6 = 833.33 m²

    Cost of the building by the plinth area method

    cost of building = plinth area × plinth area rate = 5000 × 30,000 = Rs 15,00,00,000

    Add the external services and other provisions

    cost of building Rs 15,00,00,000 water supply and sanitary installation @ 8% Rs 1,20,00,000 internal electrification @ 8% Rs 1,20,00,000 lift and fire fighting installation @ 5% Rs 75,00,000 architectural / elevation treatment @ 1% Rs 15,00,000 approach road, paving, compound wall @ 2% Rs 30,00,000 ---------------- Rs 18,60,00,000 contingencies @ 3% Rs 55,80,000 work-charged establishment @ 2% Rs 37,20,000 ---------------- approximate estimated cost Rs 19,53,00,000

    Two things carry the marks here: converting carpet area to plinth area (carpet area is the usable area only, and never equals the plinth area), and adding the external services as percentages.     ENCE 351 §3.1 and §3.3.

An approximate estimate — also called a rough, preliminary or budgetary estimate — finds the probable cost of a work quickly, before the detailed drawings exist. It is prepared at the proposal stage so that the authority can decide whether the scheme is worth pursuing and how much money to provide.

When and why it is prepared

  • To decide the financial policy — can the department afford it?
  • To obtain administrative approval and provide for the work in the budget.
  • To compare alternative proposals and choose between them.
  • When there is no time or no detailed drawing for a full estimate.

It is supported by a report explaining the necessity of the work, a site or index plan, and the rate assumed per unit, with the basis on which that rate was taken.

Methods of preparing an approximate estimate

MethodBasisSuitable for
Plinth area methodPlinth area × plinth area rate. The plinth area is measured to the outer face of the walls at floor level.Buildings — the commonest method of all
Cubical content methodPlinth area × height × a rate per m³, the height measured from floor level to the top of the roof.Buildings where height matters — halls, cinemas, godowns. More accurate than plinth area.
Unit base (service unit) methodCost per functional unit × number of units.School per classroom or per student, hospital per bed, cinema per seat, irrigation per hectare, water tank per litre, road per kilometre
Approximate quantity methodThe work is divided into two blocks — foundation and superstructure — and the running metre of wall in each is priced.Boundary walls, small buildings; more accurate than the plinth area method
Bay methodCost of one bay × number of bays.Repetitive structures — sheds, barracks

plinth area method : cost = plinth area (m²) × rate per m² cubical content : cost = plinth area × height × rate per m³ unit base method : cost = number of units × cost per unit

Accuracy improves down the table: plinth area is the crudest, approximate quantity the closest to a detailed estimate. Whichever is used, a contingency of 3–5% is added, and the estimate is expected to be within about 5–10% of the detailed figure.

  5. What are the various methods of taking out quantities of civil engineering? Explain briefly. State the different factors considered during detailed estimation. [4+2] (2078 Bhadra, Q.2)
    The first part of this question also belongs to Chapter 2.
    Solution

    a) Methods of taking out quantities of civil engineering works

    Four methods are used to take out the quantities of a load-bearing masonry building. All of them must give the same total length of wall — that identity is the check on the work.

    1. Long wall — short wall method (also called the out-to-out and in-to-in method)

    • Walls running in one direction are taken as long walls and measured from centre to centre plus one breadth of the wall (out to out).
    • Walls at right angles to them are taken as short walls and measured from centre to centre minus one breadth (in to in).
    • The length changes for every footing, because the breadth changes.
    long wall L = centre-to-centre length + breadth of the item short wall S = centre-to-centre length − breadth of the item quantity = (2L + 2S) × breadth × height

    Suitability: best when the walls of a building are of different thicknesses, or when the rooms are of different sizes, because each wall is dealt with on its own.

    2. Centre line method

    • The total centre-line length of all the walls is found once.
    • It is multiplied by the breadth and the depth of each item in turn.
    • For every junction where a wall meets another, half the breadth of the item is deducted, because that corner would otherwise be counted twice.
    deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item quantity = (total centre-line length − deduction) × breadth × height

    Suitability: the quickest method, and the one normally used when all the walls are of the same thickness and meet in simple T or L junctions. It becomes error-prone where junctions are many or irregular.

    3. Crossing method

    • The out-to-out length of the walls is taken in both directions.
    • The corners, which have then been counted twice, are deducted — one breadth for each crossing.
    quantity = [2(Lout + Bout) − 4 × breadth] × breadth × height

    Suitability: simple rectangular plans without internal cross walls; it becomes clumsy as soon as partitions are introduced.

    4. Bay method

    • A long building of repeating panels is divided into identical bays.
    • One typical bay is worked out in full and multiplied by the number of bays.
    • The two end bays are taken separately, since they carry the gable or end wall.

    Suitability: long repetitive structures — barracks, sheds, godowns, factory buildings, and framed buildings with equal spans.

    b) Factors considered during detailed estimation

    1. Completeness and correctness of the drawings — every dimension needed must be available and consistent; the estimate can be no better than the drawing.
    2. The specification of each item — the class of material and the mix decide both the quantity and the rate.
    3. The standard method of measurement (IS 1200) — particularly the deduction rules, which decide whether an opening is deducted at all.
    4. Prevailing rates of materials, labour and carriage at the site, and the district rate in force.
    5. Site conditions — nature of soil, water table, ground slope, access, and the lead and lift involved.
    6. Availability of materials, skilled labour, water and power locally, and the distance from which they must be brought.
    7. Season and duration of the work, and the likely escalation over that period.
    8. Method of construction and the plant proposed — manual or mechanised.
    9. Provisions for contingencies (3–5%), work-charged establishment (1.5–2%), and the percentage allowances for sanitary, water supply and electrical work.
    10. Overheads, profit and taxes applicable.

    ENCE 351 Chapter 2 and §3.2.

    ENCE 351 §3.2.

The detailed estimate is the accurate estimate, prepared after the drawings and specifications are complete. Every item of work is measured from the drawings, entered in a measurement (quantity) sheet, priced at the rate obtained by analysis of rates, and totalled in an abstract of cost.

How it is prepared

  1. Study the drawings and specifications and list the items of work, grouped under sub-heads (§3.6).
  2. Take out the quantities of each item from the drawings by one of the methods of Chapter 2, entering number, length, breadth and depth in the measurement sheet.
  3. Analyse the rate of each item (§6) from the norms and the district rates.
  4. Abstract the cost — quantity × rate for every item, summed by sub-head.
  5. Add contingencies, work-charged establishment and any other allowances.
  6. Attach the report, detailed specifications, drawings, calculation sheets and the analysis of rates.

What a detailed estimate must contain

  • Report and general abstract of cost
  • Detailed measurement (quantity) sheet
  • Abstract of cost
  • Analysis of rates
  • General and detailed specifications
  • Complete set of drawings — plans, elevations, sections, details
  • Site (index) plan and design calculations

Contingencies and work-charged establishment

Two allowances are added at the end of every detailed estimate, and the examiners regularly ask for the difference:

ContingenciesWork-charged establishment
What it coversSmall unforeseen items and incidental expenses that cannot be foreseen at the time of estimatingThe pay of staff employed specifically to supervise this work — site engineer, overseer, work sarkar, chowkidar
Usual allowance3–5% of the estimated cost1.5–2% of the estimated cost
Charged toThe workThe work (not the department's regular establishment)

Contingencies are not the same as overhead cost. Contingencies are a provision inside the estimate for the unforeseen; overheads are the contractor's own cost of running a business — office rent, staff salaries, insurance, depreciation of plant — and are recovered inside the rate (§6), not added to the estimate.

  1. What are the necessary points that needs to be considered while measuring earthwork? In what case do you need to prepare revise estimate? Explain with an example. [4+4] (2079 Bhadra, Q.2)
    The first part of this question also belongs to Chapter 1.
    Solution

    a) Points to be considered while measuring earthwork

    1. Classification of the soil — soft/ordinary soil, hard soil, soft rock and hard rock requiring blasting are measured and paid separately, because the effort differs greatly.
    2. Lead — the horizontal distance the excavated earth is carried. The first 30 m is normally included in the rate; beyond that, extra lead is paid in stages.
    3. Lift — the vertical height through which the earth is lifted. The first 1.5 m is included in the rate; each additional 1.5 m is paid extra.
    4. Depth of excavation, measured from the existing ground level, and taken in stages where the depth is large.
    5. Dewatering — excavation below the water table or in slush is measured separately.
    6. Working space and side slopes — measure the authorised dimensions of the trench as per drawing; extra excavation for the convenience of the contractor is not paid.
    7. Excavation and filling kept separate, and the volume of filling measured after compaction, not loose.
    8. Bulking and shrinkage — earth increases in volume when loosened and reduces when compacted, so the state in which the measurement is taken must be stated.
    9. Unit — measured in m³ and generally paid per 100 m³. Surface dressing up to 30 cm deep is measured in m².

    b) When a revised estimate is required

    b) Rate analysis

    Analysis of rates is the process of working out the cost of one unit of an item of work — per m³, per m², per m — by adding up the cost of the materials, the labour, the plant and the overheads that go into that unit, together with the contractor's profit.

    rate of one unit of work = cost of materials ( quantity × rate, including carriage ) + cost of labour ( number of man-days × daily wage ) + cost of plant and equipment ( hire / running charge ) + water charges and sundries ( 1–2% ) + overhead charges ( 5–10% ) + contractor's profit ( 10–15% )

    c) Importance of rate analysis

    1. It gives the correct rate of each item, and hence a realistic estimate; without it the estimate is only a guess.
    2. It shows the quantity of each material and each class of labour required, so they can be procured and programmed.
    3. It lets the department examine a contractor's quoted rate and judge whether it is reasonable, abnormally high or unworkably low.
    4. It is the basis for fixing the rate of extra, substituted and non-scheduled items that arise during execution.
    5. It shows the effect of a change in specification — how much the rate rises if the mix is made richer — and so helps in choosing between alternatives.
    6. It allows the estimate to be updated when material or labour rates change, without re-doing the quantities.
    7. It is required for settling claims, escalation and disputes.

  2. What are the various methods of taking out quantities? What are the sub-heads of items of work for building project? [4+4] (2080 Baishakh, Q.2)
    The first part of this question also belongs to Chapter 2.
    Solution

    a) Various methods of taking out quantities

    Four methods are used to take out the quantities of a load-bearing masonry building. All of them must give the same total length of wall — that identity is the check on the work.

    1. Long wall — short wall method (also called the out-to-out and in-to-in method)

    • Walls running in one direction are taken as long walls and measured from centre to centre plus one breadth of the wall (out to out).
    • Walls at right angles to them are taken as short walls and measured from centre to centre minus one breadth (in to in).
    • The length changes for every footing, because the breadth changes.
    long wall L = centre-to-centre length + breadth of the item short wall S = centre-to-centre length − breadth of the item quantity = (2L + 2S) × breadth × height

    Suitability: best when the walls of a building are of different thicknesses, or when the rooms are of different sizes, because each wall is dealt with on its own.

    2. Centre line method

    • The total centre-line length of all the walls is found once.
    • It is multiplied by the breadth and the depth of each item in turn.
    • For every junction where a wall meets another, half the breadth of the item is deducted, because that corner would otherwise be counted twice.
    deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item quantity = (total centre-line length − deduction) × breadth × height

    Suitability: the quickest method, and the one normally used when all the walls are of the same thickness and meet in simple T or L junctions. It becomes error-prone where junctions are many or irregular.

    3. Crossing method

    • The out-to-out length of the walls is taken in both directions.
    • The corners, which have then been counted twice, are deducted — one breadth for each crossing.
    quantity = [2(Lout + Bout) − 4 × breadth] × breadth × height

    Suitability: simple rectangular plans without internal cross walls; it becomes clumsy as soon as partitions are introduced.

    4. Bay method

    • A long building of repeating panels is divided into identical bays.
    • One typical bay is worked out in full and multiplied by the number of bays.
    • The two end bays are taken separately, since they carry the gable or end wall.

    Suitability: long repetitive structures — barracks, sheds, godowns, factory buildings, and framed buildings with equal spans.

    b) Sub-heads of items of work for a building project

  10. What do you mean by Project estimate? How do you prepare project estimate? State the reports on estimate. [1+2+3] (2075 Ashwin, Q.7)
    Solution

    a) Project estimate

    A project estimate is the estimate of the whole project — not merely of the main structure — covering every component and every ancillary cost needed to bring the facility into use: land, the main work, all its sub-works, external services, contingencies, establishment, fees and taxes. It is the document on which the project is sanctioned and budgeted.

    b) How a project estimate is prepared

    1. Define the scope and break the project into its components (work breakdown structure).
    2. Collect the data — drawings, specifications, survey and soil data, district rate, norms, lead statement.
    3. Estimate each component separately — take out the quantities, analyse the rates, and abstract the cost of each.
    4. Group the components under sub-heads and prepare the general abstract.
    5. Add the project-level provisions — land, services (8–10% each), external development, contingencies (3–5%), work-charged establishment (1.5–2%), consultancy fees and taxes (13% VAT).
    6. Prepare the summary of cost and the bill of quantities.
    7. Write the report and submit for administrative approval and technical sanction.

    c) Reports on an estimate

    ReportWhenWhat it contains
    Preliminary / feasibility reportWith the approximate estimateNeed for the work, alternatives, site, rough cost — the basis of administrative approval
    Detailed project report (DPR)With the detailed estimateDesign, drawings, specifications, quantities, rate analysis, abstract and summary of cost — the basis of technical sanction
    Revised estimate reportWhen the cost exceeds the sanction by >5%Original and revised cost of each item side by side, with the reason for every variation

    Project. A project is a temporary, non-repetitive undertaking taken up to create a unique product or facility, having a definite beginning and a definite end, its own objective, its own budget and its own team.

    Characteristics of a project

    1. Unique — no two projects are identical, even of the same design, because the site, time and people differ.

    7. Involves risk and uncertainty, which is why contingencies are provided.

    2. Land acquisition and compensation.
    3. Site clearance — clearing and grubbing, removal of structures.
    4. Earthwork — excavation in cutting by soil class, embankment in compacted layers, disposal of surplus.
    5. Pavement — subgrade preparation (m²), granular sub-base, base course, prime and tack coat, bituminous surfacing, seal coat, shoulders.
    6. Cross drainage — pipe, slab and box culverts, causeways, bridges.
    7. Protection works — retaining, breast and toe walls, gabions, slope protection.
    8. Drainage — side drains, catch drains, cross drains.
    9. Road furniture — signs, markings, guard posts, kilometre stones, parapets.
    10. Bio-engineering and plantation.
    11. Miscellaneous — diversion road, traffic management during construction.
    12. Contingencies 3–5% and work-charged establishment 1.5–2%.

    Presentation: the estimate is built on the chainage-wise earthwork sheet and the pavement quantities per km. The report should state the cost per kilometre, which is the figure used to compare road projects.

    Solution

    Works an estimator must take into account in a project estimate

    An estimator who prices only the visible structure will always under-estimate. The following heads must all be accounted for.

    1. Preliminary works — survey, soil investigation, testing, design and drawing, and the preparation of the estimate itself.
    2. Land — purchase or acquisition, compensation for crops and structures, legal and registration charges.
    3. Site works — clearing and grubbing, demolition of existing structures, site levelling, temporary access, site office, store, water and power connection, fencing and security.
    4. The main work — the structure itself, item by item under its sub-heads.
    5. Sub-works and ancillary structures — boundary wall, gate, guard room, septic tank and soak pit, overhead and underground tanks, generator room, pump house.
    6. Internal services — water supply and sanitary installation (8–10%), electrification (8–10%), and any lift, fire fighting or air conditioning installation.
    7. External services — approach road, paving, drainage, street lighting, and the external water, sewer and electric connections.
    8. Landscaping — lawns, plantation, and any architectural treatment.
    9. Furniture, fixtures and equipment needed to make the facility usable.
    10. Environmental and social mitigation as required by the IEE or EIA, and resettlement where applicable.
    11. Establishment and supervision — work-charged establishment (1.5–2%), consultancy and supervision fees.
    12. Contingencies (3–5%) for unforeseen petty expenses.
    13. Escalation over the construction period, and taxes (VAT 13%) and duties.
    14. Maintenance during the defect liability period, where it is at the employer's cost.

    Together the internal and external services alone add roughly 20–25% to the bare cost of a building. Omitting them is the single commonest cause of a project estimate falling short.

Four terms that are asked as short notes

TermMeaning
Work valueThe total of all the scheduled items of work in the estimate — that is, the estimated value excluding contingencies, work-charged establishment and tools and plant. Work value is the amount put out to invite tenders.
Departmental chargesWhere one engineering department executes work on behalf of another department, government or a local body, a charge of 10–15% of the estimated cost is recovered for its own establishment — planning, designing, supervision, pension and audit. It is shown separately from the works expenditure.
Administrative approvalThe formal acceptance by the administrative department of the proposal to incur expenditure on a work. It is in effect an order to execute the work, and is obtained on the approximate estimate.
Technical sanctionThe approval of the detailed estimate, designs and specifications by the competent engineering authority, certifying that the proposal is structurally sound and the rates are correct. No work may start until it is obtained.

Keep the last two apart: administrative approval sanctions the money and comes from the administrative side on the approximate estimate; technical sanction certifies the engineering and comes from the engineering side on the detailed estimate.
