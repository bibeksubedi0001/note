6.1 Importance and purpose of rate analysis208220812079

Past Exam Questions on this Topic

  1. What do you mean by rate analysis? What are the purposes and factors that affecting rate analysis? [1+2+3] (2082 Bhadra, Q.5)
    The last part of this question also belongs to Section 6.3.
    Solution

    Analysis of rates. The process of working out the cost of one unit of an item of work — per m³, per m², per running metre — by adding up the cost of all the materials, labour, plant and overheads that go into that unit, together with the contractor's profit.

    rate of one unit of work = cost of materials ( quantity × rate, including carriage ) + cost of labour ( man-days × daily wage, each category ) + cost of plant ( hire or running charge ) + water and sundries ( 1 – 2 % of materials ) + overhead charges ( 5 – 10 % ) + contractor's profit ( 10 – 15 % )

    Purposes / importance of rate analysis

    1. To fix the correct rate of each item, and hence arrive at a realistic estimate. Without it, an estimate is only a guess.
    2. To find the quantity of each material and each class of labour required, so that they can be procured and programmed.
    3. To examine a contractor's quoted rate and judge whether it is reasonable, abnormally high, or unworkably low.
    4. To fix the rate of extra, substituted and non-scheduled items that arise during execution.
    5. To show the effect of a change of specification — how much the rate rises if the mix is made richer — and so to help choose between alternatives.
    6. To update an estimate when material or labour rates change, without re-measuring the quantities.
    7. To settle claims, price escalation and disputes.
    8. To prepare the district rate and the departmental schedule of rates.

    Factors affecting the analysis of rates

    1. Specification of the item — the mix and the class of material. A richer mix or a better class of brick raises the rate directly.
    2. Market rate of materials, which fluctuates with season and demand.
    3. Lead and mode of transport — carriage can exceed the basic cost of a material in a remote district.
    4. Lift — the height to which materials must be raised; work at a great height or depth costs more.
    5. Wage rates and availability of labour of each category in the locality.
    6. Out-turn / task of the workman, which depends on his skill and on supervision.
    7. Site conditions — nature of soil, water table, congestion, access, and the need for dewatering or shoring.
    8. Location and accessibility — a remote hill site costs far more than an urban one for identical work.
    9. Season and weather — monsoon work is slower and costlier.
    10. Quantity and repetition — a large or repetitive quantity lowers the unit rate; a very small quantity raises it.
    11. Method of construction and plant used — manual or mechanised.
    12. Availability of water and power at the site.
    13. Overheads, profit, taxes and insurance.
    14. Wastage allowed for each material.

    ENCE 351 §6.1.

  3. Why analysis of rate is important for civil engineering work? Also, explain how rates are taken for analysis. Calculate the quantities of materials required for PCC (1:1.5:3) for RCC roof [2+2+4] (2079 Bhadra, Q.4)
    Solution

    a) Why analysis of rate is important

    Purposes / importance of rate analysis

    1. To fix the correct rate of each item, and hence arrive at a realistic estimate. Without it, an estimate is only a guess.
    2. To find the quantity of each material and each class of labour required, so that they can be procured and programmed.
    3. To examine a contractor's quoted rate and judge whether it is reasonable, abnormally high, or unworkably low.
    4. To fix the rate of extra, substituted and non-scheduled items that arise during execution.
    5. To show the effect of a change of specification — how much the rate rises if the mix is made richer — and so to help choose between alternatives.
    6. To update an estimate when material or labour rates change, without re-measuring the quantities.
    7. To settle claims, price escalation and disputes.
    8. To prepare the district rate and the departmental schedule of rates.

    b) How rates are taken for analysis

    1. The quantity of each material, each class of labour and each plant per unit of the item is taken from the departmental norms (DUDBC for buildings, DoR for roads, DWSS for sanitary works). These are national and do not change from district to district.
    2. The price of each material and of one man-day of each class of labour is taken from the district rate, published every fiscal year by the District Rate Fixation Committee under the Chief District Officer.
    3. Carriage is added for the actual lead of each material from its quarry or market, supported by a lead statement.
    4. Quantity × rate is worked out for every component and summed; then water and sundries (1–2%), overheads and profit (15–20%) are added. VAT at 13% is added at the summary stage.

    c) Quantities of material for 1:1½:3 concrete in an RCC roof

    The volume of the roof slab is not legible in the available copy of this paper. The calculation is therefore done per m³ and then shown for a 10 m³ slab; multiply by the volume given in your paper.

    Mix 1 : 1½ : 3 (M20) · dry volume factor 1.54 · density of cement 1440 kg/m³ · 1 bag = 50 kg

    Dry volume and sum of the proportions

    wet volume of concrete = 1.00 m³ dry volume = 1.00 × 1.54 = 1.54 m³ sum of proportions = 1 + 1.5 + 3 = 5.5

    Cement

    volume of cement = 1.54 × (1 / 5.5) = 0.28 m³ weight = 0.28 × 1440 = 403.20 kg bags = 403.20 / 50 = 8.064 ≈ 8.06 bags

    Sand and coarse aggregate

    sand = 1.54 × (1.5 / 5.5) = 0.42 m³ aggregate = 1.54 × (3 / 5.5) = 0.84 m³ check : 0.28 + 0.42 + 0.84 = 1.54 m³ ✓

    For a 10 m³ roof slab

    cement = 8.064 × 10 = 80.64 bags ( ≈ 4032 kg ) sand = 0.42 × 10 = 4.20 m³ aggregate = 0.84 × 10 = 8.40 m³
    Per m³ of 1:1½:3 concrete: 8.06 bags (403.2 kg) of cement, 0.42 m³ of sand and 0.84 m³ of coarse aggregate.

    Note the wording of the question: a 1:1½:3 mix is an M20 RCC mix, not a plain-concrete mix — PCC is normally 1:2:4 or leaner. If the roof is reinforced, the steel is measured and paid separately in kg, and is not part of this calculation.

    ENCE 351 §6.1 and §6.5.

Analysis of rates is the process of working out the rate of one unit of an item of work from first principles — the cost of the materials it consumes, the labour it takes, the hire of tools and plant, plus the contractor's overheads and profit.

rate per unit = cost of materials + cost of labour + hire charge of tools, plant and equipment + overheads (water, electricity, supervision, office) + contractor's profit + taxes and levies as applicable

Purpose

  1. To fix a fair and workable rate for each item, so that the estimate reflects the true cost.
  2. To work out the quantities of material and labour the item will consume, so that they can be procured and programmed.
  3. To examine tenders — to judge whether a bidder's rate is reasonable, unbalanced or below cost.
  4. To settle rates for extra and substituted items that arise during execution and were never in the contract.
  5. To revise the rates in the departmental schedule of rates when market prices move.
  6. To provide a defensible basis for claims and arbitration.

Importance

Without rate analysis a rate is only a guess. Two consequences follow:

  • An estimate built on unanalysed rates cannot be defended before a sanctioning authority, and will not match the tenders received.
  • Rates differ from place to place and from time to time — the same brickwork costs more where the lead for sand is 40 km than where it is 5 km. Only analysis captures that.

The four heads, and the percentages

A standard analysis is built under four heads:

  1. Materials — the quantity required per unit of work, priced delivered at the work site, i.e. including first cost, freight, transport, sales tax and insurance.
  2. Labour — the number of man-days (or labour hours) of each category needed to complete one unit, at the prevailing wage.
  3. Overhead, equipment and establishment charges.
  4. Profit.
water charge = 1% of ( materials + labour ) overhead + profit = 15% of ( materials + labour + water charge ) of which about 10% is the contractor's profit rate per unit = materials + labour + water charge + 15%

Departmentally supplied materials. Where the department itself issues cement, steel, stone chips or bitumen to the contractor, no profit is allowed on the cost of those materials — but the cost of carriage from the departmental godown to the work site is added. This is a favourite examination point, and it is the reason a rate analysis must always state whether a material is contractor-supplied or departmental.

Dawadi, Chapter 4 — Analysis of Rates §4.1–4.3; the percentages and the rule on departmentally supplied materials follow Chakraborti, Estimating, Costing and Specification, ch. XI §11-1, which is based on the All India Standard Schedule of Rates.

  1. What is lead and lift? [2] (2080 Bhadra, Q.4(a)(i))
    Solution

    Lead. The horizontal distance through which excavated earth or a material is carried from the point where it is dug or stacked to the point where it is used or deposited. The first 30 m is normally included in the rate for earthwork; beyond that, extra carriage is paid in stages (30 m, 100 m, 500 m, 1 km, and then per km).

    Lift. The vertical height through which the material is raised or lowered above or below the ground level at the point of excavation. The first 1.5 m is included in the rate; each additional 1.5 m of lift is paid extra.

    lead → horizontal → free up to 30 m lift → vertical → free up to 1.5 m

    Both matter because the same item of work costs different amounts at different places. Two identical foundations, one beside the spoil dump and one 300 m away and 4 m deep, cannot carry the same rate — lead and lift are how the difference is measured and paid.

Five things are needed before a rate can be analysed:

  1. Specification of the item — the mix, the class of material, the thickness, the workmanship. The rate for 1:4 cement mortar brickwork is not the rate for 1:6.
  2. Norms — the standard schedule stating how much material, labour and equipment one unit consumes. In Nepal these are the norms issued by the Government (DoR / DUDBC), uniform nationwide.
  3. Rates of materials at site — basic rate plus carriage (lead and lift), loading, unloading and stacking.
  4. Rates of labour of each category — skilled (mason, carpenter, bar bender, plumber) and unskilled (mazdoor, bhisti, coolie), taken from the district rate list.
  5. Hire charges of tools, plant and equipment — mixer, vibrator, roller, excavator; usually expressed per hour of use.

Lead and lift

Carriage of material is paid for by lead and lift, and the terms are asked for directly in the exam:

LeadLift
MeaningThe horizontal distance over which material (or excavated earth) is carried from the source to the point of useThe vertical distance through which it is raised or lowered
Measured fromCentre of the borrow pit / quarry to the centre of placingGround level datum
ChargedFirst lead (usually 30 m) is included in the rate; beyond that it is paid per unit per additional leadFirst lift (usually 1.5 m) is included; each additional lift is paid separately

Both exist because carrying earth 2 km costs far more than moving it 5 m, and lifting it 6 m out of a deep trench costs more than throwing it onto the bank. The rate must follow the effort.

    ENCE 351 §6.3.

The examiners ask for these factors almost every year, usually for 4 marks.

  1. Specification and quality of the work — richer mixes, better finishes and tighter tolerances all raise the rate.
  2. Cost and availability of materials — market price, and whether the material is locally available or must be imported.
  3. Lead and lift — the carriage distance and height, and the condition of the access road.
  4. Location of the site — urban, rural or remote hill; a site reachable only by mule track carries a very different rate.
  5. Availability and wage of labour, and the categories required; a shortage of skilled masons raises the rate.
  6. Quantity (scale) of work — a large quantity spreads the mobilisation cost and lowers the unit rate.
  7. Season and weather — monsoon working, or work at high altitude in winter, reduces output.
  8. Method of construction — manual or mechanised; the plant deployed.
  9. Facilities to be provided to labour — housing, transport, safety equipment.
  10. Overheads and profit expected by the contractor, and the taxes and levies in force.
  11. Wastage allowed on materials, and the output (productivity) assumed for labour and plant.

  1. What are the departmental norms used for analysis of rate in Nepal for building works, road works and sanitary works? Also list the data provided in such norms. [4] (2081 Bhadra, Q.6)
    Solution

    a) Departmental norms used for analysis of rates in Nepal

    Type of workNorms issued byDocument
    Building worksDepartment of Urban Development and Building Construction (DUDBC), Ministry of Urban DevelopmentNorms for Rate Analysis of Building Works
    Road worksDepartment of Roads (DoR), Ministry of Physical Infrastructure and TransportStandard Norms for Road Works, used with the DoR Standard Specification
    Sanitary and water supply worksDepartment of Water Supply and Sewerage Management (DWSSM)Norms for Water Supply and Sanitation Works
    Irrigation worksDepartment of Water Resources and IrrigationIrrigation norms
    Electrical worksDepartment of Electricity Development / NEAElectrical norms

    b) Data provided in such norms

    For one unit of each item of work (per m³, per m², per m), the norm gives:

    1. Materials — the quantity of each material required, with the wastage already included. Example: for 1 m³ of 1:2:4 PCC — 6.34 bags of cement, 0.44 m³ of sand, 0.88 m³ of coarse aggregate.
    2. Labour — the number of man-days of each category: skilled (mason, carpenter, bar bender), semi-skilled, and unskilled.
    3. Plant and equipment — the hours or fraction of a day of mixer, vibrator, roller or compressor required.
    4. Task / out-turn of work — how much a workman completes in one day.
    5. Water requirement and the allowance for sundries.
    6. Percentage allowances for tools and plant, overheads and contractor's profit.
    7. The specification of the item to which the norm applies, and the conditions assumed (for example, work up to a stated height or lead).

    The norm deliberately gives quantities only, never prices. Prices come from the district rate, which changes every year and differs between districts; the norm is national and stable.

  2. Explain in detail how Nepal Government agencies take norms and rates for rate analysis. [2+2] (2080 Bhadra, Q.4(c))
    Solution

    How Nepal Government agencies take norms and rates for rate analysis

    Two different things are combined, and the distinction is the point of the question.

    NormsRates
    What it givesThe quantity of material, labour and plant needed for one unit of an itemThe price of one unit of each material and one man-day of each class of labour
    Who fixes itThe Department of Urban Development and Building Construction (DUDBC) for buildings, the Department of Roads (DoR) for roads, and the Department of Water Supply and Sewerage (DWSS) for sanitary worksThe District Rate Fixation Committee, headed by the Chief District Officer
    ScopeNational — the same everywhere in the countryLocal — different in every district
    How often revisedRarely — only when technology or practice changesEvery fiscal year, and published as the district rate
    Example1 m³ of 1:2:4 PCC needs 6.34 bags of cement, 0.44 m³ sand, 0.88 m³ aggregate, 0.35 mason and 3 labourersIn that district a bag of cement costs Rs 850 and an unskilled man-day Rs 900

    The procedure followed

    1. The norms are taken from the relevant departmental Norms for Rate Analysis — DUDBC for building works, DoR (Standard Norms for Road Works) for roads, DWSS for water supply and sanitary works.
    2. The basic rates of materials and labour are taken from the district rate published each year by the District Rate Fixation Committee for that district.
    3. Carriage is added to each material for the actual lead from its quarry or market to the site, using the rate per unit per km in the district rate, supported by a lead statement.
    4. Quantity from the norm × rate from the district rate = cost of that component; the components are summed.
    5. Water and sundries (1–2%), overheads and contractor's profit (together about 15–20%) are added.
    6. The analysed rate is entered in the abstract of cost; VAT at 13% is added at the summary stage.
    7. Items not covered by any norm are analysed separately from first principles and got approved as non-scheduled items.

    The reason for the split is control. Norms are technical and must not vary from district to district; prices genuinely do vary, so they are fixed locally and annually. Mixing the two up — using another district's rate, or an out-of-date norm — is the commonest audit objection.

    ENCE 351 §6.4.

  3. A structure is to be constructed in Bajhang district with Plain Cement Concrete (PCC) work. Fine and coarse aggregates are available locally @ Rs. 2000/m³. Cement is not locally available and has to be bought from the nearest market 100 km away; its cost is Rs. 600 per bag and the carrying cost is Rs. 0.05/kg/km. Find the cost of 1 cum PCC work at that place if the skilled and unskilled manpower required per cum is 1 and 4 man-days with Rs. 1000 and Rs. 900 daily wages respectively. [7] (2081 Bhadra, Q.8)
    Solution
    PCC work at Bajhang, mix taken as 1 : 2 : 4
    Fine and coarse aggregate available locally @ Rs 2,000 per m³
    Cement Rs 600 per bag, market 100 km away, carriage Rs 0.05 per kg per km
    Skilled manpower 1 man-day @ Rs 1,000; unskilled 4 man-days @ Rs 900
    Dry volume factor 1.54 · density of cement 1440 kg/m³ · 1 bag = 50 kg

    Quantity of materials for 1 m³ of 1:2:4 concrete

    dry volume = 1.00 × 1.54 = 1.54 m³ sum of proportions = 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 cement = 1.54 × (1/7) = 0.22 m³ = 0.22 × 1440 = 316.80 kg = 316.80 / 50 = 6.336 bags sand = 1.54 × (2/7) = 0.44 m³ aggregate = 1.54 × (4/7) = 0.88 m³ check : 0.22 + 0.44 + 0.88 = 1.54 m³ ✓

    Cost of cement, delivered at Bajhang

    basic cost = 6.336 bags × Rs 600 = Rs 3,801.60 carriage = weight × rate × distance = 316.80 kg × Rs 0.05 × 100 km = 316.80 × 5 = Rs 1,584.00 ------------- delivered cost of cement = Rs 5,385.60

    The carriage must be worked out on the weight of cement, not on the number of bags — the rate is given per kg per km. This is where marks are most often lost.

    Cost of aggregates

    sand + coarse aggregate = 0.44 + 0.88 = 1.32 m³ available locally, so no carriage is added cost = 1.32 × Rs 2,000 = Rs 2,640.00

    Cost of labour

    skilled : 1 man-day × Rs 1,000 = Rs 1,000.00 unskilled : 4 man-days × Rs 900 = Rs 3,600.00 ------------- Rs 4,600.00

    Rate of 1 m³ of PCC

    cement (delivered) = Rs 5,385.60 sand and coarse aggregate = Rs 2,640.00 labour = Rs 4,600.00 ------------- cost of materials and labour = Rs 12,625.60 add overhead and contractor's profit @ 15% = Rs 1,893.84 ------------- RATE OF 1 m³ OF PCC = Rs 14,519.44
    Cost of 1 m³ of PCC work at Bajhang = Rs 12,625.60 for materials and labour, or Rs 14,519.44 including 15% overhead and profit (say Rs 14,520 per m³).

    The carriage of cement alone adds Rs 1,584 to every cubic metre — about 42% on top of the basic cost of the cement, and 12.5% of the whole rate. That is precisely why the same item of work carries a different rate in every district, and why a lead statement must accompany the rate analysis.

    ENCE 351 §6.4 and §6.5. The mix is not stated in the question; 1:2:4 is the standard PCC mix and is assumed here — state your assumption in the examination.

  2. Calculate quantity of materials required for 5 m³ of stone masonry in 1:6 cement sand mortar ratios. [5] (2081 Baishakh, Q.5)
    Solution
    5 m³ of stone masonry · cement sand mortar 1 : 6

    Volume of mortar

    In random rubble stone masonry the stones are irregular, so the mortar fills a much larger fraction of the volume than in brickwork — usually 30 to 35%. Take 32%. wet volume of mortar = 5.00 × 0.32 = 1.600 m³ dry volume = 1.600 × 1.30 = 2.080 m³

Cement is reckoned at 50 kg = 1 bag, and 1 m³ of cement is taken as 28.8 bags (1440 kg/m³). Dry volume of mortar or concrete is taken as 1.52–1.54 times the wet volume, to allow for the voids in the sand and aggregate — this factor is what turns a 1 m³ concrete item into its bag and m³ quantities.

Road works

Rate analysis for road items is dominated by plant hire rather than labour:

  • Earthwork in excavation or filling using equipment — excavator, dozer and tipper hours per m³, plus a roller for compaction; the norm gives hours per m³ and the district rate gives the hourly hire.
  • Gabion work — GI wire mesh crates, boulder filling, labour for filling and lacing.
  • Stone masonry in retaining and breast walls — stone, mortar, mason and mazdoor.
  • Plum concrete — concrete plus 30–40% displacer boulders.
  • Pavement works — WBM (stone aggregate, screening, binding material, roller hours), premix carpet and seal coat (aggregate, bitumen, boiler, paver, roller).

Sanitary and water supply works

These are largely supply-and-fix items, so the material dominates and the labour is a fitting operation:

  • Wash basin — basin, brackets, waste, trap, tap, C.P. fittings; plumber and mazdoor.
  • Commode / W.C. pan — pan, flushing cistern, seat cover, connecting pipe, floor trap; plumber and mazdoor.
  • Pipes and fittings — GI or HDPE pipe per running metre, with specials, clamps, jointing material; measured in running metre and paid per metre.

For a supply-and-fix item the rate is usually stated per number, and the analysis must include the cost of the fitting itself, all accessories, the plumber's and mazdoor's time, and a wastage allowance on the small fittings.

Quantity of water — from the water cement ratio

Water is a material like any other and is asked for in its own right. Once the cement has been worked out, the water follows from the water cement ratio, which is a ratio by weight:

weight of water = (w/c) × weight of cement weight of cement = number of bags × 50 kg 1 kg of water = 1 litre, so the answer is directly in litres typical w/c : 0.45 – 0.50 for RCC, 0.50 – 0.60 for PCC 0.35 – 0.60 for mortar and plaster in no case shall the water exceed 30 litres per 50 kg bag of cement

Example. For the 100 m³ M20 raft of §6.5 the cement works out to 806.4 bags. At w/c = 0.50:

weight of cement = 806.4 × 50 = 40,320 kg water = 0.50 × 40,320 = 20,160 kg = 20,160 litres ≈ 20.2 m³

Water for soaking bricks, mixing, washing aggregate and curing, and the cost of the platform, labour shed and site godown, are all included in the rate and are not paid for separately — which is why the rate analysis carries only a small percentage for "water and sundries" rather than a measured quantity. The calculation above is needed when the question asks explicitly for the quantity of water.

Volume of a cement bag — two conventions. Taking the density of cement as 1440 kg/m³, one 50 kg bag occupies 0.0347 m³, so 1 m³ = 28.8 bags. Some texts round the bag to 1/30 m³ (0.0333 m³), giving 30 bags per m³. The figures in this chapter use 28.8; state whichever you adopt, because it changes the cement quantity by about 4%.

Two allowances that are easy to forget

  • Unevenness of the base. For concrete laid on a rough base — a floor over brick soling, or a levelling course on excavated ground — add about 10% to the wet volume before converting to dry volume, to fill the hollows.
  • Reinforcement as a percentage. Where no bar bending schedule is given, take the steel as a percentage of the volume of concrete and convert at 7850 kg/m³: slab 0.7–1.0%, lintel and beam 1.0–1.5%, column 2.0–3.0%, footing 0.5–0.8%.

Example — 415 m² of 40 mm cement concrete 1:2:4 in a floor:

wet volume = 415 × 0.040 = 16.60 m³ add 10% for unevenness of the base = 18.26 m³ dry volume = 18.26 × 1.50 = 27.39 m³ sum of proportions = 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 cement = 27.39 × (1/7) = 3.913 m³ = 3.913 × 28.8 bags per m³ = 112.7 bags sand = 27.39 × (2/7) = 7.83 m³ aggregate = 27.39 × (4/7) = 15.65 m³ water at w/c = 0.50 = 0.50 × 112.7 × 50 = 2,817 litres
