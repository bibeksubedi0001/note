5.1 Pipe culverts, slab culverts and box culverts

A culvert is a cross-drainage structure of span up to about 6 m carrying the road over a small stream. Three kinds are examined:

TypeDescriptionUsed when
Pipe culvertOne or more Hume (RCC) pipes bedded in concrete between headwallsSmall discharge; the cheapest option
Slab culvertAn RCC slab spanning between two abutments, on a concrete or masonry foundationSpan 1–6 m, firm foundation
Box culvertA monolithic RCC box — base slab, two walls and top slab acting togetherPoor foundation, or where the base must resist uplift and scour

volume of deck slab = clear span + 2 × bearing × width of deck × thickness abutment volume = length × mean thickness × height ( use the mean for a battered face )

For a box culvert take the four elements as one unit: base slab, two vertical walls, top slab, plus the haunches. Its great advantage in the estimate is that no separate foundation is needed — the base slab is the foundation.

5.2 T-beam bridge (superstructure, abutment and pier)

A T-beam bridge has a deck slab cast monolithically with longitudinal girders, so that each girder and the slab above it act as a T. It is the standard medium-span (10–25 m) RCC road bridge.

Superstructure

ElementHow measured
Deck slaboverall length × width × thickness, measured over the girders
Longitudinal girdersnumber × length × web width × depth below the slab (the slab is already counted)
Cross girders / diaphragmsnumber × clear length between girders × width × depth
Kerb and footpathlength × width × thickness
Wearing coatcarriageway area × thickness
Railing / parapetrunning metre, both sides
Bearings and expansion jointscounted
Reinforcementkg, from the bar schedule of each element
Formworkm² — soffit and sides of slab, girders and cross girders

The single commonest error is double counting the flange. The deck slab is measured across the full width including the part over the girders, so the girder must be measured only for its web below the slab — not its full T-depth.

Abutment and pier

Both are measured as a stack of prisms, bottom upward, each with its own dimensions:

  • Foundation / footing — length × breadth × thickness.
  • Stem or shaft — where the face is battered, use the mean thickness of top and bottom.
  • Cap and bed block under the bearings.
  • Dirt wall and wing / return walls for the abutment, measured separately.
  • Backfilling behind the abutment, with filter media and weep holes.
battered element : volume = length × ½(ttop + tbottom) × height circular pier : volume = π/4 × d² × height

5.3 Well foundation

A well foundation is a deep foundation sunk through a river bed to a firm stratum below the scour level. It is used where an open foundation cannot be taken down — the standard foundation for bridge piers in Nepal's braided rivers.

Parts, and how each is measured

PartFunctionMeasurement
Cutting edgeThe sharpened steel-shod bottom that helps the well sinkweight of steel in kg; concrete in m³
Well curbThe wedge-shaped RCC ring above the cutting edgem³ — volume of the tapering ring
SteiningThe main cylindrical wall of the wellm³ — the annular volume × height
Bottom plugConcrete seal at the base, cast under waterm³
Sand fillingFills the dredge hole above the bottom plugm³
Top plugConcrete seal over the sand fillingm³
Well capThe RCC slab that transfers the pier load to the wellm³
steining volume = π/4 ( Douter² − Dinner² ) × height dredge hole = π/4 × Dinner² × depth ( sand filling, plugs ) well cap = π/4 × Dcap² × thickness

Brickwork in well steining is one of the classic "state the unit" questions — it is measured and paid in m³ (§1.2). Sinking of the well is usually paid separately, per metre of depth, because the effort increases with depth.

5.4 Earthwork in road construction in plain and hilly areas

Road earthwork means bringing the ground to formation level, by cutting where the ground is high and filling (banking) where it is low. The cross-section is a trapezium.

cross-sectional area A = B·D + s·D² B = formation width of the road D = depth of cutting or height of filling at that chainage s = side slope, expressed as s horizontal to 1 vertical area of cutting Ac = B·Dc + sc·Dc² area of filling Af = B·Df + sf·Df²

The s·D² term is the two side triangles taken together: each has base sD and height D, so the two contribute 2 × ½ × sD × D = sD². Cutting and filling normally take different side slopes, which is why sc and sf are kept apart.

Three methods of finding the volume between two sections

Let two cross-sections a distance L apart have depths d₁ and d₂.

MethodFormulaCharacter
1. Mid-sectional area
(average depth)		dm = (d₁ + d₂)/2
Amid = B·dm + s·dm²
Q = Amid × L		Averages the depth first. Under-estimates, because A is quadratic in d.
2. Mean sectional areaA₁ = B·d₁ + s·d₁²
A₂ = B·d₂ + s·d₂²
Q = ½(A₁ + A₂) × L		Averages the areas. Over-estimates, for the same reason.
3. Prismoidal formulaQ = (L/6)(A₁ + 4Amid + A₂)Combines the two in the ratio 1:4:1. The most accurate, and the one to use unless told otherwise.

Mid-sectional and mean-sectional bracket the true volume from below and above; the prismoidal formula is Simpson's rule applied to the cross-sectional area and lies between them. If a question asks you to "compare the methods", that bracketing is the answer.

Hilly ground — the effect of cross slope

In hilly country the ground itself slopes across the road, so the section is part cutting and part filling and the two side slopes are no longer symmetrical. The area is then found from the two-level section:

with a transverse ground slope of 1 in n : A = ( B·D + s·D² ) × n² / ( n² − s² )

and the cutting and filling parts must be worked out separately, because earth cut on the hill side is used to build the valley side. The estimate then shows how much material must be borrowed or wasted — which is exactly what a mass haul diagram answers.

Presentation

Road earthwork is set out chainage by chainage in a table — chainage, ground level, formation level, depth of cut or fill, area, mean area, length, and volume of cutting and of filling in separate columns — and the two columns are totalled separately so the balance of earthwork is visible.

Dawadi, estimating earth work §6.3 — formulas transcribed directly from that source.

5.5 Estimation of pavement work and retaining works in roads

Pavement work

Once the formation is ready, the pavement is measured layer by layer. Each layer is an area × a compacted thickness:

LayerMeasurementUnit
Preparation of subgradeformation width × lengthm²
Granular sub-basewidth × length × compacted thicknessm³
Water bound macadam baseas above, each course separatelym³
Prime coat / tack coatarea sprayedm²
Premix carpet, bituminous concretearea × thicknessm² or m³
Seal coataream²
Shoulderseparately from the carriagewaym³
volume of a layer = carriageway width × length × compacted thickness for 1 km of a 3.75 m wide, 150 mm thick WBM base: Q = 3.75 × 1000 × 0.15 = 562.5 m³

Always use the compacted thickness for payment. The loose thickness is greater, and the difference is absorbed in the rate through the norms.

Retaining and breast walls

Hill roads need a great deal of retaining work, and it is measured as a stack of prisms exactly like a bridge abutment:

  • Retaining wall — holds up filled ground on the valley side.
  • Breast wall — supports the cut face on the hill side.
  • Toe wall — protects the toe of an embankment.
  • Gabion wall — wire crates filled with boulders; measured in m³ of crate, with the wire mesh in m² or by weight.
masonry wall with a battered face: volume = length × ½( top width + bottom width ) × height

Add, separately: foundation excavation, PCC levelling course, weep holes (number), filter media behind the wall (m³), coping (running metre), and any drainage channel.

5.6 Earthwork and lining works in irrigation canals

An irrigation canal is estimated the same way as a road — a trapezoidal section repeated along a chainage — but here the section is a channel rather than an embankment, and it usually has to be lined.

Earthwork in the canal

area of the canal section A = B·D + s·D² B = bed width , D = depth of the channel , s = side slope volume between two sections, by the prismoidal formula: Q = (L/6)( A₁ + 4Amid + A₂ )

Cutting and filling are kept in separate columns, chainage by chainage, exactly as for a road. Where the canal runs partly in cutting and partly on a bank, the bank itself is a further filling item with its own section.

Lining

Lining reduces seepage and prevents scour. It is measured on the wetted perimeter — the surface actually covered:

wetted perimeter P = B + 2D√(1 + s²) area of lining = P × length of canal volume of lining = P × length × thickness of lining

The √(1 + s²) is simply the slope length of one side per unit depth — for a 1.5:1 side slope it is √(1 + 2.25) = 1.803, so each sloping face is 1.803 D long, not D. Forgetting it under-measures the lining by about half the side area.

Items to be measured

  1. Clearing and grubbing of the alignment.
  2. Earthwork in excavation, by class of soil; and earthwork in filling for banks, in compacted layers.
  3. Dressing and trimming of the bed and side slopes — m².
  4. PCC or brick lining to bed and sides — m² or m³ on the wetted perimeter.
  5. Construction joints and expansion joints — running metre.
  6. Structures on the canal — head regulator, cross regulator, outlets, falls, aqueducts and culverts, each estimated separately.
  7. Service road on the bank, and turfing of the slopes.

Dawadi, estimating earth work — canal sections; hydraulic relations are the standard trapezoidal-channel geometry.
