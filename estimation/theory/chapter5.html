5.1 Pipe culverts, slab culverts and box culverts

A culvert is a cross-drainage structure of span up to about 6 m carrying the road over a small stream. Three kinds are examined:

TypeDescriptionUsed when
Pipe culvertOne or more Hume (RCC) pipes bedded in concrete between headwallsSmall discharge; the cheapest option
Slab culvertAn RCC slab spanning between two abutments, on a concrete or masonry foundationSpan 1–6 m, firm foundation
Box culvertA monolithic RCC box — base slab, two walls and top slab acting togetherPoor foundation, or where the base must resist uplift and scour

5.2 T-beam bridge (superstructure, abutment and pier)

A T-beam bridge has a deck slab cast monolithically with longitudinal girders, so that each girder and the slab above it act as a T. It is the standard medium-span (10–25 m) RCC road bridge.

Superstructure

ElementHow measured
Deck slaboverall length × width × thickness, measured over the girders
Longitudinal girdersnumber × length × web width × depth below the slab (the slab is already counted)
Cross girders / diaphragmsnumber × clear length between girders × width × depth
Kerb and footpathlength × width × thickness
Wearing coatcarriageway area × thickness
Railing / parapetrunning metre, both sides
Bearings and expansion jointscounted
Reinforcementkg, from the bar schedule of each element
Formworkm² — soffit and sides of slab, girders and cross girders

The single commonest error is double counting the flange. The deck slab is measured across the full width including the part over the girders, so each girder must be measured only for its web below the slab — not its full T-depth.

Abutment and pier

Both are measured as a stack of prisms, bottom upward, each with its own dimensions:

  • Foundation / footing — length × breadth × thickness.
  • Stem or shaft — where the face is battered, use the mean thickness of top and bottom.
  • Cap and bed block under the bearings.
  • Dirt wall and wing / return walls for the abutment, measured separately.
  • Backfilling behind the abutment, with filter media and weep holes.
battered element : volume = length × ½(ttop + tbottom) × height circular pier : volume = π/4 × d² × height

5.3 Well foundation

A well foundation is a deep foundation sunk through a river bed to a firm stratum below the scour level. It is used where an open foundation cannot be taken down — the standard foundation for bridge piers in Nepal's braided rivers.

5.4 Earthwork in road construction in plain and hilly areas

Road earthwork means bringing the ground to formation level, by cutting where the ground is high and filling (banking) where it is low. The cross-section is a trapezium.

cross-sectional area A = B·D + s·D² B = formation width of the road D = depth of cutting or height of filling at that chainage s = side slope, expressed as s horizontal to 1 vertical area of cutting Ac = B·Dc + sc·Dc² area of filling Af = B·Df + sf·Df²

The s·D² term is the two side triangles taken together: each has base sD and height D, so the two contribute 2 × ½ × sD × D = sD². Cutting and filling normally take different side slopes, which is why sc and sf are kept apart.

Three methods of finding the volume between two sections

Let two cross-sections a distance L apart have depths d₁ and d₂.

MethodFormulaCharacter
1. Mid-sectional area
(average depth)		dm = (d₁ + d₂)/2
Amid = B·dm + s·dm²
Q = Amid × L		Averages the depth first. Under-estimates, because A is quadratic in d.
2. Mean sectional areaA₁ = B·d₁ + s·d₁²
A₂ = B·d₂ + s·d₂²
Q = ½(A₁ + A₂) × L		Averages the areas. Over-estimates, for the same reason.
3. Prismoidal formulaQ = (L/6)(A₁ + 4Amid + A₂)Combines the two in the ratio 1:4:1. The most accurate, and the one to use unless told otherwise.

Mid-sectional and mean-sectional bracket the true volume from below and above; the prismoidal formula is Simpson's rule applied to the cross-sectional area and always lies between them. If a question asks you to "compare the methods", that bracketing is the answer — and the worked table above demonstrates it.

Hilly ground — the two-level section

In hilly country the ground itself slopes across the road, so the two side slopes are no longer of the same length and the simple trapezium no longer applies. The section is then a two-level section, and its area is

s·b² + r² ( 2·b·d + s·d² ) A = ------------------------------------ r² − s² b = HALF the formation width, B/2 d = centre height of banking, or centre depth of cutting s = side slope of the road, s horizontal to 1 vertical 1 in r = cross slope of the ground, 1 vertical in r horizontal on level ground r → ∞ and this reduces to A = B·d + s·d²

Do not use A = (B·d + s·d²) × r²/(r² − s²). That expression omits the s·b² term and under-estimates the area — by about 2% in the worked example below, and by more as the formation widens. The full form above is the one that reproduces the tabulated areas exactly.

Worked example — road on a cross slope

Formation width B = 7.00 m, so b = B/2 = 3.50 m · side slope 2 : 1
Ground cross slope tan θ = 0.1, i.e. 1 vertical in 10 horizontal · cross-sections 20 m apart
at the first chainage, d = 1.20 m : A = [ 2 × 3.50² + 10² ( 2 × 3.50 × 1.20 + 2 × 1.20² ) ] / ( 10² − 2² ) = [ 24.50 + 100 ( 8.40 + 2.88 ) ] / 96 = [ 24.50 + 1128.0 ] / 96 = 12.005 m²

Retaining and breast walls

Hill roads need a great deal of retaining work, and it is measured as a stack of prisms exactly like a bridge abutment:

  • Retaining wall — holds up filled ground on the valley side.
  • Breast wall — supports the cut face on the hill side.
  • Toe wall — protects the toe of an embankment.
  • Gabion wall — wire crates filled with boulders; measured in m³ of crate, with the wire mesh in m² or by weight.
masonry wall with a battered face: volume = length × ½( top width + bottom width ) × height

5.6 Earthwork and lining works in irrigation canals

An irrigation canal is estimated the same way as a road — a trapezoidal section repeated along a chainage — but here the section is a channel rather than an embankment, and it usually has to be lined.

Earthwork in the canal

area of the canal section A = B·D + s·D² B = bed width , D = depth of the channel , s = side slope volume between two sections, by the prismoidal formula: Q = (L/6)( A₁ + 4Amid + A₂ )

Cutting and filling are kept in separate columns, chainage by chainage, exactly as for a road. Where the canal runs partly in cutting and partly on a bank, the bank itself is a further filling item with its own section.

Lining

Lining reduces seepage and prevents scour. It is measured on the wetted perimeter — the surface actually covered:

wetted perimeter P = B + 2D√(1 + s²) area of lining = P × length of canal volume of lining = P × length × thickness of lining
