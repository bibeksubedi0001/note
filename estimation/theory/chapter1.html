  1. What is an estimating? Why should prepare before construction work? Describe the data required for the preparation of an estimate. [1+1+4] (2082 Bhadra, Q.1)
    The third part of this question also belongs to Section 1.3.
    Solution

    Estimating. An estimate is the process of calculating, in advance, the probable quantity of each item of work and its probable cost, from the drawings and specifications and the rates prevailing at the time. It is prepared before the work is taken up.

    Why it must be prepared before construction. Because a client must know what the work will cost before he commits to it. Without an estimate there is no basis to arrange finance, obtain sanction, provide the amount in the budget, order materials, engage labour, fix the completion time, or invite tenders. It is the single document on which the whole administrative and contractual process rests.

    Data required for preparing a detailed estimate

    1. Drawings — complete and fully dimensioned plans, elevations, sections and detail drawings to scale. Without these no quantity can be taken out.
    2. Specifications — general specifications stating the class and quality of materials, and detailed specifications stating the proportion of the mix and the method of doing each item.
    3. Schedule of rates — the current district rate of materials, labour and carriage, and the rates of finished items of work.
    4. Standard rate-analysis norms — the departmental norms giving the material, labour and plant required per unit of each item.
    5. Standard method of measurement (IS 1200 or the departmental rules) — so that quantities are taken out and paid in a uniform way.
    6. Site conditions — nature of soil, water table, availability of materials and water, lead and lift, access and transport, and local labour rates.
    7. Provision for contingencies, work-charged establishment, and the cost of external services such as water supply, sanitation and electrification.

    • a) What is purpose of preparing detail estimate before construction work?
    • b) Estimated cost is never equal to the actual cost of a project. Give your opinion.
    [2+3] (2080 Bhadra, Q.1)
    Solution

    a) Purpose of preparing a detailed estimate before construction

    A detailed estimate gives the quantity and cost of every item of work separately. It is prepared beforehand in order to:

    1. know the accurate probable cost and decide the financial feasibility of the work;
    2. obtain technical sanction and provide the amount in the budget;
    3. work out the materials, labour and plant required, and programme their supply;
    4. prepare the bill of quantities and invite tenders on it;
    5. fix the completion period; and
    6. check the contractor's running and final bills against measured work.

    b) "Estimated cost is never equal to the actual cost" — opinion

    The statement is correct, and it is a property of estimating itself, not a defect of the estimator.

    Why the estimated cost is never exactly the actual cost

    An estimate is a forecast made before the work starts, from assumed data. The actual cost is known only after the work is finished. The two differ because:

    1. Rates change. The estimate uses the rates prevailing on the day it is prepared; prices of materials, labour and fuel fluctuate during construction.
    2. Time gap. Sanction, tendering and execution take months or years, during which escalation occurs.
    3. Site conditions differ from those assumed — unexpected soil, rock, water table, or a longer lead than allowed for.
    4. Changes in design and extra or substituted items ordered during execution.
    5. Tender premium. The contract is awarded above or below the estimated rates.
    6. Errors and omissions in taking out quantities, and approximations in the rate analysis.
    7. Unforeseen causes — weather, strikes, accidents, scarcity of materials.

    This is exactly why a contingency of 3–5% is added to every estimate, and why a revised estimate must be prepared when the cost exceeds the sanctioned amount by more than 5%. A good estimate should come within about ±5–10% of the actual cost.

    In my opinion the estimate should therefore be judged not by whether it equals the actual cost — which is impossible — but by whether it falls within a reasonable margin of it. An estimate based on correct drawings, current district rates and standard norms, and honestly provided with contingencies, is a good estimate even though the final bill differs from it.

  4. What is estimating and costing? Explain the principle of units. [2+3] (2080 Baishakh, Q.1)
    Solution

    Estimating and costing

    Estimating is calculating in advance the probable quantity of each item of work and the probable cost of the whole work, from the drawings, specifications and prevailing rates.

    Costing is determining the actual cost of the work after it has been executed, from measurements actually taken and money actually spent. Estimating looks forward, costing looks back; together they let the engineer forecast the cost, control it during execution, and account for it at the end.

    Principle of units. Each item of work is measured in the unit that best expresses the labour and material in it — the unit follows the shape of the item.

    Nature of the itemUnitExamples
    All three dimensions vary — volumetricm³earthwork, concrete, masonry
    Thin and spread over a surface — thickness fixed by specificationm²plastering, pointing, DPC, painting, flooring, formwork
    Long and thin — only the length variesrunning mcornice, string course, skirting, hand rail, kerb, pipes
    Piece or job work — countednumberdoors, windows, sanitary fittings, trees
    Sold by weightkg / MTreinforcement, M.S. grills, structural steel
    Liquidlitrepaint, bitumen, kerosene

    The principle is simply this: measure in the dimensions that actually vary. If the specification already fixes a dimension, that dimension drops out of the unit. A 12 mm plaster is paid per m² because its thickness is fixed; concrete is paid per m³ because all three dimensions vary.

    b) Methods of taking out quantities in building works

    Four methods are in use; all four must give the same total length of wall for the same building, and that identity is the check on the work.

    1. Long wall — short wall method. Walls running in one direction are taken as long walls measured centre to centre plus one breadth, and those at right angles as short walls measured centre to centre minus one breadth. Suitable when the walls of a room have different thicknesses.
    2. Centre line method. The total centre-line length of all the walls is found once and multiplied by the breadth and depth of each item, deducting half the breadth for each junction. Quickest, and best when all walls have the same thickness.
    3. Crossing method. The out-to-out length of the walls is taken and the overlapping corners are deducted. Suitable for simple rectangular plans.
    4. Bay method. The building is divided into identical bays; one bay is worked out in full and multiplied by the number of bays, the end bays being taken separately. Suitable for long repetitive structures such as barracks and sheds.

    1. Drawings — complete and fully dimensioned plans, elevations, sections and detail drawings to scale. Without these no quantity can be taken out.
    2. Specifications — general specifications stating the class and quality of materials, and detailed specifications stating the proportion of the mix and the method of doing each item.
    3. Schedule of rates — the current district rate of materials, labour and carriage, and the rates of finished items of work.
    4. Standard rate-analysis norms — the departmental norms giving the material, labour and plant required per unit of each item.
    5. Standard method of measurement (IS 1200 or the departmental rules) — so that quantities are taken out and paid in a uniform way.
    6. Site conditions — nature of soil, water table, availability of materials and water, lead and lift, access and transport, and local labour rates.
    7. Provision for contingencies, work-charged establishment, and the cost of external services such as water supply, sanitation and electrification.

    b) Circumstances in which each type of estimate is prepared

    1. Approximate estimate — when a proposal is first mooted and only the broad size is known. Example: a 40-bed hospital costed at Rs. X per bed to obtain administrative approval.
    2. Detailed estimate — once the drawings and specifications are complete and technical sanction is sought. Example: a school building measured item by item and priced by rate analysis.
    3. Revised estimate — when the expenditure will exceed the sanctioned amount by more than 5%. Example: rock is met in the foundation and the excavation rate rises sharply.
    4. Supplementary estimate — when additional work outside the original scope becomes necessary. Example: a compound wall and gate added to a building already under construction.
    5. Complete estimate — when the full cost of bringing the work into use is wanted. Example: a residential quarter including land, water supply, sanitation, electrification, approach road and fees.
    6. Annual repair and maintenance estimate — every year for an existing building. Example: whitewashing, painting and petty repairs of an office.
    7. Extension / renewal estimate — when an existing work is enlarged or a worn-out part renewed. Example: re-roofing an old shed.

    ENCE 351 §1.1 and Chapter 3.

Estimating, costing and quantity survey

Estimating is the process of calculating the quantities of the various items of work in a project and the expenditure likely to be incurred on them. An estimate is therefore the probable cost of the work, worked out theoretically from the drawings, the specifications and the current rates before construction begins.

Costing is the complementary exercise of working out what the work actually cost once it has been done. Quantity survey is the schedule of all the items of work in a project with their quantities taken from the drawings; priced, it becomes the estimate.

Why the estimated cost is never exactly the actual cost

This is a standing favourite of the examiners. The estimate is a forecast, and a forecast made months before the work is built cannot match the outturn exactly:

  1. Rates move. Material, labour and equipment rates change between the date of the estimate and the date of execution — through inflation, fuel prices, or a shortage of a particular material.
  2. Quantities change. Ground conditions revealed on excavation, design changes and extra items ordered during construction all alter the quantities.
  3. Site conditions differ from those assumed — sub-soil water, rock, poor access, longer leads for materials.
  4. Time overruns add establishment and overhead costs and expose the work to further price rises.
  5. Weather and other natural causes interrupt the programme.
  6. The estimator's own judgement — the estimate rests on assumed norms, assumed wastage and assumed productivity.

A well-prepared estimate should nevertheless come close. A variance within about ±5–10% is regarded as acceptable; a large variance points to a defective estimate, and that is why the revised estimate exists (§3.3).

Purposes of estimating and costing

  1. To know the probable cost before the work is taken in hand, so the proposal can be judged and sanctioned.
  2. To fix the budget and obtain administrative approval and technical sanction.
  3. To work out the quantities of materials required, so that controlled materials — cement, steel, bricks, timber — can be arranged in time.
  4. To work out the labour of each category required.
  5. To work out the tools, plant and equipment required.
  6. To estimate the time needed and draw up the work schedule.
  7. To invite tenders, prepare the bill of quantities and arrange the contract.
  8. To check and control expenditure during execution and to check the contractor's bills.
  9. To justify the investment from a benefit–cost point of view.
  10. To help fix standard rent, and for the valuation of the property (§7).

Administrative approval and technical sanction

Administrative approval is the formal acceptance of the proposal, in principle and as to cost, by the administrative authority that needs the work. Technical sanction is the approval of the detailed estimate and design by the competent engineering authority; it certifies that the estimate is structurally and financially sound. Work cannot begin until both are in place, and an expenditure sanction releases the money.

    a) How the items are measured, and their units

Data required for preparing an estimate

Four things are needed before an estimate can be started. The examiners ask for this list very often, usually for 3–5 marks.

  1. Drawings — complete, fully dimensioned plans, elevations, sections and detail drawings. Without dimensions no quantity can be taken out. For a building this means the site plan, floor plans, at least two sectional elevations and details of foundation, doors, windows, staircase and roof.

The same item can carry different rates on two sites.     6. Working space and side slopes — measure the authorised dimensions of the trench as per drawing; extra excavation for the convenience of the contractor is not paid.
    7. Excavation and filling kept separate, and the volume of filling measured after compaction, not loose.
    8. Bulking and shrinkage — earth increases in volume when loosened and reduces when compacted, so the state in which the measurement is taken must be stated.
    9. Unit — measured in m³ and generally paid per 100 m³. Surface dressing up to 30 cm deep is measured in m².

    b) When a revised estimate is required

    A revised estimate is a fresh detailed estimate prepared for a work already sanctioned. It becomes necessary when:

    1. the expenditure exceeds, or is likely to exceed, the sanctioned amount by more than 5%;
    2. the rates adopted in the original estimate have changed materially;
    3. there is a substantial change in the design, specification or scope;
    4. site conditions turn out to differ from those assumed; or
    5. the work has been abandoned and is restarted after a long interval.

    Example. A rural road was sanctioned for Rs. 2.00 crore on the assumption of ordinary soil throughout. On excavation, hard rock was met over 600 m of the alignment, raising the excavation cost and requiring additional breast walls. The anticipated cost rose to Rs. 2.35 crore — an excess of 17.5%, far beyond 5% — so a revised estimate was prepared showing the original and revised quantity and cost of each item side by side, with the reason for every variation, and was submitted for fresh sanction.

  3. Briefly explain the method of quantity estimate for road construction. Workout the quantities of earthwork in embankment and cutting. [2+10] (2079 Bhadra, Q.8)
    The second part of this question belongs to Chapter 5; the earthwork data table is given with the original question paper.
    Solution

    a) Method of quantity estimate for road construction

    A road is a long structure of nearly uniform cross-section, so it is estimated chainage by chainage rather than item by item over a plan. The procedure is:

    1. Carry out a longitudinal survey and plot the ground level and the proposed formation level at every chainage (usually every 20–30 m).
    2. At each chainage find the depth of cutting or height of filling, D = GL − FL for cutting, FL − GL for filling.
    3. Work out the cross-sectional area at each chainage from the trapezoidal section.
    4. Find the volume between successive chainages by the mid-sectional, mean-sectional or prismoidal method, keeping cutting and filling in separate columns.
    5. Total the two columns separately to get the balance of earthwork, and hence the quantity to be borrowed or wasted.
    6. Add the pavement layers, cross drainage, protection works and furniture as separate items, and abstract the cost.
    A = B·D + s·D² B = formation width, D = depth, s = side slope 1. mid-sectional : dm = (d₁+d₂)/2 ; Amid = B·dm + s·dm² ; Q = Amid × L 2. mean-sectional : Q = ½(A₁ + A₂) × L 3. prismoidal : Q = (L/6)(A₁ + 4Amid + A₂)

    b) Worked example

    Formation width B = 10 m, side slope s = 2:1 in both cutting and filling, cross-sections at 30 m intervals. Depths from the longitudinal section:
    Filling (embankment): d₁ = 1.0 m, d₂ = 1.6 m
    Cutting: d₁ = 1.2 m, d₂ = 2.0 m

    Embankment (filling) — prismoidal method

    A₁ = 10(1.0) + 2(1.0)² = 10.00 + 2.00 = 12.00 m² A₂ = 10(1.6) + 2(1.6)² = 16.00 + 5.12 = 21.12 m² dm = (1.0 + 1.6)/2 = 1.30 m Amid = 10(1.30) + 2(1.30)² = 13.00 + 3.38 = 16.38 m² Qfill = (30/6)[12.00 + 4(16.38) + 21.12] = 5 [12.00 + 65.52 + 21.12] = 5 × 98.64 = 493.20 m³

    Cutting — prismoidal method

    A₁ = 10(1.2) + 2(1.2)² = 12.00 + 2.88 = 14.88 m² A₂ = 10(2.0) + 2(2.0)² = 20.00 + 8.00 = 28.00 m² dm = (1.2 + 2.0)/2 = 1.60 m Amid = 10(1.60) + 2(1.60)² = 16.00 + 5.12 = 21.12 m² Qcut = (30/6)[14.88 + 4(21.12) + 28.00] = 5 [14.88 + 84.48 + 28.00] = 5 × 127.36 = 636.80 m³

    Balance of earthwork

    surplus = 636.80 − 493.20 = 143.60 m³ of cut to be disposed of
    Earthwork in embankment = 493.20 m³, earthwork in cutting = 636.80 m³; the cut exceeds the fill by 143.60 m³, which must be carried to spoil.

    Had the mid-sectional method been used the answers would be 5×98.28 = 491.40 and 5×126.72 = 633.60 m³ — slightly less; the mean-sectional method gives 496.80 and 642.60 m³ — slightly more. The prismoidal result lies between the two, as it always does.

    a) Quantity of a semi-circular arch

    A semi-circular arch over a span L has its centre at mid-span, so the rise r = L/2 and the intrados (soffit) is a semicircle of radius L/2. If the arch ring is of thickness t measured radially and the wall is of breadth b, the arch is the volume of a half hollow cylinder.

    internal radius R₁ = L / 2 ( = the rise of a semi-circular arch ) external radius R₂ = R₁ + t = L/2 + t area of the arch ring (half annulus) A = ½ × π ( R₂² − R₁² ) volume of arch V = A × b = ½π( R₂² − R₁² ) × b equivalently, using the MEAN radius Rm = R₁ + t/2 : length of centre line of the ring = π Rm V = π Rm × t × b ← the quicker form

    Numerical illustration. Span L = 2.0 m, ring thickness t = 0.30 m, wall breadth b = 0.30 m:

    R₁ = 2.0/2 = 1.00 m rise = 1.00 m R₂ = 1.00 + 0.30 = 1.30 m A = ½ × π (1.30² − 1.00²) = ½ × π (1.69 − 1.00) = ½ × π × 0.69 = 1.0838 m² V = 1.0838 × 0.30 = 0.325 m³ check by the mean-radius form: Rm = 1.00 + 0.15 = 1.15 m V = π × 1.15 × 0.30 × 0.30 = 0.325 m³ ✓
    Volume of the semi-circular arch = 0.325 m³

    Note the two checks that the examiner looks for: for a semi-circular arch the rise always equals half the span, and the mean-radius formula V = πRmtb must agree with the annulus formula.

The rules below are the Indian Standard IS 1200 rules, which Nepalese departmental practice follows. They matter because they decide what is deducted and what is ignored — and that is what the marks are for.

General rules

  1. Linear dimensions are measured to the nearest 0.01 m, areas to the nearest 0.01 m² and volumes to the nearest 0.01 m³.
  2. Work of each description, and work executed in different situations (in foundation, in plinth, in superstructure, under water, in foul position, in liquid mud) is measured separately, because each carries a different rate.
  3. Different mortar proportions and different mixes are kept separate.
  4. Measurements are taken of the finished work in place; no allowance is made for wastage, which is already inside the rate.

Earthwork

Measured in m³ as length × breadth × depth of the trench, each dimension to 0.01 m. Excavation in different classes of soil (ordinary soil, hard soil, soft rock, hard rock) is measured separately, and so is excavation below water table. Surface excavation and surface dressing up to 15–30 cm depth are measured in m².

Concrete work

Measured in m³. No deduction is made for:

  • the volume of reinforcement;
  • the bearing of beams, girders and trusses up to 500 cm² in section;
  • small voids up to 40 cm²;
  • pipes and conduits up to 100 cm² in cross-section.

Brick work

Measured in m³ to 0.01 m³. No deduction is made for:

  • bearings of joists, beams, rafters and lintels up to 0.1 m³ in section;
  • openings up to 0.1 m² in area;
  • drainage holes, iron fixtures, pipes and hold-fasts.

Half-brick and thinner walls are measured in m².

Plastering and pointing — the deduction rule

Measured in m² to 0.01 m²; ceilings, walls and different mixes separately. Deductions for openings follow a three-band rule, and no addition is made for jambs, sills and soffits except in the largest band:

Wood work

Areas to 0.01 m² and volumes to 0.001 m³. Frames (chaukhat) are measured in m³; shutters of specified thickness in m². Shoring and strutting timber is measured in m³; skirting, cornices, picture rails and similar mouldings in running metre.

Measured in kg (or MT) of the finished bar in place. The length of each bar, including hooks, bends and laps, is measured and converted to weight using
