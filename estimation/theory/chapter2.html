  1. List various methods of quantity estimate. Describe bill of quantities (BOQ) and preparation of detailed estimate of any project. [1+2+2] (2082 Bhadra, Q.2)
    The second and third parts of this question also belong to Chapter 3.
    Solution

    a) Methods of quantity estimate

    1. Long wall — short wall method
    2. Centre line method
    3. Crossing method
    4. Bay method

    b) Bill of quantities (BOQ)

    A bill of quantities is a tabulated schedule of every item of work in a project, giving a brief description of the item, the estimated quantity, the unit, the rate and the amount. It is issued with the tender documents so that all contractors price the same quantities and their offers can be compared item by item; it is afterwards used to measure and pay for the work actually done and to value variations.

    c) Preparation of a detailed estimate of a project

    1. Collect the data — complete drawings, specifications, the district rate and the departmental norms.
    2. List the items of work under sub-heads, in the order of construction.
    3. Find the wall length by the centre line or long wall–short wall method.
    4. Take out the quantities item by item on the measurement sheet (number × length × breadth × height), applying the deduction rules of IS 1200.
    5. Analyse the rate of each item from the norms and the district rate.
    6. Prepare the abstract of cost — quantity × rate = amount for every item, and total it.
    7. Add contingencies (3–5%) and work-charged establishment (1.5–2%), and the provisions for sanitary, water supply and electrical work (8–10% each).
    8. Write the report and attach the drawings, specifications, rate analysis and the abstract, and submit for technical sanction.

  2. Write down the general method of taking out quantities in load bearing brick masonry building with their suitability. [5] (2081 Bhadra, Q.3)
    Solution

    General methods of taking out quantities in a load-bearing brick masonry building, and their suitability

    Four methods are used to take out the quantities of a load-bearing masonry building. All of them must give the same total length of wall — that identity is the check on the work.

    1. Long wall — short wall method (also called the out-to-out and in-to-in method)

    • Walls running in one direction are taken as long walls and measured from centre to centre plus one breadth of the wall (out to out).
    • Walls at right angles to them are taken as short walls and measured from centre to centre minus one breadth (in to in).
    • The length changes for every footing, because the breadth changes.
    long wall L = centre-to-centre length + breadth of the item short wall S = centre-to-centre length − breadth of the item quantity = (2L + 2S) × breadth × height

    Suitability: best when the walls of a building are of different thicknesses, or when the rooms are of different sizes, because each wall is dealt with on its own.

    2. Centre line method

    • The total centre-line length of all the walls is found once.
    • It is multiplied by the breadth and the depth of each item in turn.
    • For every junction where a wall meets another, half the breadth of the item is deducted, because that corner would otherwise be counted twice.
    deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item quantity = (total centre-line length − deduction) × breadth × height

    Suitability: the quickest method, and the one normally used when all the walls are of the same thickness and meet in simple T or L junctions. It becomes error-prone where junctions are many or irregular.

    3. Crossing method

    • The out-to-out length of the walls is taken in both directions.
    • The corners, which have then been counted twice, are deducted — one breadth for each crossing.
    quantity = [2(Lout + Bout) − 4 × breadth] × breadth × height

    Suitability: simple rectangular plans without internal cross walls; it becomes clumsy as soon as partitions are introduced.

    4. Bay method

    • A long building of repeating panels is divided into identical bays.
    • One typical bay is worked out in full and multiplied by the number of bays.
    • The two end bays are taken separately, since they carry the gable or end wall.

    Suitability: long repetitive structures — barracks, sheds, godowns, factory buildings, and framed buildings with equal spans.

    For a load-bearing building the answer really turns on one number — the total length of wall. Every item from the excavation to the superstructure masonry is that length multiplied by a different breadth and depth, so choosing the method that gets the length right with the fewest chances of error is the whole art of the thing.

    ENCE 351 §2.1–§2.4.

    b) Sub-heads of items of work for a building project

    Sub-heads of items of work for a building project

    The items of a building estimate are grouped under sub-heads, taken in the order in which the work is actually built:

    1. Earthwork — site clearance, excavation in foundation, refilling, plinth filling, disposal of surplus earth.
    2. Concrete work — PCC in foundation and floors, RCC in footings, columns, beams, slabs, lintels and stairs, with formwork and reinforcement.
    3. Masonry / brickwork — brick or stone masonry in foundation, plinth and superstructure, partition walls.
    4. Damp proofing — DPC at plinth level, waterproofing of roof and wet areas.
    5. Wood work / joinery — door and window frames and shutters, cupboards, railings, with fittings and fastenings.
    6. Steel work — grills, gates, rolling shutters, structural steel.
    7. Roofing — roof slab or truss and covering, gutters, rain-water pipes.
    8. Flooring — base concrete, floor finish, skirting and dado.
    9. Finishing — internal and external plastering, pointing, white washing, distempering and painting.
    10. Sanitary and water supply installation — fittings, pipes, septic tank and soak pit — usually 8–10% of the building cost.
    11. Electrical installation — internal wiring, fittings and fixtures — usually 8–10%.
    12. Miscellaneous and external works — compound wall, gate, approach road, paving, landscaping.

    Grouping under sub-heads is not decoration. It makes the estimate easy to check, lets the work be split into separate contracts, and allows the departmental percentages for sanitary and electrical work to be applied to the right base.

  4. You are working for a design project of a building. Your Team Leader instruct you to perform a work break down structure for the quantity calculation work. How are you planning to do so for the frame structure building project? [4] (2079 Bhadra, Q.1)
    Solution

    Work breakdown structure for the quantity calculation of a framed structure building

    A work breakdown structure (WBS) splits the whole project into progressively smaller deliverables until each is small enough to be measured, priced and assigned to one person. For the quantity take-off of a framed building I would break it down as follows.

    Level 1 — the project. Quantity estimate of the framed building.

    Level 2 — by physical division, because a framed building is built floor by floor:

    • Substructure (up to plinth level)
    • Superstructure, floor by floor
    • Roof
    • Finishing
    • Services
    • External works

    Level 3 — by trade / sub-head within each division:

    DivisionPackages
    SubstructureExcavation for isolated footings · PCC bed · footing pads · column pedestals · plinth beams · backfilling and plinth filling · DPC
    Superstructure (repeat per floor)Columns · beams · slab · staircase · infill brickwork · lintels and sunshades
    RoofRoof slab · parapet · waterproofing · rain-water pipes
    FinishingInternal plaster · external plaster · flooring and skirting · painting · doors and windows
    ServicesSanitary and water supply · electrical
    External worksCompound wall · gate · paving · septic tank and soak pit

    Level 4 — the measurable item (work package). Each package is broken into the items that actually appear in the bill of quantities, with its unit:

    • Concrete of each grade, per element — m³
    • Formwork to each element — m²
    • Reinforcement, by bar diameter, from the bar bending schedule — kg
    • Brickwork of each thickness — m³
    • Plaster of each mix and thickness — m²

    How I would carry it out

    1. Number every element on the drawing with a unique mark (C1, C2, B1, S1…) so that no member is measured twice or missed.
    2. Prepare a column, beam and slab schedule giving the size, number and floor of every member — for a framed building this is the backbone of the take-off, just as the centre-line length is for a load-bearing building.
    3. Measure floor by floor, since floors repeat: measure a typical floor once and multiply, then take the ground and top floors separately.
    4. Prepare the bar bending schedule element by element and convert to weight at w = d²/162 kg/m.
    5. Assign each level-3 package to a team member, with a common measurement sheet format and a single agreed set of drawings and revisions.
    6. Roll the quantities up the WBS — item → package → division → project — and cross-check the totals against an approximate plinth-area estimate to catch gross errors.

    Two rules make a WBS work: the 100% rule — the children of any node must add up to exactly the parent, nothing left out and nothing counted twice — and mutual exclusivity, so that no item of work appears under two packages.

    ENCE 351 Chapter 2 and §3.2.

    b) Factors considered during detailed estimation

    1. Completeness and correctness of the drawings — every dimension needed must be available and consistent; the estimate can be no better than the drawing.

    9. Provisions for contingencies (3–5%), work-charged establishment (1.5–2%), and the percentage allowances for sanitary, water supply and electrical work.
    10. Overheads, profit and taxes applicable.

  7. Explain various methods of building estimate with suitable sketch. Explain the term contingencies and work charge establishment. [5+5] (2074 Chaitra, Q.2)

    4. Bay method

    • The two end bays are taken separately, since they carry the gable or end wall.

    Suitability: long repetitive structures — barracks, sheds, godowns, factory buildings, and framed buildings with equal spans.

    Worked check — the three methods give the same answer

    A room whose walls are 5.00 m × 4.00 m centre to centre, wall thickness 0.30 m, height of masonry 1.00 m.

    Long wall — short wall method

    long wall = c/c + breadth = 5.00 + 0.30 = 5.30 m × 2 = 10.60 m short wall = c/c − breadth = 4.00 − 0.30 = 3.70 m × 2 = 7.40 m --------- total effective length 18.00 m

    Centre line method

    total centre line = 2 (5.00 + 4.00) = 18.00 m no junction deduction arises here — the centre line of a single closed rectangle already closes on itself, with no wall running into it

    Crossing method

    out-to-out = (5.00 + 0.30) × (4.00 + 0.30) = 5.30 × 4.30 m length = 2 (5.30 + 4.30) − 4 × 0.30 = 19.20 − 1.20 = 18.00 m

    Quantity of masonry

    V = 18.00 × 0.30 × 1.00 = 5.40 m³ ( by all three methods )
    All three methods give an effective wall length of 18.00 m and 5.40 m³ of masonry. Agreement between any two of them is the standard arithmetical check on a quantity sheet.

    The junction deduction in the centre-line method applies only where one wall runs into another — a T-junction with an internal partition, or a cross wall. If this room had one internal cross wall, that wall would make two T-junctions, and the deduction would be 2 × ½ × 0.30 = 0.30 m. A plain closed rectangle has none.

    b) Contingencies and work-charged establishment

    Contingencies. A lump-sum provision, normally 3–5% of the estimated cost of the work, added to every estimate to meet unforeseen and petty expenses that cannot be foreseen or itemised at the time of estimating — small omissions in the quantities, minor changes at site, small variations in rates, and incidental charges. It is not intended to pay for a change in the scope of the work; that requires a supplementary or a revised estimate. The amount can be spent only with the sanction of the competent authority.

    Work-charged establishment. A provision of about 1.5–2% of the estimated cost to pay the staff engaged specifically and temporarily for that one work and charged directly to it — the site overseer, work sarkar, chowkidar, storekeeper, work assistant and similar staff whose appointment ends when the work ends. It is distinct from the regular establishment (the permanent departmental staff), whose salaries are met from the department's own budget and are not charged to the work.

    ContingenciesWork-charged establishment
    Provision3–5% of estimated cost1.5–2% of estimated cost
    MeetsUnforeseen petty expenses and minor omissionsPay of temporary staff engaged for that work only
    NatureA reserve that may or may not be spentA definite recurring expense during the work

  9. Write short notes on (any three):
    • i) Approximate estimate
    • ii) Revised estimate
    • iii) Centre line method
    • iv) Capitalized value (Items i, ii belong to Chapter 3 and item iv belongs to Chapter 7.)
    [6] (2066 Bhadra, Q.2(b))
    Solution

    Answer any three. All four are given below.

    i) Approximate estimate. A rough estimate prepared at the proposal stage, before detailed drawings exist, from a single overall parameter — plinth area, cube content, cost per bed, per student, per km of road. It is used to judge whether the scheme is worth pursuing, to obtain administrative approval and to provide the amount in the budget. It is supported by a report, a site plan and line drawings, and is accurate only to about ±15–20%.

    ii) Revised estimate. A fresh detailed estimate prepared for a work already sanctioned, when the expenditure exceeds or is likely to exceed the sanctioned amount by more than 5%, or when the rates or the design change materially. It shows the original and the revised quantity and cost of every item side by side, with the reason for each variation, and must be sanctioned afresh by the competent authority.

    iii) Centre line method. A method of taking out quantities in which the total centre-line length of all the walls is found once and then multiplied by the breadth and the depth of each item in turn. Where walls meet, half the breadth of the item is deducted for each junction, because that corner would otherwise be counted twice.

    deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item quantity = (total centre-line length − deduction) × breadth × height

    It is the quickest method and is best suited to buildings in which all the walls have the same thickness.

    iv) Capitalized value. The amount of capital whose interest, at the prevailing rate, would yield the net annual income actually received from the property. It is the value of the property on an income basis:

    capitalized value = net annual income × year's purchase year's purchase (perpetuity) = 100 / rate of interest = 1 / i net annual income = gross annual income − all outgoings

    For example, a property yielding a net income of Rs 1,20,000 a year, capitalised at 8%, has a capitalized value of 1,20,000 × (100/8) = 1,20,000 × 12.5 = Rs 15,00,000.

    ENCE 351 Chapters 2, 3 and 7.

All four methods in this chapter answer the same question — how long is the wall? — and all four must give the same quantity. They differ only in how that length is measured, and therefore in how much arithmetic each involves and how easily it goes wrong.

Every building estimate starts from a section like this. Each band is a separate item with its own breadth, so one length is multiplied by a different breadth and depth for each:

The method

Also called the individual wall method, or out-to-out and in-to-in. The walls running one way are taken right through; the walls running the other way are measured between them, so no corner is counted twice.

length of long wall = centre-to-centre length + breadth of the item (out-to-out) length of short wall = centre-to-centre length − breadth of the item (in-to-in)

The breadth added and subtracted is the breadth of the item being measured, not of the wall. So as you work down through the wider footings the long-wall length grows and the short-wall length shrinks by exactly the same amount — which is why the total stays right.

for the 0.30 m thick wall above: long wall = 5.00 + 0.30 = 5.30 m , 2 of them short wall = 4.00 − 0.30 = 3.70 m , 2 of them total = 2(5.30) + 2(3.70) = 18.00 m

Now take the same walls on the 0.90 m wide PCC bed. The lengths change, but the total does not:

long wall = 5.00 + 0.90 = 5.90 m short wall = 4.00 − 0.90 = 3.10 m total = 2(5.90) + 2(3.10) = 18.00 m ✓ unchanged

Advantages. The safest method where walls differ in thickness — a 0.30 m external wall and a 0.23 m partition can never be confused, because each is handled on its own. It also yields quantities wall by wall, which is what the site engineer actually wants.

Disadvantage. The longest method: the length has to be recomputed for every item, so every footing needs its own addition and subtraction.

Dawadi, Estimating ch.2 §3.3(b); Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual ch.2.

2.2 Centre line method

The total centre-line length is found once, then multiplied by the breadth and depth of each item in turn.

quantity = total centre-line length × breadth of item × depth of item

Because the centre line runs continuously round the building, the corners look after themselves: whatever length is gained on one side of a corner is lost on the other. For the plan in §2.1,

total centre line = 2(5.00) + 2(4.00) = 18.00 m ✓ agrees with the long wall – short wall total

The junction rule

Where a cross wall meets a main wall the centre line is interrupted, and the length must be reduced:

deduction = (number of junctions) × ½ × breadth of the item

A T-junction counts as one junction; a cross (+) junction counts as two. Note again that the breadth is that of the item — so the deduction is larger for the wide PCC bed than for the plinth wall, and it must be recalculated for each item.

This junction correction is the commonest source of error in the centre line method, and the reason the long wall – short wall method is preferred when a plan has many cross walls.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages. Much the quickest method, and it works for any closed shape — rectangular, circular, hexagonal or octagonal — provided the walls are all of the same thickness.

Limitations. It needs the junction correction, and where walls differ in thickness each set of walls must be taken separately, which removes most of the saving.

Dawadi, Estimating ch.2 §3.3(a).

2.3 Crossing method

A short cut for obtaining the same centre-line length from the outside dimensions of the building:

total centre-line length = outside perimeter − 4 × (thickness of wall)

Checking it on the plan in §2.1, whose out-to-out size is 5.30 × 4.30 m:

2(5.30 + 4.30) − 4(0.30) = 19.20 − 1.20 = 18.00 m ✓

The 4 × thickness is not arbitrary — it is one wall thickness for each of the four corners, where the outside perimeter runs round the outside of the corner instead of through the middle of it. The same length may equally be found from the inside dimensions:

total centre-line length = inside perimeter + 4 × (thickness of wall)
check: inside is 4.70 × 3.70 2(4.70 + 3.70) + 4(0.30) = 16.80 + 1.20 = 18.00 m ✓

Use. Strictly a device for getting the centre-line length quickly on a simple rectangular plan; the quantities are then taken out by the centre line method. It is also the fastest way to check a long wall – short wall total in the exam.

Dawadi, Estimating ch.2 §3.3(c).

2.4 Bay method

Used where a building repeats itself — a factory shed, a barrack block, a row of identical classrooms, a bridge of equal spans. The quantity of one typical (intermediate) bay is worked out in full and multiplied by the number of such bays; the end bays, which differ because of the gable or end wall, are estimated separately and added.

total = (quantity of one intermediate bay × number of intermediate bays) + quantity of the two end bays

A bay is the portion of the structure between two consecutive frames, trusses or cross walls — the unit that repeats.

Advantage. An enormous saving of labour on a repetitive structure: one bay is costed and the rest follow by multiplication.

Limitation. It applies only where the repetition is genuine. Any variation in span, height, foundation depth or opening must be picked up separately, or the estimate is wrong by that difference multiplied by the number of bays.

Choosing between the four methods

SituationMethodWhy
All walls the same thickness, simple closed planCentre lineOne length serves every item
Walls of different thickness, or many cross wallsLong wall – short wallEach wall handled on its own; no junction corrections to get wrong
Only the outside dimensions are givenCrossingGives the centre line in one line of arithmetic
Repetitive structure — sheds, barracks, equal spansBayOne bay costed and multiplied

Whichever method is used the answer must come out the same. A good exam habit is to find the centre-line length by the crossing method and use it to check the long wall – short wall total.

Dawadi, Estimating ch.2; Pandey & Katwal, Complete Manual ch.2.
